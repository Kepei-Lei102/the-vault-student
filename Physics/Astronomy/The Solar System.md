# The Solar System 太阳系

> *In the second week of January 1610 Galileo turned a home-made telescope on Jupiter and saw three small stars in a line beside it. The next night they had moved, and to the wrong side. Within a week there were four, shuffling back and forth night after night but never leaving the planet. He understood what he was seeing: four moons circling Jupiter. Here was a centre of motion that was plainly not the Earth, and with it the oldest argument against Copernicus, that a moving Earth would leave its Moon behind, was finished. Anyone with binoculars can repeat the observation tonight.*

## Definition

### Formal

The **Solar System** is the Sun together with everything held in orbit around it by the Sun's gravitational attraction: **one star**; **eight planets** (in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune); **minor planets**, which include **dwarf planets** such as Pluto and the **asteroids** of the asteroid belt; **moons** (natural satellites), which orbit planets; and smaller bodies such as **comets**. The Sun contains 99.9 % of the system's mass, which is why everything else orbits it.

### Intuitive

Nearly everything in this topic follows from two facts. Gravity pulls towards the biggest mass, and the biggest mass by far is the Sun. And the Earth is a spinning ball lit from one side, leaning over as it goes round. The first fact gives the orbits, their speeds and the way the system formed; the second gives day and night, the seasons, and (with the Moon) the phases.

## Part I — The Earth: a spinning, leaning, orbiting ball

### Day and night

The Earth is a planet that **rotates on its axis once in approximately 24 hours**. The Sun lights one half of it at a time. A point on the surface is carried through the lit half (day) and the unlit half (night) once per rotation. Because the Earth turns from west to east, the Sun, the Moon and the stars all *appear* to move the other way: rising in the east, crossing the sky, setting in the west. The Sun is not moving across the sky; the ground you stand on is turning under it.

### The seasons

The Earth **orbits the Sun once in approximately 365 days**, and its axis is **tilted**, by 23.4° from the upright. The axis keeps pointing the same way in space all year (at present towards the Pole Star). So for part of the orbit the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun, and half a year later it leans away.

![[solar-system-seasons.svg|860]]

Leaning towards the Sun does two things, and both heat the ground:

- **The Sun is higher in the sky**, so a beam of sunlight is spread over less ground. In Chengdu the noon Sun stands 83° above the horizon in June and 36° in December; a beam one metre wide lights 1.01 m of ground in June and 1.71 m in December.
- **The day is longer**: 14.0 hours against 10.0.

Together they deliver 2.3 times as much solar energy per square metre per day in June as in December. The southern hemisphere has the opposite season at the same time, because when the north leans towards the Sun the south leans away.

Distance has nothing to do with it. The Earth is *closest* to the Sun in early January (147.1 million km) and furthest in early July (152.1 million km). The 7 % difference in sunlight that this makes is real, and it works against the northern seasons.

### The Moon and its phases

**The Moon orbits the Earth in approximately one month.** Like the Earth, it is a ball lit from one side: at every moment exactly half of it is in sunlight. We see different amounts of that lit half depending on where the Moon is in its orbit, and that is the whole of the explanation of the **phases**.

![[solar-system-moon-phases.mp4]]
*The Moon goes once round the Earth with its sunward half always lit, while the inset shows what that looks like from the Earth, day by day.*

![[solar-system-moon-phases.svg|820]]

When the Moon is between us and the Sun its lit half faces away from us: **new Moon**. A week later it has gone a quarter of the way round and we see half of the lit half: **first quarter**. When it is on the far side of the Earth from the Sun we look straight at the lit half: **full Moon**. Then the sequence runs backwards through **last quarter** to new again. "Waxing" means growing and "waning" shrinking; "gibbous" means more than half. The cycle repeats because the orbit repeats.

The phases are **not** the Earth's shadow. The Earth's shadow touches the Moon only during a lunar eclipse, a few times a year, and only at full Moon. The proof is in the sky: a crescent Moon is often visible in daylight, not far from the Sun, with its bright edge pointing at the Sun. The Earth cannot be between them.

One detail for the curious. The Moon takes 27.3 days to go once round the Earth relative to the stars, yet 29.5 days pass from one full Moon to the next. In those 27.3 days the Earth has moved 27° along its own orbit, so the Moon needs two more days to line up with the Sun again: $1/(1/27.32 - 1/365.25) = 29.5$ days.

### Average orbital speed

For any body in a near-circular orbit of radius $r$ and period $T$, the distance round one orbit is the circumference, so

$$v = \frac{2\pi r}{T},$$

the **average orbital speed**, with $r$ the average radius of the orbit and $T$ the orbital period. For the Earth, $v = 2\pi(1.50\times10^{11})/(365.25\times24\times3600) = 2.98\times10^{4}$ m/s. You are moving at 30 km/s as you read this. For the Moon, $2\pi(3.84\times10^{8})/(27.3\times86\,400) = 1.0$ km/s. The one trap is units: the period must be in seconds.

## Part II — What is out there

![[solar-system-inventory.svg|900]]

**One star**, the Sun. **Eight planets.** The order is worth fixing in memory; a sentence such as *My Very Eager Mother Just Served Us Noodles* carries the initials. The four nearest the Sun are **small and rocky**; the four furthest are **large and gaseous**. **Minor planets** orbit the Sun and are too small to count as planets: the **asteroids**, most of them in the **asteroid belt** between Mars and Jupiter, and the **dwarf planets**, of which **Pluto** is the best known and Ceres, in the asteroid belt, the nearest. (Pluto was reclassified in 2006 after several bodies of similar size were found beyond Neptune. Nothing about Pluto changed.) **Moons**, or natural satellites, orbit planets: the Earth has one, Mars two, Jupiter and Saturn around a hundred each. **Comets** are balls of ice and dust, a few kilometres across, on very elongated orbits; near the Sun the ice turns to vapour and streams away as a tail that always points away from the Sun.

**Reading the table** is an examined skill, and the patterns all have reasons:

- **Further out, longer year.** The orbit is longer *and* the planet moves more slowly.
- **Further out, slower.** The Sun's gravitational field is weaker further away, so less speed is needed to stay in orbit: Part IV.
- **Further out, colder**, since sunlight spreads over a larger sphere. The exception proves a point about atmospheres: **Venus is hotter than Mercury** although it is nearly twice as far from the Sun, because its thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere traps infrared radiation.
- **Density** splits the planets into two families, about 4000 to 5500 kg m⁻³ for the rocky four and 700 to 1600 for the giants. Saturn is less dense than water.
- **Surface field strength depends on mass and on radius**, $g = GM/R^2$ from [[Gravitational Fields]]. Bigger mass, bigger $g$; but Saturn, with 95 Earth masses, has almost the Earth's $g$, because its surface is nine times further from its centre. Around any planet, $g$ **decreases as the distance from the planet increases**.

**How long light takes** is distance over speed, $t = d/c$ with $c = 3.0\times10^{8}$ m/s: 500 s from the Sun to the Earth, 1.3 s from the Moon, over four hours to Neptune. A radio command to a rover on Mars takes between 3 and 22 minutes to arrive, depending on where the two planets are, which is why rovers must drive themselves.

## Part III — Why rock inside and gas outside: the accretion model

A system with four small dense planets close in and four huge light ones far out asks for an explanation, and the **accretion model** gives one. ("Accretion" means growth by gathering material.)

1. **A cloud and gravity.** About 4.6 billion years ago there was an **interstellar cloud of gas and dust**, mostly hydrogen and helium but **containing many elements**, the debris of earlier stars (see [[Stellar Evolution]]). **Gravity** pulled it inward.
2. **Rotation makes a disc.** The cloud was turning slightly. As it shrank it turned faster, as a skater does on pulling in the arms: shrink a cloud from 10 000 AU to 50 AU across and it turns 40 000 times faster. Along the axis of rotation nothing resists the fall; across it, the spin does (the reason is worked through below). The ball therefore collapsed into a flat, spinning **accretion disc**, with most of the mass at the centre, where it became the Sun. This is why all the planets orbit in nearly the same plane, in the same direction.
3. **Temperature sorts the material.** Near the young Sun the disc was hot. There only materials with high melting points, **rock and metal**, could exist as solid grains, and these are a small fraction of the cloud. Further out it was cold enough for **ices** of water, ammonia and methane to freeze as well, and there was far more solid material to gather. The boundary, the **frost line**, lay at about 2.7 AU: a small dark body there settles at about 170 K, where ice survives in a vacuum. It falls between Mars and Jupiter.
![[solar-system-accretion.mp4]]
*A slowly turning cloud collapses under gravity, spins faster, flattens into a disc, lights a Sun at its centre, and gathers into four small rocky planets inside the frost line and four large gaseous ones outside it. Schematic, not to scale.*

4. **Grains to planets.** Grains collided and stuck; larger lumps pulled in smaller ones by gravity. Inside the frost line the planets grew from rock and metal only and stayed **small and rocky**. Outside it the cores grew large enough, about ten Earth masses, for their gravity to capture the hydrogen and helium gas of the disc itself, and they became **large and gaseous**. Between them Jupiter's pull stirred the rubble so that it never assembled: the asteroid belt.

![[solar-system-densities.svg|820]]

The densities are the evidence you can check: a sharp drop exactly where the model says the building material changed.

### Why a disc, and why no planet orbits at right angles to the others

Step 2 deserves a slower look, because it answers a question the finished system raises: the eight orbits all lie within 7° of one plane. Why is there no planet going round over the Sun's poles?

The order of events is the answer: **the flattening happened to the gas, before there were any planets.**

A cloud with a slight overall turn has one special direction, its axis of rotation, and the motion of the gas can be split into two parts that fare very differently.

- **Motion parallel to the axis** (up and down through the middle plane). Gas falling from above meets gas falling from below, and the two streams collide. Gas collisions are sticky: the opposing motions cancel, and their energy is radiated away as heat. No conservation law protects this motion, because the upward and downward momenta add up to zero to begin with. It dies out.
- **Motion around the axis.** This carries the cloud's angular momentum. Collisions can pass angular momentum from one parcel of gas to another, but nothing inside the cloud can destroy the total ([[Angular Momentum]]). It survives, and as the cloud shrinks it shows up as faster spin.

Everything that can cancel does cancel, and what is left is the one thing that cannot: a common circulation about one axis. That shape is a disc.

Now imagine a clump of gas that did start out on an orbit tilted at 90° to the rest. Twice in every orbit it would have to pass through the disc, ploughing into the material there, and each passage would strip away some of its up-and-down motion. Within a few orbits it would be part of the disc. A tilted orbit breaks no law of physics; gas simply cannot stay on one. The planets then grew from material that was already circulating in one plane and in one direction, and they inherited both. The Sun formed from the same cloud and spins about the same axis, which is why its equator lies within about 6° of the planets' plane.

The argument depends on collisions, and so on gas, and that gives a test. Bodies that were thrown outward after the gas had cleared had nothing to flatten their orbits, and they should be found at all angles. They are. Comets arriving from the distant Oort cloud come in from every direction. Comet Hale–Bopp, the bright comet of 1997, has an orbit tilted at 89°, almost exactly at right angles to the planets; Halley's comet is tilted at 162°, which means it goes round the Sun backwards; the dwarf planets Pluto and Eris are tilted at 17° and 44°. The largest tilt among the eight planets is Mercury's 7°, and their average is 2°. The planets are flat because they formed in the gas, and the comets are not because they were scattered after it had gone.

Other planetary systems show the same rule from the other side. Some planets round other stars do orbit over their star's poles, and some go round backwards. They almost certainly formed in a disc too and were tilted later, by a close encounter with another planet or by the slow pull of a companion star, with no gas left to pull them back. The flatness of our own system therefore says two things: it formed from a disc, and nothing violent has happened to it since.

## Part IV — Orbits: gravity, speed and energy

**The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System**, 99.87 %; all eight planets together make 0.13 %, and Jupiter is 71 % of that. Gravitational attraction is mutual, but the far heavier body barely moves, so **the planets orbit the Sun**, and **the force that keeps each object in its orbit is the gravitational attraction of the Sun**. A planet moving sideways at the right speed falls continually towards the Sun and continually misses it: [[Circular Motion]] explains why a force at right angles to the motion changes direction and not speed.

**The Sun's gravitational field gets weaker with distance, and the planets' orbital speeds decrease with distance.** A planet further out needs a gentler curve and gets a weaker pull, and both requirements are met by a lower speed.

![[solar-system-speeds.svg|820]]

The eight points lie on one line, $v = 29.8\ \text{km/s} \div \sqrt{r/\text{AU}}$, which [[Gravitational Fields]] derives as $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$. The same data give $T^2/r^3$ equal to 1.00 for every planet when $T$ is in years and $r$ in AU. One mass at the centre sets every orbit.

### Ellipses

**Planets, minor planets and comets have elliptical orbits.** An ellipse has two foci, and **the Sun is at one focus, not at the centre**, except when the orbit is nearly circular, in which case the two foci merge at the centre. The planets' orbits are close to circles (the Earth's distance varies by 3 %); comets' orbits are very elongated.

![[solar-system-comet.svg|820]]

**An object in an elliptical orbit travels faster when it is closer to the Sun**, and conservation of energy explains why. There is no friction in space, so the total of kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy stays constant. As the comet falls towards the Sun it loses gravitational potential energy, and that energy appears as kinetic energy: it speeds up. Swinging away again it climbs against the Sun's pull, gains potential energy and slows down. Halley's comet does 54.5 km/s at its closest (0.59 AU) and 0.91 km/s at its furthest (35 AU), sixty times slower, and the computed ledger balances: per kilogram, kinetic 1483 MJ plus potential −1508 MJ near the Sun, kinetic 0.4 MJ plus potential −25.3 MJ far away, a total of −24.9 MJ both times.

## Where it is the working tool

**Calendars.** A day is one rotation, a year one orbit, a month (in origin) one cycle of phases, and none of them divides into another: 365.24 days per year, 29.53 days per lunar month. The Gregorian calendar keeps the year and abandons the Moon, adding a leap day. The traditional Chinese calendar keeps both: months start at new Moon, so the fifteenth is always the full Moon (which is why the Mid-Autumn Festival has one), and a thirteenth **leap month** is inserted seven times in nineteen years, because 19 years equal 235 lunar months to within two hours. The 24 solar terms mark the Earth's position in its orbit, which is what farming follows.

**Finding planets round other stars.** A planet that crosses the face of its star dims it by a fraction of a per cent at regular intervals. The interval is $T$, the star's mass gives $r$, the depth of the dip gives the planet's radius, and the star's wobble gives the planet's mass, hence its density, hence whether it is rock or gas: the reading of Part II's table, applied to a world nobody can see. Over five thousand have been found, and many systems look nothing like ours, with giant planets closer in than Mercury. The accretion model has had to grow a second chapter, on planets that migrate inward after they form.

**Steering spacecraft.** Every mission is planned with $v = 2\pi r/T$, $g = GM/r^2$ and the energy ledger of Part IV. Light-travel time decides how much a probe must do for itself: a lander on Mars goes from the top of the atmosphere to the ground in seven minutes, usually less time than the news of its arrival takes to reach the Earth.

**Tides** follow the Moon's phases: the highest tides come at new and full Moon, when the Sun's pull lines up with the Moon's.

## Hands-on

**The Moon in your hand.** In a dark room with one bare lamp at head height (the Sun), hold a ball at arm's length (the Moon) and turn slowly on the spot, keeping the ball just above head height. Your head is the Earth. You will see every phase in turn on the ball, in the right order, and you will see that your head's shadow plays no part except at one position: an eclipse.

**Your own latitude, and the tilt.** At local noon, measure the length of the shadow of a vertical metre stick. The Sun's height is $\tan^{-1}(1/\text{shadow})$. Do it near 21 June and near 21 December: the two angles differ by twice the tilt, 47°, and their average is $90°$ minus your latitude.

**Watch Jupiter's moons.** With binoculars steadied against a wall, find Jupiter (the brightest "star" that does not twinkle) and sketch the line of dots beside it on four or five nights. You are repeating January 1610.

**Run the model.** `python3 solar-system-model.py` prints every number in this page; change the latitude in `seasons()` to your own city, or the tilt to zero, and see what happens to the seasons.

## Common Misconceptions (Teaching Notes)

### 1. "It is summer when the Earth is closer to the Sun"
The Earth is closest in January. The cause is the tilt: a higher Sun and a longer day. If distance were the cause, both hemispheres would have summer together.

### 2. "The phases of the Moon are caused by the Earth's shadow"
The Moon is always half lit by the Sun; the phase is how much of the lit half faces us. The Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only in a lunar eclipse.

### 3. "The far side of the Moon is the dark side"
The far side gets exactly as much sunlight as the near side; at new Moon it is in full daylight. We never see it, because the Moon turns once on its axis for each orbit and so keeps one face towards us.

### 4. "The Sun is at the centre of a planet's orbit"
It is at one focus of the ellipse. For nearly circular orbits the difference is small; for a comet it is most of the orbit.

### 5. "Planets further out are slower because they are heavier" (or "because they have further to go")
They are slower because the Sun's field is weaker there. They take longer for two reasons at once: lower speed and a longer path.

### 6. "The planets share a plane because gravity pulls things into a plane"
Gravity pulls towards the centre and has no preferred plane; a cloud with no rotation would collapse to a ball. The plane comes from the cloud's rotation: collisions in the gas cancel every motion except the shared circulation about one axis. Comets, which were scattered after the gas had gone, orbit at all angles.

### 7. "The asteroid belt is crowded"
The whole belt has about 3 % of the mass of the Moon, spread through a volume far larger than the inner Solar System. Spacecraft cross it without steering.

**§6.1.1 Core:** rotation on a tilted axis once in about 24 hours, used to explain the apparent daily motion of the Sun and day and night; an orbit of about 365 days, used to explain the seasons; the Moon's orbit of about one month, used to explain the phases. **Supplement:** define and use $v = 2\pi r/T$, naming $r$ as the *average radius of the orbit* and $T$ as the *orbital period*. **§6.1.2 Core:** the contents of the Solar System, with the eight planets **in order**; the four inner planets rocky and small, the four outer gaseous and large, explained by the accretion model with its three named ingredients (gravity; many elements in the interstellar cloud of gas and dust; rotation forming an accretion disc); field strength at a planet's surface depends on its mass, and falls with distance from the planet; light-travel-time calculations; the Sun holds most of the mass, which is why the planets orbit it; the orbiting force is the Sun's gravitational attraction. **Supplement:** elliptical orbits with the Sun *not at the centre* unless the orbit is nearly circular; analysing planetary data (orbital distance and duration, density, surface temperature, surface field strength); the Sun's field and the planets' speeds both decrease with distance; faster when closer, explained by conservation of energy. Two habits are worth marks: recall $c = 3.0\times10^{8}$ m/s without being given it, and convert days to seconds before using $2\pi r/T$. The frost line, the 27.3-day period and Kepler's laws are beyond the syllabus.

### Where it is *not* examined

**Cambridge 9702**, **IB Physics** (first assessment 2025) and **AP Physics 1, 2 and C** examine orbits through gravitation ($v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, $T^2 \propto r^3$, orbital energy), which is taught in [[Gravitational Fields]], and none of them lists seasons, phases, the inventory of the Solar System or the accretion model. The descriptive content here is IGCSE content; the physics beneath it is on every board.

## Beyond Syllabus

### Kepler's three laws
Recall that the planets' data gave $T^2/r^3 = 1.00$ for all eight. Johannes Kepler found that pattern in 1619, after two decades of work on Tycho Brahe's naked-eye measurements, along with the two earlier laws used in Part IV: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at a focus, and a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (so it moves faster when nearer). Newton later showed that all three follow from one inverse-square force; the derivation is in [[Gravitational Fields]], and the second law is conservation of [[Angular Momentum]].

### Why we always see the same face of the Moon
The Earth's gravity raises a slight bulge in the rock of the Moon. When the young Moon spun faster than it orbited, the Earth's pull on that bulge acted as a brake until the spin matched the orbit. The Moon's tides are doing the same to the Earth: the day lengthens by about two milliseconds per century, and the Moon, which gains the angular momentum the Earth loses, recedes by 3.8 cm a year, measured by timing laser pulses off reflectors left by the Apollo astronauts.

### The edge of the Solar System
Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper belt of icy bodies, Pluto among them, and far beyond that, out to perhaps 100 000 AU, the Oort cloud, a thin shell of comets that has never been seen directly and is inferred from the orbits of the comets that fall in from it. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is about 170 AU away, and its radio signals take more than 23 hours to arrive.
