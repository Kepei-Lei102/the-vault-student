# How a Language Model Works 语言模型是怎么工作的

> Type "Happy" on your phone and the keyboard offers "birthday" before you have thought of it. It is not reading your mind. It has counted, across an enormous amount of text, which word tends to follow which, and "birthday" is the best bet.
>
> A language model is that bet made enormous. Everything in this card is built on the vault's own sixty Stories, in numpy, with nothing hidden: a model that counts, a model that learns, and the moment you can watch one turn from noise into words.

## What this is for

[[Artificial Intelligence]] built machine learning from scratch: regression, clustering, a network that learned a spiral, and Q-learning. It gave the language models of the 2020s one paragraph. This card is that paragraph unpacked: what a language model actually computes, why predicting the next token is enough to produce paragraphs, what attention does, what scale changed, and what such a model is not. Every claim about behaviour is checked on a model you can train in three minutes with `language-model-lab.py`, and every number quoted comes from that run.

## Definition

### Formal

A **language model** assigns a probability to the next **token** (a character, a word, or a piece of a word) given the tokens before it: $P(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t)$. Text is generated by sampling a token from that distribution, appending it, and repeating. The model is **trained** by adjusting its parameters to make the tokens of a large corpus of real text more probable, which is the same thing as minimising the average number of bits the model would need to encode that text. Modern language models are **transformers**: deep networks whose layers use **attention** to let each position's representation be rebuilt as a weighted mix of the positions before it, with the weights computed from the tokens themselves.

### Intuitive

Cover the last word of a sentence with your thumb and guess it. You will be right surprisingly often, and when you are wrong your guess will still be a word that fits. You can do this because you have read a great deal, and reading has left you with a feel for what follows what. A language model is a machine built for nothing but that game, played on more text than any person could read in ten thousand lifetimes. It never learns a rule of grammar, a fact, or the meaning of a word directly. It learns them only to the extent that knowing them makes the next guess better, which turns out to be a very large extent. The surprise of the last decade is not that the game can be played by a machine; it is how much of what we call understanding falls out of playing it well.

### 中文锚点 (Chinese Anchor)

在手机上打出"生日"两个字，输入法就先一步跳出"快乐"，你还没来得及想。它不是在读你的心，而是在海量的文字里数过：哪个词后面往往跟着哪个词，"快乐"是最稳的一注。语言模型玩的就是同一种赌法，只是赌局大到了惊人的规模。它读进一段文字，给每一种可能的下一小段文字算出一个概率；挑一个，接上去，再赌下一注，一小段一小段地接，直到一整段回答立在那里。它肚子里没有一本事实词典，也没有一张语法规则表。它看起来懂的一切，从拼写到一部小说的情节，之所以在那里，只是因为懂了这些，在训练用的文字上，下一个词的这一注就押得准一点。所以它能写出一段流畅的文字，却把一个日期写错：对一台只学过"接下来通常是什么"的机器来说，一个看起来像真的日期和一个真的是对的日期，在它眼里一模一样。

## Notation

| Symbol | Meaning | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| $x_1, \dots, x_t$ | the tokens so far, the **context** | characters in this card's lab; word pieces in real models |
| $P(x_{t+1} \mid x_1 \dots x_t)$ | the model's distribution over the next token | one number per token in the vocabulary, summing to 1 |
| $V$ | the vocabulary size | 96 characters here; about 100 000 pieces in a modern model |
| $T$ | the context length | 64 here; hundreds of thousands in a modern model |
| $d$ | the width of a token's vector | 96 here; thousands in a modern model |
| $\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k}, \mathbf{v}$ | query, key, value: three vectors made from each token | what I am looking for, what I offer, what I pass on |
| $\text{softmax}(z)_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$ | turns any list of scores into probabilities | used twice: for attention weights and for the final prediction |
| bits per character | $-\tfrac1n \sum \log_2 P(\text{actual next character})$ on unseen text | the loss; also the size the model could compress the text to |
| temperature $\tau$ | divides the scores before the softmax | below 1 sharpens the choice, above 1 flattens it |

> [!warning] Notation trap
> "Bits per character" and "loss" are the same number in different units: the loss is usually reported in nats (natural logarithms) and divides by $\ln 2 = 0.693$ to give bits. A model quoted as "loss 1.8" is at $2.6$ bits per character. Papers report **perplexity**, $2^{\text{bits}}$, which is the effective number of equally likely choices the model is left with: $2.6$ bits per character is a perplexity of $6$, as if the model were choosing among six characters each time instead of ninety-six.

## Part I — Count what comes next

Start with the simplest model that can be called one. Take the corpus, here $1{,}144{,}638$ characters of the vault's Stories with front matter and links stripped, hold back the last tenth as **unseen text**, and count. With no context at all, the model is the letter-frequency table: it predicts a space, then *e*, then *t*, whatever came before. With one character of context it knows that *q* is followed by *u*. With three it knows that *the* is followed by a space and *tio* by *n*. The measure of a model is how many bits it needs, on average, to say which character actually came next in text it never saw; a model that always knew would need zero, and a model that assigns every one of the $96$ characters the same chance needs $\log_2 96 = 6.6$.

|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 4.67 | 1 | `The e. .itste yeilih tanwnh0voen xe dldk` |
| 1 | 3.71 | 96 | `The e: rterve's s arrd; nd Sat` |
| 2 | 3.10 | 3 066 | `The prat a post re a rem mand notedeas of anniuss` |
| 3 | **2.64** | 19 880 | `The New posity disp most frammitter explic Cler's perfect` |
| 5 | 2.96 | 161 670 | `The lunar suddenly happens. He would have shilling Germain was just look` |
| 8 | 4.66 | 542 937 | `The  hcA searched the Mémoire sur les probably not Hopper's teacher has arrived` |

Read the samples down the column. Zero context is noise; one context has the rhythm of English and no words; three contexts has words, mostly; five has phrases lifted whole from the Stories, "He would have shilling Germain"; eight has entire clauses copied out. Now read the bits. They fall, and then at five characters they rise again, and at eight the model is as bad as the letter-frequency table. It has not become stupid. It has run out of data. Eight characters of context give half a million distinct contexts in a million-character corpus, so most have been seen once or twice, and on unseen text the model constantly meets a context it never counted and has nothing to say. The five-character samples look better than the three-character ones because they are quotations; on text it has not seen, quotation is no use.

![[language-model-bits.svg|900]]

This is the wall every counting model hits, and it is the reason the field needed something else. The number of possible contexts grows exponentially with their length, and no corpus is exponential. A model that could **share** what it learns between contexts, so that what it learned from *the letter* also helps with *a letter* and *the note*, would not need to have seen each one. That sharing is what a neural network does.

## Part II — Tokens

Before the network, one practical decision: what is a token? This card's lab uses single characters, which keeps the vocabulary at $96$ and the ideas visible. Real models use pieces of words, found by **byte-pair encoding**: start with single characters, count which adjacent pair occurs most often in the corpus, merge that pair into one new token, and repeat. On the Stories the first twenty-four merges, in order, are

`e_ s_ th d_ t_ n_ er the_ in an y_ ar or ed_ a_ en ti er_ al o_ re on and_ g_`

with an underscore marking the end of a word. The tokeniser has discovered *th*, *the*, *ing*'s beginning, *ed* endings, *and*, and it has discovered them from counts alone, with no dictionary. Run it for fifty thousand merges instead of twenty-four and common words become single tokens while rare ones stay in pieces: *unbelievable* might be *un* + *believ* + *able*. The model then predicts one piece at a time, sees any text at all, and pays for rare words with more steps.

![[language-model-bpe.svg|880]]

Two consequences are worth knowing before you use one of these models. It does not see letters, so counting the *r*'s in *strawberry* is genuinely hard for it: the word arrives as two or three pieces whose letters it has never been shown. And it does not see numbers as numbers: *1234* may be one token and *1235* two, which is one reason arithmetic is not its strong suit.

## Part III — Learn instead of count

The lab's neural model has one attention layer, a width of $96$, a context of $64$ characters, and $126{,}528$ parameters, every one of them a number that training adjusts. Its forward pass is six steps, and each is a matrix operation you could write out.

**1. Embedding.** Each token becomes a vector of $96$ numbers, looked up in a table $E$ ($96 \times 96$ here); each position $1$ to $64$ adds a second vector from a table $P$. Tokens that behave alike end up with similar vectors, because the training below moves them together; the position vector is how the model knows which character came first, since nothing else in attention cares about order.

**2. Query, key, value.** From each token's vector $\mathbf{x}$, three matrices make three new vectors: $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{x}W_q$, $\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{x}W_k$, $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{x}W_v$. The names are a metaphor that holds up: the query is what this position is looking for, the key is what each position advertises, the value is what it hands over if chosen.

**3. Attention.** Position $i$ scores every earlier position $j$ by the dot product $\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j / \sqrt{d}$, sets the score of every *later* position to $-\infty$ so that the future cannot leak, and turns the scores into weights with a softmax. Its new vector is the weighted mix $\sum_j A_{ij}\mathbf{v}_j$. That is all attention is: for every position, a probability distribution over the positions before it, computed from the tokens, and a weighted average taken with it. The Manim clip below runs one such step slowly.

![[language-model-attention.mp4]]

**4. The residual.** The mix is *added* to $\mathbf{x}$, not substituted for it, so each token keeps what it was and gains what it gathered.

**5. A small network.** Each position's vector then passes on its own through a two-layer network, $\max(0, \mathbf{h}W_1 + \mathbf{b}_1)W_2 + \mathbf{b}_2$, again added to what was there. Attention moves information between positions; this step processes it in place. Real models stack the pair, attention then network, a hundred times.

**6. Prediction.** A final matrix turns the last vector into $96$ scores, one per character, and a softmax makes them probabilities. The loss is the negative log of the probability the model gave to the character that actually came next, averaged over every position of every training batch: bits per character, exactly the number the counting models were scored on.

**Training** is [[Artificial Intelligence]]'s gradient descent applied to those $126{,}528$ numbers: run the forward pass on a batch of $32$ windows of $64$ characters, compute the loss, run the **backward pass** to get the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter, and step each parameter a little downhill. The lab writes the backward pass by hand, one line of calculus per line of the forward pass, and before training it checks every kind of parameter against finite differences: the worst disagreement is $1.1 \times 10^{-8}$. That check is the difference between a program that seems to learn and one that does.

![[language-model-attention.svg|700]]

The figure is the attention matrix of the trained model for one probe sentence. Each row is a character deciding what to look at; each row sums to one; the upper triangle is blank because the future is masked. The final *e* of *wrote*, for instance, puts $0.35$ of its weight on the *o* and $0.31$ on the *t* just before it, $0.07$ on the *r*, and only $0.06$ on the space before the word: it is spelling, reading the letters of the word it is inside, and it learned to do that because those letters are what decide the next one. Nobody told the model where words begin. It found that looking there helped it guess.

What the network learned about the letters after *the* is stored in the same weights that handle *a*, *this* and every other word, so evidence from each context helps all of them.

## Part IV — Sampling, and the temperature knob

A trained model gives probabilities; something has to choose. After the seed `The theore` the lab's model says `m` with probability 0.84, then a space and `d` at 0.06 each, then `c`, `s` and `v` at about 0.01. Three ways to choose:

- **Take the most likely token every time** (temperature $0$). The output is the model's single most probable continuation and it is usually dull, and often a loop: the lab's model at temperature $0$ writes `Newton a company and the same the same the same the same the same the same the same the sa`, because once *the same* is the most likely continuation of *the same*, nothing will ever break the cycle.
- **Sample from the distribution, slightly sharpened** (temperature $0.7$). The text is varied and mostly word-shaped: `Newton published eparatus — and the mechanic single sentral had from the have from the construction `.
- **Flatten the distribution first** (temperature above $1$): scores are divided by $\tau$ before the softmax, so unlikely tokens gain ground. At $1.5$ the model produces `Newton lose8s⏎^pZ3) eloyins Folancondal Nich rads.) Tarms jame thristome; Bugney, put, Jun`: creative, and falling apart.

![[language-model-temperature.svg|880]]

The temperature does not change what the model knows; it changes how much of the probability mass the sampler is willing to spend on the tail. This is the whole reason the same question gets different answers on different days: the answers are draws from a distribution, not lookups in a table. Commercial systems add refinements, sampling only from the top few tokens or the top slice of probability, but the knob is the same.

## Part V — What scale bought

The lab's model and a frontier model are the same design at different sizes, and the sizes are the story.

| | the lab's model | a frontier model of the mid-2020s |
|---|---|---|
| parameters | $1.3 \times 10^5$ | $10^{11}$ to $10^{12}$ |
| training text | $10^6$ characters | $10^{13}$ tokens, most of the public internet |
| context | 64 characters | $10^5$ to $10^6$ tokens |
| attention layers | 1, with 1 head | around 100, each with many heads |
| training compute | three minutes of one laptop core | months of tens of thousands of GPUs |
| bits per character | 2.54 on the Stories | below 1 on English text |

The empirical finding that drove the last decade is that the loss falls as a smooth power law in each of parameters, data and compute, over many orders of magnitude, and keeps falling. Nothing in the design changed between a model that writes *the same the same the same* and one that writes a working program; the design was simply scaled until prediction became that good. Capabilities that looked like new kinds of thing, translation, arithmetic, following instructions, appeared along that curve as the next-token loss fell, because each of them is, in the end, a pattern that makes the next token more predictable. [[The GPU — From Triangles to Tensors]] is the reason the scaling could be paid for: attention and the small network are matrix multiplications, and a GPU does nothing else.

## Part VI — From predictor to assistant

The model that comes out of Part V is a completer. Given *Q: What is the capital of France?* it continues with whatever usually follows such lines on the internet, which may be the answer, or another question, or an advertisement. Turning it into something that answers takes two more steps, both of which [[Artificial Intelligence]] already named.

1. **Supervised fine-tuning.** People write a few tens of thousands of example dialogues, question then good answer, and the model is trained a little more on those, so that the most probable continuation of a question becomes an answer.
2. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback.** The model writes several answers, people rank them, a second model learns to predict the rankings, and the language model is trained by reinforcement learning to produce answers the second model scores highly. This is where a model learns to decline, to hedge, to be polite, and also where it can learn to flatter.

What you then talk to is still the completer. The whole conversation, including hidden instructions from the company that runs it, is one long document, and the model's reply is its continuation. It has no memory between conversations unless the system stores one and pastes it back in. It can use tools, search or run code, only because the system watches for a token pattern that means "call this" and inserts the result into the document. And everything it seems to know, it knows in the one way Part III showed: because knowing it lowered the loss.

## What it is not

- **A database.** There is no table of facts to consult. A fact is present only as a tendency in the weights, which is why a model can state a date confidently and wrongly: a plausible date and a true one produce the same fluent sentence, and only the true one was ever rewarded, slightly, during training. Fluency is what the model is built for. Truth is a side effect, and an unreliable one.
- **A copy of the training text.** The lab's counting model with eight characters of context copies whole clauses; the neural model, with a thousandth of the capacity needed to store the corpus, cannot, and its samples are new sentences. Large models can memorise passages they saw many times, and do, but the ordinary output is composed, not retrieved.
- **A reasoner in the sense of a proof checker.** It produces text that reads like reasoning because reasoning-shaped text was in the corpus. It can be made much better at this, and a "think step by step" prompt works precisely because it makes the model write the intermediate tokens it needs. But the check is the reader's job.

## Where this is the working tool

**Your keyboard and your search box.** Predictive text, spelling correction, and the completions in a search bar are small language models running on the phone, and they have been for years. Speech recognition uses one as the prior: the acoustic model hears something between *recognise speech* and *wreck a nice beach*, and the language model decides which is likelier.

**Translation and code.** Machine translation became a language-model problem in 2017, when the transformer was introduced for exactly that task. Code assistants are the same model trained on repositories, which is why they are excellent at boilerplate and dangerous at the edge cases the corpus did not cover; the same rule about checking applies.

**Reading for you.** Summarising a document, answering questions about a course's notes, drafting a reply: each is the completer conditioned on a document pasted into its context. That is the shape of every "AI assistant" in a product, and it is why the quality depends first on what is in the context and only second on the model.

**The cost.** Bits per token is the product metric. A frontier model's training run consumes electricity on the scale of a small city for months, and every answer costs a fraction of a joule per token; the price of a token is now a line in company accounts, and the incentive to make models smaller for the same loss is the incentive that drives the field.

**Hands-on.** `language-model-lab.py`, in this folder, does everything in this card in about three minutes and writes its results to `language-model-lab.json`. Change the seed text; change the temperature; point `load_corpus` at your own writing and see the model learn your habits. Then try the exercise that separates understanding from watching: delete the mask line in `forward` and predict, before running it, what happens to the loss and why the resulting model is useless.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: a counting model's probability

*In a corpus, the two-character context `th` was followed by `e` 41 020 times, by `a` 6 310 times, by `i` 4 480 times, and by other characters 9 190 times. What probability does the bigram-of-characters model give to `e` after `th`, and how many bits does it spend if `e` is what comes next?*

**Trigger:** "followed by … times" is a count table; a counting model's probability is the relative frequency. **Tool: $P = \text{count}/\text{total}$, then bits $= -\log_2 P$** from [[Information Theory]].

$$P(e \mid th) = \frac{41\,020}{41\,020 + 6\,310 + 4\,480 + 9\,190} = \frac{41\,020}{61\,000} = 0.672, \qquad -\log_2 0.672 = 0.57 \text{ bits}.$$

A confident, correct prediction is cheap. Had `q` come next, with a count of zero, the unsmoothed model would charge infinite bits, which is why every real counting model adds a small constant to every count.

### Example 2: bits per character as a file size

*A model scores $2.54$ bits per character on a text of $1{,}144{,}638$ characters. How small could the text be compressed with that model, and what is the model's perplexity?*

**Trigger:** bits per character is a code length; **tool: total bits $=$ rate $\times$ length**, and perplexity $= 2^{\text{bits}}$, both from [[Information Theory]] and [[Compression Is Intelligence]].

$$2.54 \times 1{,}144{,}638 = 2.91 \times 10^6 \text{ bits} = 363 \text{ kB}, \text{ against } 1{,}145 \text{ kB stored one byte per character}; \qquad 2^{2.54} = 5.8.$$

The model, used as a compressor, would shrink the Stories to under a third. And at every character it is, on average, as uncertain as someone choosing among six equally likely letters. A frontier model on English text is below one bit per character: a perplexity under two, which is the "cover the word with your thumb" game played better than you play it.

### Example 3: one attention step by hand

*Three tokens have queries, keys and values in two dimensions: $\mathbf{k}_1 = (1, 0)$, $\mathbf{k}_2 = (0, 1)$, $\mathbf{k}_3 = (1, 1)$; $\mathbf{v}_1 = (2, 0)$, $\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 2)$, $\mathbf{v}_3 = (1, 1)$; the third token's query is $\mathbf{q}_3 = (2, 0)$. With $d = 2$, what does the third token's attention output?*

**Trigger:** "attention output" is the three-line recipe: scores, softmax, weighted mix. **Tools: the dot product for the scores; the softmax; a weighted sum.**

Scores: $\mathbf{q}_3 \cdot \mathbf{k}_j / \sqrt 2 = 2/\sqrt2,\ 0,\ 2/\sqrt2 = 1.41,\ 0,\ 1.41$. Softmax: $e^{1.41} = 4.11$, $e^0 = 1$, so the weights are $4.11/9.22,\ 1/9.22,\ 4.11/9.22 = 0.446,\ 0.108,\ 0.446$. Output: $0.446(2, 0) + 0.108(0, 2) + 0.446(1, 1) = (1.34, 0.66)$.

The third token asked for "first-coordinate" content, its query pointing along the first axis, and got mostly the values of the two tokens whose keys had a first component. Change the query to $(0, 2)$ and the same sentence yields $(0.66, 1.34)$: the same tokens, a different question, a different mix. That is what the Manim clip's second half shows.

### Example 4: the temperature knob on real numbers

*After `The theore` the lab's model gives `m` 0.84, space 0.06, `d` 0.06. What are these three probabilities at temperature $0.5$ and at temperature $2$?*

**Trigger:** temperature acts on the scores, and the scores are the logs of the probabilities. **Tool: raise each probability to the power $1/\tau$ and renormalise**, which is what dividing the scores by $\tau$ before the softmax amounts to.

At $\tau = 0.5$: $0.84^2 = 0.706$, $0.06^2 = 0.0036$, $0.06^2 = 0.0036$; renormalised over the three, $0.990,\ 0.005,\ 0.005$. At $\tau = 2$: $\sqrt{0.84} = 0.917$, $\sqrt{0.06} = 0.245$, $\sqrt{0.06} = 0.245$; renormalised, $0.652,\ 0.174,\ 0.174$.

Low temperature drives the leading token to near certainty, one chance in a hundred of anything else; high temperature lets each of the other two in one time in six. Neither changes the order.

### Example 5: counting the parameters

*The lab's model has vocabulary $96$, width $96$, context $64$, a hidden layer of $4 \times 96 = 384$, and one head. Count its parameters.*

**Trigger:** every matrix and bias in the forward pass is a parameter; count each shape. **Tool: rows times columns, plus biases.**

Embeddings $96 \times 96 = 9\,216$ and positions $64 \times 96 = 6\,144$; the three attention matrices $3 \times 96 \times 96 = 27\,648$; the network $96 \times 384 + 384 + 384 \times 96 + 96 = 73\,920$; the output $96 \times 96 + 96 = 9\,312$. Total $126{,}240 + 288 = 126{,}528$, which is what the lab prints. Scale the width to $12\,288$ and the layers to $96$, and the same arithmetic gives the $1.75 \times 10^{11}$ of a 2020 frontier model; the recipe did not change.

## Common Misconceptions (Teaching Notes)

### 1. "It looks the answer up"

Nothing is stored as an answer. The model computes a probability distribution over the next token from the context, using weights that were tuned to predict text. When it states a fact, it is producing the tokens that usually followed such a question in its training text. That is why it is fluent about things that do not exist.

**Fix:** run the lab and ask the three-character counting model a question. It answers in perfect English rhythm and complete nonsense, and it is the same mechanism.

### 2. "If it sounds right, it is right"

Fluency is the thing the model was optimised for; correctness was rewarded only in so far as correct text was more common than incorrect text on the same topic. For well-trodden topics that is often; for anything rare, recent, numerical or specific to you, it is not. Treat a model's statement as a claim from a confident stranger: a good starting point and never a citation.

### 3. "It just copies the training data"

The lab's neural model has $126{,}528$ parameters and its corpus has $1{,}144{,}638$ characters; it cannot store the text, and its samples are sentences that appear nowhere in the Stories. The counting model with eight characters of context *does* copy, and its bits-per-character score shows what copying is worth on new text: nothing. Large models can and do memorise passages seen many times, but composition, not retrieval, is the normal mode.

### 4. "Temperature zero gives the true answer"

Temperature zero gives the *most probable* continuation, which is the safest guess, not a truer one; a wrong fact that is the most common phrasing in the corpus is exactly what temperature zero returns. It also produces the loops in Part IV.

### 5. "It remembers our earlier conversation"

Between conversations the weights do not change and nothing is stored, unless the product around the model saves a note and pastes it into the next context. Within a conversation the memory is the context window, and when it fills, the earliest tokens fall off the front.

### 6. "Bigger context means it read all of it"

Everything in the window is available to attention, but the weights decide what is used, and a model can attend weakly to the middle of a long document. Put the thing that matters near the question.

## Beyond the syllabus

> [!info] What the lab leaves out, and why it still works
> Recall that the lab's model is one attention head and one layer. Real transformers add three things. **Layer normalisation** rescales each vector before attention and before the network, which keeps training stable at depth. **Multiple heads** run several attentions side by side with smaller $\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k}, \mathbf{v}$ each, so one head can track "which word came before" while another tracks "which noun does this pronoun refer to". **Depth**: the attention-plus-network pair is stacked dozens of times, and the residual additions mean every layer reads and writes the same running vector, the *residual stream*, which is why the design scales. None of these changes what attention is; they multiply how much of it there is.

> [!info] Compression is prediction, again
> The loss in bits per character is not a metaphor for compression; it is a compressor's output length. Feed the same model to an arithmetic coder and the corpus shrinks by exactly the bits the model saves, which is the identity [[Compression Is Intelligence]] measured on this vault's prose. The Hutter Prize, a standing bounty for compressing Wikipedia, has been won by language models for that reason, and a frontier model's compression ratio on English is a fair one-number summary of how much of English it has captured.
