# Inertia and Bootstrapping 惯性与启动

> *A body remains at rest, or in uniform motion in a straight line, unless acted on by a resultant force.*
>
> *That includes you. Especially in the morning.*

## What this card is for

[[Newton's Laws of Motion]] gave Newton's First Law its physics treatment: bodies don't slow down on their own. The Aristotelian prejudice that motion needs a constant push is wrong — what looks like "needing a push" is friction subtracting momentum, and remove the friction and the body coasts forever. The card closed with a one-paragraph "Beyond physics" tip pointing at this card. Now we cash that pointer.

The claim of this card is sharper than "N1 makes a nice metaphor for habits." It is:

> **N1 is not a metaphor for habit formation. The same activation-hump physics that governs friction, chemical reactions, and operating-system boot sequences also governs the human nervous system. Five very different fields measured the hump independently and got the same shape. That's not coincidence — it's a structural property of any system that has to leave one stable state to enter another.**

The technical name for "the same shape recurring across scales and substrates" is a **fractal** (分形, fēnxíng). Strictly, mathematical fractals (the Mandelbrot set, the Koch snowflake, the boundary of a coastline) self-repeat under magnification of the *same* object. The cross-substrate version — friction-mechanics, chemistry, software, cognition all wearing the same hump — is a slightly different formal cousin (universality class / structural isomorphism), but the everyday word for it is the same: this card's claim is that **the activation hump is a fractal of nature**, repeating at every scale where one stable state has to give way to another. Once you see it once, you can't stop seeing it.

The methodology quartet ([[Chain of Thought]], [[Choosing Effective Equations]], [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]], [[Why Probability and Statistics]]) was about *how to think*. This is the fifth card and it asks the question that comes before any of them: **how do you actually start doing the thing every morning?** A hunter who can't leave the cabin doesn't hunt. A problem-solver who can't open the textbook doesn't solve. Reasoning is downstream of *initiation*, and initiation has its own physics.

> [!info] Where this card sits
> [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]] asked *what kind of person solves problems well in the abstract?* This card asks the more practical question one rung down: *how does that person actually get started in the first place?* Pair them. The hunter's craft is reasoning under uncertainty; the bootstrap is the act of becoming the body that does the reasoning. They are the same project at two timescales.

**惯性 (guànxìng)** —— 物体保持当前运动状态的倾向。

**启动 (qǐdòng)** —— 让一个静止的系统动起来；从「不动」到「动」之间的那个跨越。

**媒介 (méijiè)** —— 环境、媒体、所有把人和世界连起来的物质载体。本卡片第二部分的核心策略「环境设计」其实就是 *媒介学* (media studies) 的应用 —— 麦克卢汉 (McLuhan) 1964 年那句「媒介即讯息」(the medium is the message) 讲的就是这件事。

**分形 (fēnxíng)** —— 同一种形状在不同尺度、不同载体上反复出现。这张卡片的核心断言：启动峰是大自然的一个分形。

中文里有一句老话讲得很准：

> **万事开头难。** (wànshì kāitóu nán) —— "Everything is hard at the start."

这一句话总结了这张卡片的全部物理学。从静止到运动需要一个比维持运动更大的能量；这不是比喻，是物理学的客观事实，从牛顿第一定律到摩擦力、化学反应活化能、计算机冷启动、人类执行功能 —— 全是同一种「启动峰」(activation hump)。

> **核心结论：不要用意志力对抗惯性，要用环境设计降低启动峰。**

适用范围：起床、写作、运动、学习、项目启动、戒掉坏习惯、培养新习惯、写论文、写作业、动笔、动身、动心。

# Part I — Five faces of the same physics

## 1. Newton's First Law, applied to a nervous system

From [[Newton's Laws of Motion]]:

> *A body remains at rest, or in uniform motion in a straight line, unless acted on by a resultant force.*

The temptation is to call the application to humans a metaphor and move on. **Resist that temptation.** The brain is a physical system — neurons, glia, synapses, electrical currents, neurotransmitter reservoirs, all sitting in a body that has weight, body temperature, blood pressure, and inertia in the literal sense. The state of "lying in bed" and the state of "running" are two stable configurations of that physical system, and getting from one to the other requires a real expenditure of energy that the system has to find from somewhere.

The deeper claim is that the *shape* of this expenditure — a hump that's higher at the start than the steady-state cost — is a generic property of any physical system that has to leave one stable state to enter another. We'll see this hump four more times in the next four sections, in four very different physical regimes. The fact that humans show the same hump is not a metaphor. It's a structural inevitability.

> [!tip] The two stable states
> Notice what N1 actually says: bodies prefer to keep doing what they're doing. *Both* "not moving" and "moving steadily" are stable states under N1 — friction is what makes the second one *look* unstable on Earth. The honest reading: the cost of switching states is what's expensive, not the maintenance of either one. Once you've been running for ten minutes, running is *easy* relative to the first thirty seconds. The first thirty seconds were the trade.

## 2. Static vs kinetic friction — why the bus lurches

The first place the activation hump shows up cleanly in physics is in the friction model from [[Friction (Vocab)]]:

$$F \leq \mu_s R \quad \text{(static)} \qquad F = \mu_k R \quad \text{(kinetic)}$$

with the empirical fact that for the same pair of surfaces, $\mu_s > \mu_k$ — almost always, and often by a factor of $1.2$ to $1.5$. The static coefficient is *bigger* than the kinetic coefficient. This is why:

- **The bus lurches when it starts.** That hiccup you feel at the moment of departure is static friction releasing and kinetic friction taking over; the resistance suddenly drops, the wheels grip differently, and the carriage jerks forward.
- **A heavy box "breaks loose" the moment you push hard enough.** Below the static ceiling, friction is whatever you push with — the box doesn't move. Push past the ceiling and friction *suddenly* drops to the kinetic value and the box accelerates from a standstill. The whole experience is "stuck, stuck, stuck, stuck — *go*", not a smooth ramp.
- **Door hinges squeak louder on the first centimetre.** The dry-contact static welds at rest take more torque to break than the smooth slide afterward.

### Why is $\mu_s > \mu_k$? The cold-weld picture

The atomic-level explanation is that any two surfaces in stationary contact have *time* to settle into each other. Microscopic asperities (rough peaks on each surface) sink into the opposing surface; intermolecular forces (van der Waals attractions, sometimes incipient covalent bonds) pull contact points together; the longer the surfaces sit, the more of these "cold welds" form. To start sliding, all the welds at the current contact patch must break *simultaneously*, which costs more force than maintaining sliding (where new welds form continuously but never have time to fully mature before being torn).

This is the first appearance of the universal pattern: **a stationary system has had time to settle into the local equilibrium; leaving that equilibrium costs more than the steady-state cost of motion.** The longer the rest, the deeper the welds, the higher the hump. (This is why a habit you've broken for a year is harder to restart than one you broke yesterday — the welds had time to set.)

## 3. Activation energy in chemistry

The same shape, in a totally different physical regime. The **Arrhenius equation** (Svante Arrhenius, 1889) describes how the rate $k$ of a chemical reaction depends on temperature:

$$k = A \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right)$$

where $E_a$ is the **activation energy** (活化能, huóhuànéng), $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, and $A$ is a frequency factor. The exponential dependence on $-E_a / RT$ is the key piece — it says the rate of reaction is *exponentially* suppressed by the size of the activation hump.

The picture chemistry students learn is the **reaction coordinate diagram**:

```
energy
  │       ┌─── transition state
  │      /  \
  │     /    \
  │    /      \
  │   /        \         (height of hump = E_a)
  │  /          \
  │─/            \─── products  (lower than reactants → exothermic)
  │ reactants
  │
  └──────────────────── reaction coordinate
```

The reactants sit in a stable valley. The products sit in another stable valley, often lower (energy is released in an exothermic reaction). But to go from one valley to the other, the molecules have to climb over a **transition state** in between — a high-energy configuration where bonds are partially broken and partially formed. The height of that hump is the activation energy $E_a$, and the Arrhenius equation says the reaction rate is exponentially sensitive to it.

### What catalysts actually do

This is where the chemistry side pays the most pedagogical rent for this card. **A catalyst lowers the activation energy without changing the energies of reactants or products.** It does *not* push the reaction harder; it does *not* supply energy. It changes the *shape of the path* between the two valleys, finding a route over a smaller hump.

This is the model the productivity literature has been groping toward for fifty years. Willpower, in the productivity analogy, is the strategy of *raising your kinetic energy* (i.e., pushing harder, eating more sugar, hyping yourself up) so that more molecules have enough energy to clear the hump. **Catalysis is the strategy of lowering the hump itself** — laying out your gym clothes, pre-writing tomorrow's first sentence, putting the meditation app on the home screen, removing the password from your laptop. The catalyst does not give you more energy. It changes the shape of the path so that the energy you *already have* is enough.

The Arrhenius exponential is what makes this so leveraged: a small reduction in $E_a$ produces an exponential increase in $k$. **Small environmental changes produce disproportionately large behavioural changes.** That's not motivational-poster optimism; it's $e^{-E_a/RT}$ being very sensitive to $E_a$.

> [!info] Why life evolved enzymes
> Enzymes are catalysts — proteins that lower activation energies for biochemical reactions by orders of magnitude. A reaction that would take centuries at body temperature in pure water completes in milliseconds inside an enzyme's active site. The whole evolutionary point is that **at body temperature, raising $T$ further is not an option** (the cell cooks), so the *only* lever evolution had was lowering $E_a$. Every enzyme in your body is a few billion years of natural selection saying "lower the hump, don't push harder." The lesson generalises further than chemistry.

## 4. Cold start in software

Same shape, third regime. Every distributed system, programming language runtime, and database has a documented "cold start" problem — the first request after a period of inactivity is dramatically slower than steady-state requests, by anywhere from $10\times$ to $1000\times$. A short tour, starting with the example everyone has lived a hundred times:

- **Turning on a computer or phone.** The single most universal cold-start experience on Earth. Press the power button on a laptop and watch a $30$–$90$-second sequence of BIOS / UEFI handshakes, kernel boot, driver initialisation, file-system mounting, login service, window-manager startup, and background-daemon launch — *before* the desktop is responsive. Once everything is loaded, opening the next app takes a fraction of a second. Phones do the same dance more elegantly (and faster, on warm boot, because the OS keeps state in flash memory) but the hump is identical: the first second after power-on is the most expensive second in the device's day. Every other example below is a smaller-scale repetition of this one.
- **AWS Lambda cold start.** A serverless function that hasn't been invoked recently is not running. The first request has to spin up a container, load the runtime, JIT-compile the function code, establish database connections, and warm up any caches — typically $200\,\text{ms}$–$5\,\text{s}$ for the first request, then $<10\,\text{ms}$ for each subsequent request that hits the same warm container. AWS publishes detailed mitigation guides because the hump is real and expensive.
- **JIT compiler warmup.** V8 (the JavaScript engine in Chrome and Node.js) and the JVM (Java) both *interpret* code on first execution and only *compile* it after observing it run hot enough to be worth optimising. The first thousand calls to a function are slow; the millionth call is fast. JIT warmup is so significant that Java benchmarks routinely include a warmup phase to discard before measuring "real" performance.
- **Database connection pool.** Establishing a TCP connection, performing TLS handshake, authenticating to the database, and opening a session takes $50$–$500\,\text{ms}$. Reusing an already-open connection takes $<1\,\text{ms}$. Production systems maintain *connection pools* — a small inventory of pre-warmed connections — precisely to avoid the cold-start hump on every request.
- **TCP slow start.** Even at the network protocol level, the very same shape: a new TCP connection starts with a small congestion window and *exponentially* grows it as packets are successfully delivered. The first few packets travel slowly; later packets travel at link speed. RFC 5681 names the phenomenon literally "slow start."
- **CDN cache miss.** A content delivery network's first request for a new file has to fetch from origin (slow); the second request from the cached edge node is fast. The hump is the trip to origin; warming the cache flattens it.

The unifying pattern: **every system that has both a "running" state and a "not running" state pays a one-time cost to transition.** Engineers have responded with a uniform vocabulary — pre-warming, pooling, caching, JIT, eager initialisation — that is, in every case, the software equivalent of the chemistry catalyst: *lower the hump in advance so the first request is fast.*

The same physics runs in reverse, and it is just as important: once a system *is* moving, changing its direction costs the hump — which is why computing's past never really dies, and why every interface is secretly a museum ([[The Ghost of Drive A]]). Inertia is not only the enemy of starting; it is the guardian of everything already started.

> [!info] Beyond syllabus — the bootstrap problem in compilers
> *Recall that [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]] mentions self-hosting compilers as one of the formal twins of "discovery is an instance of the discovered."* The literal compiler bootstrap is also a cold-start problem. To compile a compiler written in language X, you need an existing compiler for X. The very first compiler had to be hand-translated to machine code (huge activation cost!); every subsequent compiler can use the previous one to compile itself. **The first hump is the worst hump.** Once a language has a working compiler, evolving it costs almost nothing — you compile each new version with the previous version. The expression "pulling oneself up by one's bootstraps" is mathematically accurate here: there is no external lever; the system has to start itself, and starting is the entire cost.

## 5. Executive function — task initiation as the choke point

The fifth and most personal regime. Cognitive psychology has, since the 1990s, identified **task initiation** (or "behavioural activation") as a *distinct* executive function — separate from planning, separate from working memory, separate from inhibition. Some people are great planners but cannot start. Some can start anything but cannot finish. The components are dissociable.

The clinical literature on **ADHD** names this explicitly. Children and adults with ADHD often score normally on tests of *intelligence*, *knowledge*, and even *planning* — but they show a specific deficit in moving from "I should do X" to "I am doing X". Russell Barkley's executive-function model lists task initiation as one of seven distinguishable executive skills; treatment plans target it specifically. The hump is real, measurable, and clinically named.

The non-clinical population has the same hump, just at a lower amplitude. The morning hesitation before getting out of bed, the writer's block before the first sentence, the "okay, one more YouTube video and *then* I'll start" — these are not character flaws. They're the static-friction ceiling for a brain. The brain has to break a few cold welds (the comfort of the current state, the unknown of the next state, the activation costs of whichever motor program the new task requires) before kinetic-mode work can begin.

### What the productivity research actually says

The popular productivity literature, when it's grounded, is mostly variations on a single move: **lower the activation cost.** A short tour of the load-bearing findings:

- **Implementation intentions** (Peter Gollwitzer, 1999, *American Psychologist*). The classic finding: replacing the goal "I will exercise more" with the *plan* "I will go to the gym at 6 PM on Monday, Wednesday, Friday after I finish work" reliably triples adherence. Why it works: the plan pre-binds the cue to the action, removing the in-the-moment decision (which is the activation cost). You don't have to deliberate at 6 PM; you have to *execute*.
- **Habit formation as cue-routine-reward** (Charles Duhigg, *The Power of Habit*, 2012, popularising the academic literature on basal-ganglia learning). A habit is a behaviour where the *cue* directly triggers the *routine* without conscious deliberation. The deliberation is the hump; once the routine is automated, it runs at kinetic-friction cost. Building a habit is building a catalyst.
- **Habit stacking** (BJ Fogg, *Tiny Habits*, 2019; James Clear, *Atomic Habits*, 2018). Attach the new behaviour to an existing automated cue. ("After I pour my morning coffee, I will read for ten minutes.") The previous habit's *kinetic state* becomes the new habit's *initial momentum* — you're not starting from rest, you're transferring from a body that's already moving.
- **Environmental design** (Wendy Wood, *Good Habits, Bad Habits*, 2019 — the most rigorous academic synthesis). The single largest predictor of habit success is not motivation, identity, or willpower; it's **friction**. Make the desired behaviour low-friction (running shoes by the door, water bottle on the desk, app on the home screen) and unwanted behaviours high-friction (junk food in a different room, social-media app in a folder behind a password). Behaviour follows the friction gradient. Not metaphorically.
- **The two-minute rule** (David Allen, *Getting Things Done*, 2001; later popularised by James Clear). If the new habit can be started in under two minutes, start. The two-minute version of "exercise" is "put on running shoes." The two-minute version of "write a paper" is "open the document." The two-minute rule tricks the static-to-kinetic transition by keeping the activation cost below the brain's hump.

These are not five different theories. They are five operationalisations of the same insight: **the cost is the start, not the steady state. Manage the start.**

> [!warning] What the research does *not* say
> Popular productivity culture conflates two findings that are actually quite different. **Wendy Wood's habit-science is well-established**; the behavioural-economics finding that environmental friction dominates intention is robust across replications. **Roy Baumeister's "ego depletion" / willpower-as-finite-resource** literature, by contrast, has *not* replicated reliably (the 2016 Hagger et al. multi-lab replication failed to find the original effect at full size). The pop-version "willpower is a muscle that gets tired" is not a settled finding.
>
> What this means for the card: don't lean on the strong claim "willpower is finite." The weaker, well-supported claim is enough. *Willpower is expensive at any level — measured by the fact that environmental design beats it on every replication that compared the two.* You don't need the willpower-tank model to conclude that environment is the better lever; the comparative finding is direct evidence.

## The unifying shape

Five physical regimes, one diagram:

```
  cost / energy / effort
    │
    │       ╱╲   ← activation hump
    │      ╱  ╲     (μ_s, E_a, cold-start, task-init)
    │     ╱    ╲
    │    ╱      ╲___________  ← steady-state cost
    │   ╱        (μ_k, k, warm, habit-mode)
    │  ╱
    │_╱
    │  state A         state B
    └──────────────────────────────
              transition
```

- **Mechanics:** $\mu_s > \mu_k$, the cold-weld picture, the bus lurch.
- **Chemistry:** $E_a > 0$, the transition state, $k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$ exponentially punishing the hump.
- **Software:** cold-start vs. warm-start, JIT, connection pools, TCP slow start.
- **Cognition:** task-initiation as a discrete executive skill; the morning hesitation, the writer's block, the ADHD initiation deficit.

These are not analogies of one another. They are *instances* of the same generic principle: **any system that has to leave one stable configuration to enter another pays a one-time transition cost that exceeds its steady-state cost.** The ratio between the two costs varies by regime, but the shape — a hump higher than the plateau — is universal.

# Part II — The momentum corollary

Knowing the hump exists is half the card. The other half is what to do about it. Five tactics fall out of the physics, in increasing order of leverage.

## 1. Lower the hump (the catalyst principle)

This is the master move. **Don't push harder; reduce the activation cost.** Every effective technique below is a special case.

The reason it dominates "push harder" is the Arrhenius exponential: the rate of starting is *exponentially* sensitive to the height of the hump. Small reductions in friction produce large increases in initiation rate. This is the same reason enzymes evolved instead of warmer body temperatures — at any fixed energy budget, the catalyst beats the brute force.

**Concrete examples:**
- Lay out tomorrow's gym clothes the night before. ($E_a$ for the morning workout drops by minutes of decision.)
- Pre-write the first sentence of a paper before going to bed. (The blank-page hump becomes a continue-where-I-left-off plateau.)
- Put the meditation app on the home screen; bury social media in a folder. (Friction gradient becomes a friction *cliff*.)
- Charge the laptop at the desk where you want to work; charge the phone in another room. (The body that moves to the laptop is the one that ends up working.)

## 2. Stack onto existing momentum (habit stacking)

Newton's First Law's other half: **bodies in motion stay in motion.** A body that's already running at kinetic-friction cost does not pay the static-friction premium for any continuation that fits the existing motion. The cheapest new behaviour is one that rides an existing one.

**The form:** `After I ⟨existing automatic behaviour⟩, I will ⟨new behaviour⟩.`

- "After I make my morning coffee, I will write for ten minutes."
- "After I close my laptop in the evening, I will do five minutes of stretching."
- "After I sit on the train, I will open the textbook."

The existing behaviour pays the activation cost; the new behaviour pays only the (small) marginal cost of an additional motion. This is why stacking dominates "schedule the new behaviour at a fixed time" — the fixed-time version still has to fight static friction in isolation.

## 3. Engineer the environment (Wendy Wood's lever)

The most leveraged tactic, because it operates *before* willpower is invoked. The environment determines what's easy and what's hard; behaviour follows the gradient. **Don Norman**'s concept of an *affordance* — a property of an object that suggests its use — is the right vocabulary. A book on the desk affords reading. A book on the shelf does not. A phone on the nightstand affords scrolling. A phone in another room does not.

**Field-test rule:** when designing your day, look at your physical and digital environment with this question: *what is each object whispering to me?* Then change the objects, not your discipline. The whispering is the whole game.

> [!info] Beyond syllabus — environmental design *is* media studies (媒介学)
> The academic discipline that has been telling us this for sixty years is **media studies** — the study of *media* (媒介, méijiè) in the broad sense: any technology, environment, or material substrate that mediates between a person and the world. The Canadian theorist **Marshall McLuhan** (*Understanding Media*, 1964) gave the field its motto: **"the medium is the message."** McLuhan's claim was that the *content* you consume (a news article, a movie, a sermon) matters far less than the *medium* through which you consume it (newspaper vs television vs phone vs in-person), because the medium silently shapes attention, time-budget, social posture, and cognitive habit before any specific content can register. Wendy Wood's friction-dominates-intention finding is McLuhan in lab clothes; "lay out tomorrow's gym clothes" is McLuhan with a duffel bag.
>
> The genealogy is broader than McLuhan. **Harold Innis** (*The Bias of Communication*, 1951) is the upstream source — empires shaped by clay tablets vs papyrus vs printing presses behave differently, because the medium biases what's easy to record, copy, and remember. **Neil Postman** (*Amusing Ourselves to Death*, 1985) extended McLuhan to the television age. **Nicholas Carr** (*The Shallows*, 2010) extended it to the internet — the claim that hyperlinked, interrupt-driven media reshape the brain's reading habits is the productivity-card claim writ large at the civilisation scale. **Jean Baudrillard** (*Simulacra and Simulation*, 1981) generalised it to the level of culture itself.
>
> The point for this card: when you "engineer your environment", you are practising applied media studies — choosing which media will *mediate* your day, knowing they will shape what you do far more than your declared intentions will. The home screen of your phone is a medium. The lighting of your desk is a medium. The chair, the wall colour, the music, the open browser tabs, the people in the room — all media in McLuhan's sense, all silently authoring the next ten minutes of your behaviour.
>
> **This subject belongs to hunters.** Recall that [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]] argued that a hunter is someone who can constantly trace causality. Most of the causality you'll ever need to trace runs through a medium. A hunter who can't read media is missing the substrate on which most of modern life is staged. McLuhan, Innis, Postman, Carr — they were all hunters in this card's sense, tracing causality through the most invisible terrain there is. The reason media studies is so often dismissed as "soft" is exactly that the medium hides itself; only the trace makes it visible. The trace is the hunter's job.

## 4. The two-minute rule (the catalyst pre-shaper)

If the desired behaviour can be defined as a two-minute version of itself, start there. The static-friction ceiling for "go for a run" is high; the ceiling for "put on running shoes" is low. Once the shoes are on, kinetic friction takes over and most people finish the run. The trick is not that two minutes is sufficient — it isn't — but that two minutes *crosses the hump* and lets steady-state take over.

This is Allen's GTD insight, popularised by Clear. It's a special case of "lower the hump" by *redefining the goal* so the hump is below the ceiling.

## 5. Audit the inherited motion (the inertia trap)

The First Law cuts both ways. Bodies in motion stay in motion *whether or not they are moving in the direction you want.* The same physics that makes good habits compound makes bad habits compound; the same momentum that makes a project worth continuing makes a doomed project hard to kill. The *body* doesn't know the difference between a habit you chose and a habit you inherited from a flatmate, an ex, an algorithm, a tired Tuesday.

So the bootstrap card has to close with an audit:

> **Of the motions I am currently maintaining at kinetic-friction cost — eating habits, sleep schedule, scrolling rituals, conversational defaults, work patterns, emotional postures — which would I choose to start today, from rest, knowing what I now know?**

The motions that fail this test are the ones to *deliberately* fight to stop, paying the painful one-time deceleration cost — exactly because their kinetic-friction maintenance cost is so deceptively cheap. (You don't notice the bad habit; that's the point. It runs at near-zero conscious cost, like all stable kinetic states.)

This connects directly back to [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]]. The wishful-invariant failure mode there ("Pitou can resurrect Kite") shows up here as the inherited-motion failure mode ("this routine is who I am, so it must be working"). The hunter's audit and the bootstrap audit are the same audit at different timescales: *read what is, even when it costs you what you wanted.* Some of the things you maintain are wishes that hardened into routines, and the cost of stopping them is high precisely because you have been paying the maintenance cost for years.

## "Set up the environment, momentum carries you"

The whole card in one sentence: **don't fight the activation hump with willpower; lower the hump by designing the environment, then let steady-state physics take over.**

This is the practical advice that falls out of taking N1 seriously as a description of the human nervous system. It's also the advice that almost every productivity school, properly stripped, eventually arrives at — Stoicism's emphasis on what's controllable (the environment) over what isn't (your in-the-moment motivation), Zen's emphasis on disciplined daily form, monastic rules of life, the design school's "shape the cue", behavioural economics' "nudge", James Clear's atomic habits. They are all the same Arrhenius observation: **lower the hump.**

The advice that *doesn't* age well — gritting your teeth, motivational hype, self-flagellation, willpower hagiographies — keeps trying to push molecules over the hump by raising their kinetic energy. It's expensive, it doesn't scale, and it loses to a five-second environmental tweak almost every time.

## When the model breaks — limits and honest caveats

A short audit, in the spirit of [[Why Probability and Statistics]]'s Part II.

**1. Physical inertia is conservative; cognitive inertia isn't.** A frictionless puck coasts forever; an unmotivated person does not. Brains have all sorts of internal forces — fatigue, mood, hunger, rumination — that don't appear in the simple N1 picture. The card's claim is not that brains are *literally* Newtonian; it's that the *activation hump* (the hard-to-start part) maps cleanly. The steady-state part is more complicated than uniform motion.

**2. The hump's height is not fixed.** Sleep, mood, weather, social context, prior workload, blood sugar — all change the activation cost from day to day. An environment that catalyses on Monday may not on Friday. The honest model is that you're managing a *distribution* of activation costs, not a single number, and the environmental tactics shift the distribution leftward without eliminating its variance.

**3. Some behaviours genuinely *require* high effort, not just initiation.** Hard intellectual work, creative breakthroughs, training adaptations — these have steady-state costs that don't drop to "kinetic friction" levels. The hump model handles initiation; it does not promise that *maintenance* is free. A long-distance runner still has to run.

**4. The "set up the environment" move can be overdone.** Spending three months designing the perfect productivity system is itself a sophisticated avoidance behaviour — the activation cost of *starting* the actual work has been displaced into the activation cost of *configuring tools*. The two-minute rule applies to system-design too. Pick one tweak. Try it. Iterate.

**5. The model says nothing about *what to do*, only how to start doing it.** Every section of Part II is silent on the question "is the thing I'm trying to start worth doing?" That question belongs to [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]] (read the situation; don't pursue wishful invariants), [[Why Probability and Statistics]] (don't be misled by summary statistics about success), and [[Chain of Thought]] (work out the consequences of the goal before optimising the start). The bootstrap is downstream of the choice. Start the right thing well; that's the whole pipeline.

## Pedagogical bottom line

For students reading this card: most of the productivity advice you'll encounter in the next decade of your life is some version of this physics. Recognise the shape and you can compress the genre. There are five faces of the hump and one corollary; the rest is variation.

For students taking exams in the next month: this card's most actionable claim is that *you cannot exam-prep your way out of static-friction-on-the-textbook by trying harder*. You exam-prep by *changing where the textbook is*. Open it on the kitchen table the night before. Pre-write the first practice question's setup. Put your phone in a different room. The morning's static friction will be lower; the morning self will *feel* more disciplined; you will have caught yourself with a catalyst. The discipline was a downstream effect, not a cause.

For the rest of life: the audit at the end of Part II is the highest-leverage move in the card. Most of what you do is inherited motion. The list of motions you would *choose* to start today, from rest, is much shorter than the list of motions you are currently maintaining. The gap is the work. Closing the gap — paying the one-time deceleration cost on the wrong motions, and the one-time activation cost on the right ones — is what the card calls **bootstrapping**.

Newton wrote the equation in 1687. Arrhenius wrote it in 1889. Cold-start engineers re-derived it for the cloud era around 2010. Cognitive psychologists pinned down task initiation around 2000. The students reading this card in 2026 inherit all four versions and can use any of them as the handle. The handle doesn't matter — the principle is the same. **Lower the hump.**
