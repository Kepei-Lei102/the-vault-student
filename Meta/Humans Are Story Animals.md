# Humans Are Story Animals 人是故事的动物

> Ten words: *lumberjack, duck, quilt, hedge, pillow, stream, nurse, colony, vest, moat.* Close your eyes. How many can you give back, in order?
>
> Now this: *A **lumberjack** darted out of the forest, skirted a **duck**, tripped on a **quilt** left on a **hedge**, and landed on a **pillow** by the **stream**, where a **nurse** from the **colony**, in a bright **vest**, was swimming the **moat**.* Close your eyes again.

## Try it before you read on

The second version is three times longer and much sillier, and it is the one you can give back. Nothing about the words changed. What changed is that each word now has a *reason* to follow the one before it.

In 1969 Gordon Bower and Michal Clark ran this as an experiment. Students learned twelve lists of ten unrelated nouns. Half studied the lists however they liked. The other half were told to weave each list into a story of their own making. At the end, asked for all 120 words, the students who had simply studied recalled a median of **13 %** of them. The students who had made up stories recalled **93 %**. Same words and the same study time.

The claim to be examined is this: **the human mind holds on to chains of cause and purpose far better than it holds on to separate facts, and a story is what a chain of cause and purpose looks like from the inside.** That is good news for anyone learning mathematics, physics or computing, because those subjects are made of such chains. It also comes with two traps, and both are described further down.

*The oldest classroom. Nobody round the fire is memorising a list of facts about lions. They are following what happened to somebody, and the facts come along.*

## 中文锚点

上课听老师讲了一个笑话，十年以后你还记得；同一节课黑板上的那条公式，下周就忘了。这不是因为你不用功，而是因为人脑记东西的方式本来就偏心：零散的事实，它留不住几条；可是“谁想要什么、遇到了什么麻烦、后来怎么样了”这样一环扣一环的经过，它几乎不费力就能记住，因为每一环都是下一环的原因，想起一环，后面的就自己跟着出来了。数学的推导、物理的机制、程序的运行，其实也是这样一环扣一环的因果链，只是平时被写成了一条条孤立的结论。学的时候多问一句“是因为什么，才有了下一步”，把结论还原成一段经过，它就从你记不住的那一类，变成了你忘不掉的那一类。要当心的只有一点：放在知识旁边的有趣故事，会把知识本身挤掉；故事必须就是那条因果链，才帮得上忙。

## 1. Three experiments

### Two triangles and a circle (1944)

Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel showed students a film of about two and a half minutes. It contained a large triangle, a small triangle, a disc, and a rectangle with a flap that opened and closed. The shapes moved about. That is a complete description of what was on the screen.

Asked to write down what happened, nearly every viewer wrote a drama. The large triangle was a bully. The small triangle and the disc were friends, or lovers. There was a fight, a chase, a hiding place, an escape. Viewers gave the shapes wishes, fears and characters, and they agreed with one another about who the villain was.

![[story-animals-triangles-comic.png|720]]
*What was on the screen, and what was in the room.*

No one decides to do this. It happens before you can stop it, and it shows what kind of explanation the mind reaches for first: **somebody wanted something, so something happened.**

### The lists (1969)

This is the experiment you tried at the top. The size of the effect is the point: not a few per cent, but a factor of seven, produced by an instruction that takes one sentence to give. Bower and Clark's explanation was that the story gives every word a place in a connected structure, so that recalling any part helps to rebuild the rest.

### Seventy-five samples (2021)

One experiment can mislead, so it matters what happens when the whole literature is pooled. Raymond Mar and colleagues collected every study they could find that compared memory and comprehension for **narrative** texts (things happening to someone) against **expository** texts (explanations and descriptions): more than 75 samples and more than 33,000 participants. Narratives were remembered better and understood better. The result did not depend on any single study, and it held across the ways the studies differed.

> [!info] What these results do not show
> They compare texts, not teachers, and a narrative text differs from an expository one in several ways at once: more familiar content, simpler vocabulary, people in it. They do not show that everything should be taught as a tale, and they do not show that a story makes a false thing true. They show that a connected account of events is a format the mind handles unusually well. What to do with that is the subject of everything below.

## 2. What a story is, mechanically

"Story" sounds soft. It has a definite structure. The psychologist Daniel Willingham summarises it as four Cs:

| | What it is | In "The king died, and then the queen died of grief" |
|---|---|---|
| **Causality** | each event happens *because of* an earlier one | "of grief" |
| **Conflict** | someone wants something and something is in the way | she wanted him alive |
| **Complications** | the attempts to get past the obstacle, which create new problems | none here; this is why it is one sentence and not a novel |
| **Character** | someone whose wants make the events matter | the queen |

Take away "of grief" and you have the novelist E. M. Forster's other sentence, "The king died, and then the queen died": two facts in time order. (Forster, who made the pair famous, kept the word "story" for the bare sequence and called the causal version a "plot". In the everyday sense used here, the causal link is what turns a sequence into a story.) It is also the link that memory uses. Tom Trabasso and Paul van den Broek showed this directly in 1985. They drew each story as a network, with an arrow wherever one event caused or enabled another, and then looked at what readers recalled. **The more causal connections an event had, and the more firmly it sat on the main causal chain, the more likely it was to be remembered, to be put in a summary, and to be judged important.** Events off the chain were the ones forgotten.

Put that beside two other facts. A person can hold only about four separate things in mind at once, which is the bottleneck described in [[The True IO Bound]]. And a hunter, in the sense of [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]], is someone who can constantly trace causality. A story gets round the first fact by means of the second. Ten unconnected nouns are ten things. A chain in which each link produces the next is *one* thing with a rule for unrolling it, in the spirit of [[Compression Is Intelligence]], where a short rule that regenerates a long sequence is worth more than the sequence. That last connection is an interpretation and not a finding from the studies above, but it fits them: what is stored is the rule, and recall is running it forward.

## 3. Turning a result into a story

Here is the practical move. Most of what you are given to learn arrives as conclusions: a formula, a definition, a procedure. Behind every one of them is a chain that produced it. **Put the chain back.**

Ask four questions of anything you need to learn. They are the four Cs, pointed at a piece of mathematics or science.

1. **Who wanted what?** What problem was someone trying to solve?
2. **What was in the way?** Why did the obvious approach fail?
3. **What was tried, and what did it cost?** What was the move, and what new difficulty did it create?
4. **What follows from what?** Can you say "because" between every pair of neighbouring steps?

**An example: the irrationality of $\sqrt2$.** As a conclusion it is one line. As a story it goes like this. The Pythagoreans *wanted* every length to be a ratio of whole numbers, because their picture of the world depended on it. The diagonal of a unit square was *in the way*. Suppose it is a ratio $p/q$ in lowest terms. Then $p^2 = 2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even, so $p$ is even, so $p = 2r$, so $4r^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2$ is even, so $q$ is even. *So* both are even, and the fraction was not in lowest terms after all. Every "so" is a causal link, and the contradiction at the end is the twist. A student who can tell it in this form has not memorised a proof. They can regenerate it, which is the test set out in [[The Feynman Technique]].

**A second example: the displacement current**, which [[Maxwell's Equations]] develops in full under "The patch". Ampère's law worked for steady currents. Maxwell *wanted* the four laws to be consistent with the conservation of charge. A charging capacitor was *in the way*: current flows in the wires and none crosses the gap, so the law gives two different answers for the same loop. His *move* was to add a term for the changing electric field in the gap. The *cost* was a new prediction nobody had asked for, that changing fields sustain each other and travel, and the speed came out as the speed of light. Conflict, complication and a surprise ending, and every step is physics.

**The "because" test.** Question 4 is the one to keep. Read your notes on a topic and try to put "because" or "so" between each pair of neighbouring lines. Wherever you cannot, you have found either a gap in the notes or a gap in your understanding, and Trabasso and van den Broek's result says that is exactly the item you were going to forget.

This is also why the history of a discovery is worth knowing, with its difficult people left difficult. A discovery told with its wanting, its obstacle and its cost is the discovery in the format you are built to keep.

## 4. Reading an exam question twice

Exam questions are often dressed as small stories: a locomotive on a hill, a shop's database, a patient's scan. Students are very good at this part, usually better than they expect, provided they are told that there are **two readings and both are wanted**.

- **What could this mean in reality?** Picture it. Would it really go like this? What has been left out?
- **What does it mean for the exam?** Each phrase of the story is an instruction about which model to use.

### A real question, read both ways

> *Cambridge 9709 Paper 42, November 2025, Question 2.* A railway locomotive of mass 240 000 kg is towing a coach of mass 36 000 kg down a hill inclined at an angle of $\sin^{-1} 0.04$ to the horizontal. The driving force produced by the locomotive is 450 000 N and there are resistances to motion of 120 000 N on the locomotive and 15 000 N on the coach. The coupling between the locomotive and the coach is light, rigid and parallel to the hill. Find the acceleration of the locomotive and the tension in the coupling. [5]

| The story says | In reality | For the exam |
|---|---|---|
| "locomotive … towing a coach" | two vehicles joined by a coupling that has slack, springs and mass | two particles; draw them separately when the coupling force is wanted |
| "down a hill … $\sin^{-1} 0.04$" | a 4 % gradient is steep for a railway; the driver would be braking, not at full power | weight has a component $mg \sin\theta = 0.04\,mg$ **helping** the motion |
| "driving force … 450 000 N" | of the order of the greatest pull a large locomotive can manage, and only at low speed; it falls as speed rises | a constant force up the list of forces, acting on the locomotive only |
| "resistances … 120 000 N … 15 000 N" | friction and air resistance, which grow with speed | constants, one on each body, opposing motion |
| "light" | a real coupling is a heavy steel casting | its mass is ignored, so the force at its two ends is equal: one $T$ |
| "rigid" | it can push as well as pull | both bodies share one acceleration, and $T$ may come out negative (a thrust) |
| "parallel to the hill" | near enough | no resolving of $T$: it acts along the line of motion |

**The exam reading gives the method.** *Tool: Newton's second law on the whole system, then on one part.* Trigger: "find the acceleration **and** the tension" with two connected bodies that share an acceleration. The internal force cancels for the whole and appears for the part.

Whole train, down the slope, with $g = 10$ as the paper specifies:
$450\,000 + 276\,000 \times 10 \times 0.04 - 135\,000 = 276\,000\,a$, so $425\,400 = 276\,000\,a$ and $a = 1.54\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

Coach alone: $T + 36\,000 \times 10 \times 0.04 - 15\,000 = 36\,000 \times 1.5413$, so $T = 56\,100$ N. The published mark scheme gives $a = 1.54\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ and $T = 56\,100$ N.

**The reality reading gives the check.** An acceleration of $1.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ is more than a city metro train, built to accelerate hard, usually manages, and here a 276-tonne train is doing it downhill under full power. As a real event it is alarming. As an exam answer it is right, because the question asked what the *model* does. Holding both readings at once is a skill in its own right: the first keeps your physical sense alive and catches the answer that is wrong by a factor of a thousand, and the second gets the marks. [[When a Model Breaks]] takes the first reading further, to the point where the gap between the model and the world is the thing being studied.

> [!tip] The words that are instructions
> Exam stories use a small fixed vocabulary, and every item in it switches something off. *Light* removes a mass. *Smooth* removes friction. *Inextensible* makes two accelerations equal. *Particle* removes size and rotation. *Uniform* puts the weight at the middle. *Initially at rest* sets a starting value to zero. *Modelled as* announces that the story is about to be simplified and tells you how. [[Choosing Effective Equations]] has the fuller table of cue phrases and what each one licenses.

## 5. Where the instinct misleads

The same machinery that makes stories memorable makes two mistakes easy. An honest account has to include them.

### A good story beside the idea pushes the idea out

In 1998 Shannon Harp and Richard Mayer gave students a short illustrated lesson on how lightning forms. Some versions also contained interesting extras on the same topic: a football player struck by lightning, the glassy tubes called fulgurites that lightning leaves in sand. Across four experiments with 357 students, those who got the extras recalled **fewer** of the main ideas and did **worse** on problems that required applying the explanation. The extras were true, relevant to lightning and enjoyable. They were not part of the causal chain from rising air to the stroke, and they took its place. The authors called them *seductive details*.

![[story-animals-seductive-detail-comic.png|720]]
*The anecdote was a success. The cloud diagram behind it was the lesson.*

The rule that follows is strict. **The story has to be the causal structure of the idea, not a decoration next to it.** The $\sqrt2$ story above passes: every event in it is a step of the proof. A story about a Pythagorean being drowned for revealing the secret does not, however memorable, because you can know it perfectly and still not know why $\sqrt2$ is irrational. There is room for both kinds. Only one of them is doing the teaching.

### A story that hangs together feels true

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described a woman, Linda: thirty-one, outspoken, a philosophy graduate who had been active against discrimination. They asked which was more probable: that Linda is a bank teller, or that Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement. A large majority chose the second. It cannot be right. Everyone in the second group is also in the first, so adding a detail can only make a statement less probable or leave it the same. The detail made a better story, and a better story *felt* more likely.

This is the Heider–Simmel film again, running in a place where it costs something. We supply wants and causes where there may be none. In science the same habit produces explanations such as "the electron wants to reach a lower energy" or "the gas tries to fill the container". They are useful as a first handle and false as mechanisms: nothing in the gas is trying. The safeguard is the one [[Why Probability and Statistics]] develops at length. A causal story is a **hypothesis**. It earns belief by surviving a test that could have broken it, not by being satisfying to hear. [[Learning as Verification]] is about building that check into how you study.

## 6. Carry this into the next thing you learn

- When a result will not stay in your head, stop rereading it and ask the four questions. Find who wanted what.
- Put "because" between the lines of your notes. The places where it will not go are your revision list.
- Read a story question twice: once as something that could happen, once as a list of modelling instructions.
- When a teacher, a video or a book gives you a wonderful anecdote, enjoy it, and then ask whether it was the mechanism or something standing beside the mechanism.
- When an explanation feels very satisfying, ask what would have shown it to be wrong.

## Misconceptions

- **"Stories are for younger children and for the humanities."** The effect in the list experiment was a factor of seven in university students. A proof and a derivation are causal chains already; telling them as chains is not a simplification.
- **"Any story helps."** A story beside the idea hurts learning of the idea, measurably. Only the story that *is* the idea's causal structure helps.
- **"If I remember it, I understand it."** Remembering the anecdote is not knowing the mechanism. The test is whether you can regenerate the steps with a reason for each.
- **"If it makes a convincing story, it is probably true."** Added detail makes a story more convincing and a claim less probable. Convincingness measures the listener.
- **"People learn best in their own style, and I am a story learner."** The research on matching teaching to a preferred "learning style" has not found that it improves results. The advantage of narrative is not a style. It shows up across people in general.
