# Learning with the Whole Body 用整个身体学习

> Hold a heavy book against your chest for twenty seconds. Easy. Now hold the same book at the end of a straight arm for twenty seconds.
>
> The book did not get heavier. Your shoulder has just measured a **moment**: the same force, four times further from the pivot, is four times harder to hold. You will not need to memorise that again.

## Try it before you read on

Do the book first. A 1 kg book weighs about 9.8 N. Against your chest it sits perhaps 0.15 m from the shoulder joint, and the turning effect is $9.8 \times 0.15 \approx 1.5$ N m. At arm's length, 0.60 m, it is $9.8 \times 0.60 \approx 5.9$ N m. [[Torque]] gives you that equation in one line. Your shoulder gave you what the equation is *about*.

Then a second test. If you read Chinese, write 打喷嚏 by hand, now, without looking it up. You have read those three characters thousands of times. Most people stall on the third.

Both tests point at the same claim, and everything below examines it: **what you have done with your body is understood and remembered differently from what you have only looked at.** Three questions follow. Is it true? Why, and in particular is it because more of the brain is switched on? And when is it false?

## 中文锚点

“打喷嚏”这三个字，你一年要读上百遍，从来不用停顿。现在拿起笔，把第三个字写出来。大多数人写到一半就卡住了：一眼就能认出来的字，手却写不出来。这不是一般意义上的“忘了”，眼睛通向这个字的那条路完好如初；荒掉的是另一条路，手的那条，因为这些年手只是敲拼音、从候选里挑字，从来没有把那些笔画走过一遍。一段记忆并不是存在某一个地方的一样东西：通向它的路可以有好几条，眼睛一条，耳朵一条，手一条，对力的感觉又是一条，而每一条路都得常有人走才不会荒。所以亲手做过的事，比只用眼睛看过的事记得牢：回去的路多了，一条断了，另一条还通。

## 1. Three findings

### The bicycle wheels (2015)

Angular momentum is the topic in first-year mechanics that students most reliably fail to believe. A spinning wheel resists being tilted, and pushes back in a direction nobody expects.

Carly Kontra, Sian Beilock and colleagues at Chicago took a pair of bicycle wheels on one axle, spun them, and asked students to tilt the axle from horizontal to vertical while keeping a laser pointer fixed on a line on the wall. Half the students held the axle. The other half stood beside them and watched. Both groups saw the same wheels and took the same quiz.

The students who had held the axle scored higher. The researchers then ran it as a randomised experiment inside a real college physics course: the students assigned to handle the apparatus earned quiz grades about 7 % higher than those assigned to watch, though the two groups did equally well on every other quiz that term. And when students answered angular-momentum questions inside a brain scanner, those who had held the wheels showed more activity in the sensory and motor regions that deal with forces and movement, and that activity accounted for their better scores. They were lying still, reading text. Part of their answer was coming from their hands.

### The hand that writes (2005–2021)

- **Children.** Marieke Longcamp's group taught 3-to-5-year-olds new letters for three weeks, either by copying them by hand or by typing them. The older children who had written them recognised them better afterwards, and the advantage was still there when they were tested again later.
- **Inside the brain.** Karin James and Laura Engelhardt had five-year-olds who could not yet read print, type or trace letters, and then scanned them while they simply *looked* at letters. The brain's reading circuit responded only to the letters the child had written freehand. Tracing was not enough, and neither was typing.
- **Adults.** Robert Wiley and Brenda Rapp taught 42 adults the Arabic alphabet by handwriting, by typing or by watching videos. All three groups learned the letters. The handwriting group learned them fastest and was the most accurate at naming and identifying them afterwards.
- **Chinese.** Tan, Spinks, Eden, Perfetti and Siok found that how well Chinese children could *copy* characters predicted how well they could *read*, more strongly than the sound-based skills that predict reading in English. Their paper is titled "Reading depends on writing, in Chinese". The adult end of the same story is 提笔忘字: in a 2010 *China Youth Daily* survey, 83 % of 2,072 respondents said they had trouble writing characters by hand. They can all still read them.

### The hands that explain (2008)

Susan Wagner Cook and Susan Goldin-Meadow taught primary-school children to solve problems like $4 + 3 + 6 = \_ + 6$. Some children were taught to *say* "I want to make one side equal to the other side". Others were taught to say it while sweeping a hand under the left side and then under the right. Four weeks later, the children who had gestured had kept what they learned. The children who had only spoken were much less likely to have kept it.

An older result fits with all of these. Give people a list of short commands such as *break the match* or *lift the pen*. Those who carry the actions out remember far more of the list than those who only read it. Psychologists call it the **enactment effect**, and it has been reproduced for forty years.

## 2. Is it because more neurons are active?

This is the natural explanation: moving, feeling and seeing at once must light up more of the brain than reading does, and more brain ought to mean more learning. **It is the wrong explanation, and the evidence against it is a video game.**

In 1992 Richard Haier scanned eight young men playing *Tetris* for the first time, measuring how much glucose their brains burned. Then they practised daily for four to eight weeks and were scanned again. Their scores had risen more than sevenfold. Their brains were using **less** energy than before, and the players who had improved the most showed the largest drops. Learning had not recruited more of the brain. It had stopped recruiting the parts that were not needed. Skilled performance often looks like this: as a task is mastered, the brain does it with less.

So "more activation" cannot be the mechanism, or beginners would be the best learners in the room. Two better-supported ideas replace it.

**Which systems, not how many.** Beilock's group scanned ice-hockey players, hockey fans and people who had never watched a game while all of them listened to sentences about hockey. The players and fans understood the sentences better, and the difference ran through the premotor cortex, a region that normally plans actions. Nobody was moving. The experienced listeners were using their action system *to understand language*. The students with the bicycle wheels did the same thing: they understood "torque" partly with the machinery that had once felt one. A concept connected to the systems that handle force and movement can be run, like a simulation. A concept connected only to words can be recited.

**How many routes lead back.** This is the 中文锚点 above. A memory that can be reached through the eye, the hand and the feel of a force has three ways in. Typing pinyin exercises one of them. The others close, and one day the character will not come.

Neither idea is about quantity. Both are about **connection**: what the new idea is wired to.

## 3. When the body does not help

The finding is narrower than "hands-on is better", and the limits are where most of the practical value lies.

- **The movement has to *be* the idea.** The wheels worked because the force in the student's wrists *was* angular momentum. Nicole McNeil and colleagues gave 9-to-12-year-olds realistic toy banknotes and coins to help with word problems about money. The children with the realistic money got **fewer** answers right than children given nothing, and children given plain, boring tokens did better than those with the realistic notes. Vivid objects pulled attention to themselves. (An honest edge: the mistakes those children made were less often conceptual ones, so the realistic money did call up what they knew about shopping. It got in the way of turning that knowledge into arithmetic.) This is the same trap as the seductive detail in [[Humans Are Story Animals]]: a rich experience beside the idea competes with the idea.
- **Handwriting is not magic.** A well-known 2014 study found that students who took lecture notes by hand beat students who typed. In 2021 Heather Urry and colleagues repeated it exactly with 142 students and found no reliable difference, and their pooled analysis of eight similar studies did not support an advantage either. The evidence for handwriting is strong where the *shape is the thing being learned*: letters, characters, diagrams, the layout of a derivation. It does not show that ink improves understanding of a lecture.
- **There are no "kinaesthetic learners".** The claim that each person has a style, visual or auditory or kinaesthetic, and learns best when taught in it, has been tested many times. Matching teaching to a student's stated style does not improve results. The effects in section 1 held for ordinary, unselected students. Doing helps *everyone* when the doing carries the idea, and helps *no one* when it does not.
- **Doing is not enough without the link.** A student can spin the wheel, enjoy it and never connect the push in the wrists to $\tau = \dfrac{dL}{dt}$. The experiments all paired the experience with the explanation. The body supplies the meaning, and the symbols still have to be attached to it.

## 4. A climbing wall is a mechanics laboratory

Three ideas from mechanics that climbers know in their forearms before they ever see them in a textbook. The numbers are for a 60 kg climber.

![[whole-body-climber-moments.svg|820]]

**Moment: "keep your hips in."** Stand on two footholds on a vertical wall with your hands on holds 1.4 m above them. Your weight, 588 N, acts at your centre of mass. Take moments about your feet: the weight tries to rotate you outwards, away from the wall, and the only thing stopping it is your fingers pulling in. With your hips hanging 0.40 m out, the fingers must supply $588 \times 0.40 / 1.4 = 168$ N. Turn one hip in to the wall so the centre of mass is 0.15 m out, and the figure falls to $63$ N. Nobody who has felt that difference at the top of a long route needs to be told that a moment is force times *perpendicular distance*. ([[Torque]], [[Centre of Mass]].)

**Momentum: the deadpoint.** To reach a hold 0.30 m beyond full stretch you must jump, leaving at $v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 0.30} \approx 2.4$ m/s. There is one instant, 0.25 s later, at the top of the rise, when your velocity is zero. Catch the hold then and your fingers only have to hold your weight. Catch it a tenth of a second late and you are already falling at about 1 m/s, with a momentum of 59 kg m/s that your fingers must destroy. If they do it in 0.05 s, that costs an average of about 1200 N on top of your weight: two extra climbers hanging from your fingertips. Climbers call the zero-velocity instant the **deadpoint**. A physicist calls it the moment when $p = 0$ and no impulse is required. ([[Linear Momentum]].)

**Moment of inertia: the swing.** When your feet cut loose on an overhang, you swing from your hands like a pendulum. Treated crudely as a uniform rod 2.0 m long, a straight body swings with a period of about 2.3 s. Tuck your knees to your chest and the rod is nearer 1.3 m: the mass is closer to the pivot, the moment of inertia is smaller, the period drops to about 1.9 s, and your feet come back to the wall sooner. The same tuck is how a diver spins faster. ([[Moment of Inertia]], [[Angular Momentum]].)

You do not need a climbing wall. You need *some* place where the quantity you are studying pushes on you. A swivel chair and two heavy books will show you conservation of angular momentum. A door pushed near its hinge and then near its handle is a moment. A lift starting and stopping, with your phone's accelerometer recording, is Newton's second law.

## 5. What this means at a desk, and in an exam room

An exam gives you a chair, a pen and silence. None of the apparatus comes with you. What comes with you is what the students in the scanner had: the ability to **run the experience again in your head**, which you only have if there was an experience.

So the work is done before the exam, and it is cheap.

- **Produce, do not re-read.** Working a solution by hand on blank paper is an action. Reading a worked solution is watching someone else hold the wheels. [[The Feynman Technique]] makes the same point from the side of explanation.
- **Draw the diagram yourself**, every time, even when one is printed. Drawing a force diagram forces a decision about where each arrow starts and which way it points, and the hand remembers the decision.
- **Be the machine.** Trace an algorithm on paper with a finger on the current line and a table of variables filled in by hand. In [[Recursion]], physically stack a card for each call and remove it on each return. You are enacting the computer, and enacted steps are the ones that stay.
- **Use your hands in the exam room.** Nobody will stop you holding out three fingers for the force on a current in [[Lorentz Force]], tilting your hand to see which way a plane's normal points in [[Planes in 3D]], or turning your pen like a screw. This is the intended use of those rules.
- **When a quantity feels like nothing, go and find where it pushes on you.** If "moment of inertia" is only a formula, spin on a chair. A brief turn with the apparatus was all the bicycle-wheel students had.
- **Keep the experience plain and aimed.** One quantity, felt clearly, then written down with its equation straight away. An elaborate activity with the idea buried somewhere inside it is the toy banknotes again.

## Misconceptions

1. **"It works because more of the brain is active."** Practice makes brains quieter. What matters is which systems an idea is connected to, and how many routes lead back to it.
2. **"I am a kinaesthetic learner."** Tests of learning styles keep failing. The benefit of doing is available to everyone, and only when the doing carries the idea.
3. **"Any hands-on activity beats a lecture."** The toy banknotes made children worse. The movement has to be the concept.
4. **"Handwritten notes are better than typed notes."** The exact replication found no difference. Handwriting helps where the shape is the content.
5. **"If I can recognise it, I know it."** 提笔忘字. Recognition and production are different routes, and only the ones you use stay open.
6. **"Physical intuition is for physics."** Gesture improved *arithmetic*. Tracing by hand is how programs are understood.
