# Fun Is the Brachistochrone 乐趣是最速降线

> *乐趣是最速降线，而乐趣来自于狩猎感。最速降线是弯路。*
> *Fun is the brachistochrone — and fun comes from the feeling of hunting. The brachistochrone is, to every appearance, a detour.*

## What this card is for

Every card in this vault is built under one operating rule: *rigor is table stakes; fun is the bar.* Stories are the third leg of the whole structure, not a garnish; worked examples are allowed to be a game mechanic; a comic is required in every historical card. Those are decisions, and until now the reason for them lived only in the builders' heads. This card states the reason as a thesis with a mechanism, so that it can be argued with — and so that a student can use it on themselves.

The thesis has two halves, and each is a claim about *speed*, not about pleasure:

1. **Fun is the fastest path to the goal, and it does not look like it.** The straight line from where you are to what you must learn is *not* the quickest route. The quickest route dips below it first. That is a theorem about beads on wires, and this card argues it is also true of minds.
2. **Fun has a source, and the source is the feeling of hunting** — which comes from having chosen your target, or at least believing you did. Assigned targets do not produce it, however worthy. The best game designers, the best teachers and the best labs all know this, and all of them *hide the hints* so that the choice feels like yours.

### 中文锚点

你站在《旷野之息》(Breath of the Wild) 的一座高塔上，远处有一座神庙在发橙光。**没有人叫你去**——你就去了。路上一块石头摆得不太对劲，你把它翻开，一个呀哈哈跳出来。三个小时后，你学会了这张地图的地形、天气、敌人的巡逻路线、怎么用磁铁开桥。**没有人布置过作业，你却把一门课学完了。** 反过来，几乎每一部 JRPG 都有这样的时刻：一个 NPC 拦住你，说"帮我通一下马桶"（找十条老鼠尾巴、送一封信）——同样是"去某个地方做某件事"，你却只想按跳过键。区别不在任务，在于**目标是谁选的**。同一座游乐园，敞开心扉到处逛的人带着一肚子故事回家；拿着小红书攻略一条条打勾的人，勾完了，却没有玩到。这张卡讲两件事：第一，**乐趣是最速降线**——从起点到终点，最快的路不是直线，而是先向下俯冲、看起来绕远的那条曲线（1696 年伯努利的题，牛顿一夜解出，答案是摆线）；把学习画在同一张图上，"直线"是硬啃大纲，"俯冲"是先玩起来——玩看起来是绕路，而且它**确实是弯路**——最速降线本来就是弯路；玩是**先把速度攒起来**。第二，**乐趣来自狩猎感**，而狩猎感来自**自愿选定的目标**——猎人是不停追溯因果的人，但没有人追溯自己被指派的因果。好的游戏把提示藏起来（神庙的光、呀哈哈的石头），让你以为是自己发现的；好的老师把谜题先于概念摆出来；现实世界里帮助别人之所以像狩猎，正因为它是自愿的。**把"通马桶"变成狩猎的办法只有一个：在任务里面，找到一个你自己想追的问题。**

## Part I — The brachistochrone: why the fastest path dips

### The theorem

In June 1696 Johann Bernoulli published a challenge to "the sharpest mathematicians in the whole world": a bead slides without friction along a wire from a point $A$ to a lower point $B$, not directly below it, under gravity alone. *What shape of wire gets it there in the least time?* The story of the challenge — Newton solving it overnight and answering anonymously, Bernoulli recognising "the lion by its claw" — is told in [[The Bernoulli Family]]. What matters here is the answer, because the answer is surprising in a specific way.

The shortest *path* is the straight line. The shortest *time* is not. Every solution — Newton's, Leibniz's, both Bernoullis', L'Hôpital's — converged on the **cycloid**, the curve a point on the rim of a rolling wheel traces, hung upside down. The cycloid **dips below the straight line**: it starts steeper, plunges early, then flattens out to meet $B$ from below. Say it plainly, because it is the point: **the fastest path is a detour.** Not "looks like" one — it is longer than the line, it goes somewhere the line never goes, and it wins anyway.

![[fun-is-the-brachistochrone.svg|760]]

The figure is not a sketch; it is three slides timed by simulation from the same start to the same goal. The straight line takes just over a second. The cycloid takes about a fifth less. And note the third curve — a crude "drop steeply, then coast" — it *also* beats the straight line, by nearly as much. That third curve is going to matter.

### Why the detour wins

A bead has no speed at $A$. The straight line spends its early length crawling: shallow slope, small acceleration, the bead barely moving through the part of the journey where it is slowest. The cycloid spends its early length *falling*: it converts height into speed as early as possible, and then covers the remaining distance — longer than the line's — at a speed the line never reaches. **The detour is not a cost paid for later; it is the investment that makes every later metre cheaper.** The extra path is more than repaid by the extra velocity.

That is the whole metaphor, and it should be checked rather than admired. Map it:

| The bead | The learner |
|---|---|
| $A$: rest | Where you start — no momentum, no interest yet |
| $B$: the goal | The exam, the skill, the thing you must be able to do |
| The straight line | The syllabus taken head-on: topic 1, topic 2, drill, drill |
| Height converted to speed | Attention, curiosity, the pull of a target you want — *velocity of mind* |
| The cycloid's early plunge | Play, exploration, the story first, the mystery before the concept |
| The line's slow start | The first weeks of any course taught as a list |

The straight-line learner is not lazy; they are *slow at the start* for the same reason the bead is: no speed has been built, and nothing on a shallow incline builds it. The learner who spent the first hour on the story of why $e$ exists, or on the game where the vector triangle is the mechanic, looks like they took a detour. They did. They also arrive first, because they arrive *moving*.

> [!tip] The third curve is the honest one
> Look again at "steep drop, then coast." It is not the optimum — but it beats the straight line by almost as much as the cycloid does. The lesson is not that there is one perfect curve of fun you must find. It is that **almost any early plunge beats the line.** A clumsy story, an imperfect game, a mystery only half-connected to the syllabus — these still build speed. Perfectionism about the detour is how people end up on the straight line.

### Where the metaphor stops

Three honest limits, so the card is a claim and not a slogan.

- **Not every detour is a cycloid.** A path that dips *too* far — that spends its whole length on play and never turns toward $B$ — loses. The cycloid *bends back up*. Fun that never reconnects to the goal is a different curve, and a slower one.
- **The bead is frictionless; you are not.** [[Inertia and Bootstrapping]] is the card on what it costs to *start* — the static friction that every real mind pays before the first metre. The brachistochrone says nothing about that cost; it only says that once moving, the plunging path wins. The two cards are companions: bootstrapping gets you off $A$; the brachistochrone tells you which way to fall.
- **The line is sometimes the only path you are allowed.** An exam three days away has no room for a plunge. Then the straight line is correct — as triage, not as pedagogy. The mistake is teaching a *year* as if it were three days.

> [!info] The tautochrone, for the curious
> The same curve has a second property, discovered by Huygens thirty years earlier: a bead released from *anywhere* on an inverted cycloid reaches the bottom in the *same* time. Start high, start low — you arrive together. [[The Pendulum Story]] tells why Huygens wanted that. For this card it is a grace note: on the right curve, where a learner starts matters less than most people fear.

## Part II — Where fun comes from: the feeling of hunting

### The hunter, and the one thing hunting requires

[[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]] defines the vault's central figure: *a hunter is someone who can constantly trace causality.* That card is about the *act* — following the thread. This card is about the *feeling* that makes a person want to follow it, and the feeling has a precondition the definition leaves implicit:

> **Nobody hunts a target they were handed.** The feeling of hunting arises only when the target was chosen — or when the hunter believes it was.

Test it on the same task. Kill ten wolves. In one game an NPC by the town gate assigns it — *bring me ten pelts* — and every player alive recognises the feeling: the fetch quest, the errand, 通马桶. In another game the wolves are simply *between you and the shrine you spotted from the tower*, and you kill them without noticing you have done a chore, because the wolves were an obstacle on a hunt you chose. Same ten wolves. Same buttons. The difference is entirely in who picked the target.

### The theme-park test

You do not need a game to see it. Watch two visitors enter the same theme park on the same afternoon.

The first walks in with no plan and an open door where a plan would be. They wander off the main path and, because they are looking, things keep happening to them: a white rabbit in a waistcoat hurrying past with a pocket watch, a warrior with a glowing sword, a Victorian gentleman at a café table with a cup of black tea — and they stop, and talk, and follow whichever one is most interesting. When a child ahead of them drops an ice cream, they crouch and help; nobody asked. They leave at closing time tired, broke, and full of stories nobody else will have, because nobody else walked *their* line.

The second walks in with a phone. On it is a list — the ten *must-dos* and *must-haves* someone on 小红书 or Facebook compiled — and they move through the park ticking it off, one line to the next, in the order the list gives. They pass the rabbit without seeing him. They stand in the long queue the list says to stand in. They leave with every box ticked, and they were not happy, and if you ask them what they saw they will read you the list.

![[fun-is-the-brachistochrone-theme-park.png|720]]

Look at the two routes drawn on the ground. The second visitor's is a straight line from gate to exit — the shortest path, and the slowest way to actually *have* the park. The first visitor's is a loop that dips into every corner: a brachistochrone, to every appearance a detour. Same park, same afternoon, same entry ticket. The difference is that one of them **chose every target** and the other **was handed a list by strangers** — and the list was written by people who had, themselves, once been the first kind of visitor. Opening your heart to the whole park is not a personality trait; it is the precondition for the hunting feeling, and the checklist is the toilet.

The research literature has names for this, and they are worth knowing because they are not folklore:

- **Self-determination theory** (Deci and Ryan, developed from the 1970s; the standard statement is their 2000 *American Psychologist* paper) identifies three needs behind intrinsic motivation — **autonomy**, competence, relatedness — and finds autonomy the lever that external structures most easily break. A target you chose feeds it; a target assigned to you starves it.
- **The overjustification effect** (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett, 1973): children who loved drawing were told they would get a certificate for drawing. Afterwards, they drew *less*, and less well, than children who had drawn for nothing. The reward had converted a chosen activity into an assigned one. **This is the mechanism by which points, badges and prizes kill the very hunting they are meant to encourage** — and why "gamification" so often produces the opposite of a game.
- **Flow** (Csikszentmihalyi): the absorbed state that appears when challenge and skill are matched — the competence leg. Necessary, but not the source; a fetch quest can be perfectly matched to your skill and still feel like a toilet.

### How good designers hide the hints

Here is the part that turns the observation into a craft. The player *believes* they chose the shrine. In fact the shrine was placed. Nintendo's designers explained this openly in their 2017 Game Developers Conference talk on *Breath of the Wild*: shrines glow orange so they are visible from any tower; the terrain is built on what they called the **triangle rule** — a landmark is put behind every ridge, so that reaching one viewpoint always reveals the next thing worth walking to; and nine hundred Korok seeds sit under rocks that are *slightly* wrong — a lone stone on a hilltop, a ring of lilies with one missing — with no marker, no quest log, no NPC. The player who lifts the odd rock feels like a hunter. The rock was put there for exactly that reason.

That is the whole art: **make the assigned feel chosen by hiding the assignment inside something the player will notice on their own.** It is not manipulation in the cheap sense, because the choice is real — the player genuinely could walk past the rock. It is design in the deep sense: the world is arranged so that curiosity, left alone, walks the intended path.

A classroom can be built the same way, and the good ones are:

- **The mystery before the concept.** A lesson that opens with a real aviation directive — *this aircraft must be powered down every 248 days* — and lets the class hunt for why, arrives at 32-bit signed overflow as *the answer to a question they asked*, not as item 4.1 on a list. The syllabus content is the same. The order is reversed, and the order is everything.
- **Puzzles left lying around.** A cipher on the board with no instructions. A "rabbit hole" marker on a slide that says, in effect, *this is off the exam, genuinely fun, and entirely up to you* — with the class holding the veto. Korok seeds.
- **Real choices, both branches built.** *Do we do this in Java or Python? I prepared both* — and the class votes. The choice is real (either answer was available) and the lesson is the same lesson either way. Autonomy fed; content unchanged.
- **This vault's own seeds.** A dangling `[[link]]` is a rock that looks wrong: the material expects something that is not there yet. A *Beyond syllabus* section is a shrine glowing off the path, marked optional. A Story card is the thing you were not asked to read. None of them is on any exam. All of them are why a student keeps walking.

### The real world: why helping is a hunt and chores are not

The clearest case is outside games and classrooms. Helping another person is, almost by definition, voluntary — nobody can assign you to care — and that is exactly why it produces the hunting feeling rather than the chore feeling, even when the work itself is unglamorous. The doctor tracing a symptom to its cause ([[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]]'s Leorio) is doing a hunt *because they chose the patient's problem as their target*. The same doctor filling in a form the hospital requires is unclogging a toilet.

The vault's own histories are full of this. Unix was built because one programmer wanted to play a game for free and *chose* to make a cast-off machine run it ([[Space Travel]]) — nobody assigned the file system; it was the next rock that looked wrong. The engineers at Xerox PARC were the most gifted hunters of their decade and their own company never asked them for anything ([[A Rich Neighbor Named Xerox]]) — invention with no one to deliver it to. And this vault exists because its builders kept noticing what was missing and going after it; no syllabus assigned a single card. Exams only ever decided the *order*.

## Part III — The trap, and the one move out of it

### 通马桶 in learning

A syllabus is a quest log. An exam is an NPC at the town gate. Read that way, the entire structure of formal education is a fetch quest, and the wonder is not that students disengage but that any of them hunt at all. The straight line of Part I and the assigned target of Part II are the *same* line: the syllabus taken head-on is slow *because* it was handed to you, and it was handed to you *as* a straight line.

Two failure modes follow, and both are common:

- **Drill-then-coast.** Grind the technique first, hope interest arrives later. This is the third curve — it beats the pure line, but only because *any* early intensity beats none; it never finds the cycloid, and the interest usually doesn't arrive.
- **Rewards on top of the quest log.** Points, stickers, leaderboards, a prize for the first to finish. The overjustification effect guarantees that whatever hunting instinct the student brought will be converted into errand-running by the reward itself.

### The move

There is exactly one way to turn an assigned target into a chosen one, and every hunter in the vault's stories does it: **find a target inside the assignment that you actually want.** Not *instead of* the assignment — inside it.

- The syllabus says *elastic potential energy, $\lambda x^2/2L$*. The hunter asks: *why is there a half? Why does the same half appear in $\tfrac12 kx^2$ and $\tfrac12 mv^2$ and the area of a triangle?* That question was not assigned. Answering it covers the syllabus row on the way through, at speed.
- The mark scheme says *show that*. The hunter asks: *what would break if the claim were false?* — and now the proof is a hunt for the load-bearing step.
- The exam says *three past papers*. The hunter picks the one question they got wrong last time and asks what it was *really* testing — the skill, not the topic — and the next paper is a check on a hypothesis they made.

This is not a trick to make homework palatable. It is the method of [[Forward Reading and Problem Discovery]] pointed at the *choice of target* rather than the trace: the hunter chooses which thread to pull, and the choosing is what makes the pulling feel like anything.

> [!warning] Two honest limits
> **Duty is real.** Sometimes the toilet is in your own house and nobody is coming; you unclog it because it needs doing, and no reframing is required or honest. Fun is *the* fastest curve, not the *only* road, and a person who can only act when it feels like a hunt has a different problem. **And the illusion has an ethics.** Hiding hints so that curiosity walks the intended path is design; hiding the fact that there *is* a path — pretending the shrine was not placed — shades into manipulation. The difference is whether the player, told the truth, would still have chosen. Nintendo published its GDC talk. Good teachers tell students why the mystery came first.

## How to use this card

1. **Before starting anything you were assigned**, spend five minutes finding the target inside it that you would chase unassigned. If there is none, look harder; there is almost always a *why* that nobody has answered for you. That five minutes is the plunge.
2. **Never put a reward on a thing you already enjoy.** Not for yourself, not for anyone you teach. The certificate kills the drawing.
3. **Plant your own rocks.** Leave a question unanswered at the end of a session on purpose. Keep the *Beyond syllabus* sections for when you are tired of the path. Read the Story before the card when you can — that is the cycloid's early drop, and it is the reason the vault has Stories at all.
4. **When you notice the fetch-quest feeling**, treat it as diagnostic, not moral: it means the target is not yours yet. Change the target, not your character.
