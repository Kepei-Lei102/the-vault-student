# Cartesian Coordinates 笛卡尔坐标

## Definition

A **Cartesian coordinate system** (笛卡尔坐标系) uses two perpendicular number lines — the **axes** — to specify any point in a plane by a pair of numbers. The horizontal axis is the $x$-axis, the vertical axis is the $y$-axis, and they meet at the **origin** $(0, 0)$.

A point is written as an **ordered pair** $(x, y)$, where $x$ is the horizontal distance from the origin and $y$ is the vertical distance.

### 中文锚点

笛卡尔坐标 = 用两条互相垂直的数轴（$x$ 轴和 $y$ 轴）来表示平面上任何一点的位置。点用有序对 $(x, y)$ 表示：先写 $x$，再写 $y$。

## The Four Quadrants

The two axes divide the plane into four regions, numbered **anticlockwise** starting from the top right:

| Quadrant | 中文 | Signs of $(x, y)$ | Example |
|----------|------|-------------------|---------|
| I (first) | 第一象限 | $(+, +)$ | $(3, 5)$ |
| II (second) | 第二象限 | $(-, +)$ | $(-4, 2)$ |
| III (third) | 第三象限 | $(-, -)$ | $(-1, -6)$ |
| IV (fourth) | 第四象限 | $(+, -)$ | $(7, -3)$ |

> [!warning] Order matters — $(x, y)$ not $(y, x)$
> The ordered pair is always **$x$ first, then $y$**. $(3, 5)$ is 3 right and 5 up; $(5, 3)$ is 5 right and 3 up. These are different points. The classic memory device: "$x$ before $y$ alphabetically, $x$ before $y$ on the page."

## A Fly on the Ceiling — The Story Behind the Name

"Cartesian" comes from **Cartesius**, the Latin form of the name **René Descartes** (1596–1650), the French philosopher and mathematician who introduced this system in his 1637 book *La Géométrie*. (This is the same book where he introduced the $a^n$ notation for powers — see the history note in [[Laws of Indices]].)

Legend has it that Descartes invented the coordinate system while lying sick in bed, watching a fly crawl on the ceiling. He realised he could describe the fly's position at any moment using just **two numbers**: its distance from two perpendicular walls. That moment — whether literally true or not — captures the revolutionary idea: **geometry and algebra are the same thing, viewed from different angles.** A point becomes a pair of numbers; a curve becomes an equation; a circle becomes $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. Entire fields of mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, modern physics — are built on this bridge.

## Beyond the Syllabus

At A-Level and university, Cartesian coordinates generalise in two directions:
- **Higher dimensions** — $(x, y, z)$ in 3D, then $(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ in $n$-dimensional space. This powers linear algebra, computer graphics, and machine learning (each data point is a vector in a high-dimensional space).
- **Other coordinate systems** — **polar coordinates** $(r, \theta)$ describe points by distance and angle; **spherical** and **cylindrical** coordinates are used in 3D physics. Each system is best for different symmetries: Cartesian for rectangles, polar for circles.
