# Additional Financial Literacy — United Kingdom 英国金融常识

## What this card is

A **life card** supplementing [[Financial Literacy (Life)]]. For the student arriving in the United Kingdom. Compared to the US, the UK system is simpler in three directions: the **NHS** mostly removes the healthcare-insurance puzzle, there's one national tax authority (**HMRC**), and payments run on real-time rails (**Faster Payments**). It's *more* complex in two directions: the **ISA** (tax-free savings wrapper) has no clean US analogue, and the **UK student loan** works so differently from the US version that Chinese students routinely mis-classify it as debt.

### 中文锚点

与美国相比，英国的优势在于：**NHS (国民医疗服务)** 基本覆盖医疗，税制较简单 (HMRC 一家管), 即时转账 (Faster Payments) 普及。复杂的地方：**ISA (免税储蓄账户)** 没有中国对应物; **英国学生贷款**虽名为"贷款"，实为 *按收入征收的毕业税*，千万不要按美式债务思维理解。

> [!info] Numbers in this card are 2024–2026
> Tax thresholds, ISA allowances, and student-loan repayment thresholds change in the April budget each year. Values here are accurate as of early 2026; check gov.uk for current-year figures.

## 1. Banking

### Account types

| English | 中文 | What it is |
|---------|------|-----------|
| Current account | 活期账户 | = US checking; day-to-day spending, debit card |
| Savings account | 储蓄账户 | Interest-bearing; "easy access" or "fixed-rate bond" varieties |
| Cash ISA | 现金免税账户 | Savings inside the ISA wrapper — interest is tax-free (see §2) |
| Fixed-rate bond | 固定利率债券账户 | Misleadingly named — it's a term deposit, not a traded bond |
| Premium Bonds | 国债抽奖账户 | NS&I prize draw instead of interest; tax-free, capital safe, no guaranteed return |

### Key protections and identifiers

**FSCS protection** — Financial Services Compensation Scheme. Covers **up to £85,000 per person per banking group** (higher for joint accounts and certain temporary balances). Note: *per banking group*, not per brand — HSBC and First Direct share one FSCS allocation.

**Sort code + account number** — UK banks use a **6-digit sort code** (identifies the bank and branch) plus an **8-digit account number**. Both are shown on your debit card and needed for any transfer. Unlike the US routing number, the sort code is *always* visible.

### Transfers and payments

| Method | Speed | Cost | Use case |
|--------|-------|------|----------|
| Faster Payments | Seconds | Free | Standard bank transfer — UK's real-time backbone |
| BACS | 3 business days | Free | Batch system for payroll, pensions |
| CHAPS | Same day | £20–30 | Large transfers (house purchases) |
| Direct Debit | 1–3 days | Free | **Payee pulls** — for bills, utilities, subscriptions |
| Standing Order | Seconds to minutes | Free | **You push** on a schedule — for rent, regular transfers |
| Contactless | Instant | Free | £100 cap per tap since Oct 2021 |

> [!warning] Direct Debit vs Standing Order — the two that look the same
> Both are recurring automatic payments, but they differ in *who controls the amount*.
>
> - **Direct Debit**: the *payee* (utility company, subscription service) pulls a variable amount each period. You can cancel anytime, and the **Direct Debit Guarantee** protects you — unauthorized or incorrect amounts are refunded by your bank.
> - **Standing Order**: *you* instruct your bank to push a fixed amount on a schedule. You set and cancel it. Used for rent where the amount doesn't change until notified.
>
> Landlords sometimes ask for a Direct Debit (gives them control); Standing Order is safer for tenants.

### Fintech banks

**Monzo, Revolut, Starling, Chase UK, Wise** — app-first banks used widely by students, especially for international transfers, multi-currency accounts, and budgeting features. All are FCA-regulated and FSCS-covered (except Revolut for some products — check). These typically offer better FX rates and instant notifications than traditional high-street banks.

## 2. The ISA ecosystem — the UK's tax-free wrapper

An **Individual Savings Account (ISA)** is not an investment; it's a *tax-free wrapper* around savings or investments. Money inside an ISA grows free of income tax and capital gains tax. Each UK resident gets a **£20,000 annual ISA allowance** (2024–2026), split however they like across ISA types.

| ISA type | 中文 | What sits inside | Key detail |
|----------|------|-----------------|-----------|
| Cash ISA | 现金 ISA | Savings at bank interest | Tax-free interest |
| Stocks & Shares ISA | 股票 ISA | Funds, ETFs, shares | Tax-free dividends and capital gains |
| Innovative Finance ISA | P2P ISA | P2P lending | Tax-free interest, much higher risk |
| Lifetime ISA (LISA) | 终身 ISA | Cash or stocks/shares | **25% government bonus** on contributions; first home or age 60+ |
| Junior ISA (JISA) | 少年 ISA | Cash or stocks/shares | For under-18s; different £9,000 allowance |

### The Lifetime ISA — deserves its own callout

Open between age 18 and 39. Contribute up to **£4,000/year**. The government adds **25%** — £1,000/year maximum bonus. Withdraw only for a **first home purchase under £450,000**, or after age **60**. Any other withdrawal incurs a **25% penalty** on the whole balance (not just the bonus — so you lose principal). Extraordinarily generous for the narrow use case, extraordinarily punishing otherwise.

> [!tip] Why the ISA is unusual globally
> Most countries tax *all* interest, dividends, and capital gains. The UK gives every resident £20k of annual "growth space" that is simply outside that tax — no need to track basis, no wash-sale rules, no complicated forms. The US has nothing directly equivalent (Roth IRA is similar in spirit but with earned-income requirements and much lower limits).

## 3. Pensions

Three layers, like the US but with different mechanics.

| Layer | English | What it is |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| 1 | State Pension | Paid by government from age 67 (rising); based on **National Insurance** contribution years |
| 2 | Workplace pension | Employer-sponsored, now universal under **auto-enrolment** (since 2012) |
| 3 | SIPP | Self-Invested Personal Pension — individual, tax-advantaged, you pick investments |

### Auto-enrolment and workplace pensions

Since 2012, every UK employer must **auto-enrol** eligible employees into a workplace pension. Default contributions: **5% employee + 3% employer = 8% total** of qualifying earnings. You can opt out, but doing so forfeits the employer contribution (same "free money" logic as US 401(k) match).

### Tax relief on pension contributions

Pension contributions receive **income-tax relief**: if you're in the 20% basic-rate band, £80 of take-home becomes £100 in the pension (HMRC adds £20). Higher-rate (40%) and additional-rate (45%) earners claim additional relief via Self Assessment. The **annual allowance** in 2024–2025 is **£60,000** (or your total earnings, whichever is lower).

## 4. Taxes

### How tax is collected — PAYE

Most UK workers are taxed via **PAYE (Pay As You Earn)**: the employer withholds income tax and National Insurance every paycheck and pays HMRC directly. Most employees never file a tax return — HMRC reconciles automatically. Self-employed, high earners, and landlords file **Self Assessment** annually (deadline: 31 January).

### Identifiers

- **National Insurance number (NI number / NINo)** — two letters + 6 digits + one letter (e.g. `QQ123456C`). One per person for life. Required to work, pay NI, claim benefits, or access the State Pension.
- No equivalent of the US SSN for general ID purposes — the UK has no national ID card.

### Tax code — decoded

Your **tax code** tells your employer how much of your income is tax-free. Common codes in 2024:

| Code | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **1257L** | Standard — £12,570 personal allowance, normal tax; the typical code for most employees |
| **BR** | Basic Rate — all income taxed at 20% (often a second job) |
| **D0** / **D1** | All income taxed at higher (40%) / additional (45%) rate |
| **K123** | Negative allowance — more tax collected than standard (unusual) |
| **NT** | No tax (rare) |

The number × 10 = your personal allowance in £. `1257L` → £12,570 tax-free. If your code is wrong (HMRC estimated your second job wrong, say), you over- or under-pay for the year and reconcile after April.

### Income tax and National Insurance

| Band | Threshold (2024–25) | Income-tax rate | NI rate (employee) |
|------|--------------------:|-----------------|--------------------|
| Personal allowance | Up to £12,570 | 0% | 0% |
| Basic rate | £12,571 – £50,270 | 20% | 8% |
| Higher rate | £50,271 – £125,140 | 40% | 2% |
| Additional rate | £125,141 + | 45% | 2% |

The personal allowance is **tapered away** above £100,000 (lose £1 for every £2 over), creating an effective marginal rate of ~60% in the £100k–£125k band. A famous quirk of the system.

## 5. Student loan — Plan 2 / Plan 5, not US-style debt

The most important single thing to understand about UK university finance: **the "student loan" is legally a loan but behaves like a graduate tax**.

### How it works

- You receive up to ~£9,250/year tuition loan (paid to the university) + a means-tested maintenance loan (paid to you) to cover living costs.
- Interest accrues from day 1 (RPI-linked, with additional margin depending on income).
- Repayment is **income-contingent**: you pay **9% of gross income above a threshold**. Below threshold → **£0/month**.
- Anything unpaid after 25–40 years (depending on plan) is **written off**.

### Which plan you're on

| Plan | Who | Repayment threshold (2024–25) | Write-off |
|------|-----|---------------------------------:|-----------|
| Plan 1 | Pre-2012 England/Wales; Scotland; NI | ~£22,015 | 25 years |
| Plan 2 | England/Wales undergrad 2012–2022 | ~£27,295 | 30 years |
| Plan 4 | Scotland post-2021 | ~£27,660 | 25 years |
| Plan 5 | England/Wales undergrad from Aug 2023 | **~£25,000** | **40 years** |
| Postgrad | Master's / PhD loans | ~£21,000 | 30 years |

### Why it's not US-style debt

Under Plan 2, a graduate earning £30k pays (£30,000 − £27,295) × 9% ≈ **£244/year**. At that rate a £50,000 balance is **never repaid** — it's written off at year 30. Many graduates pay nothing for long stretches and never clear the balance. The effective economic interpretation is a **graduate tax of 9% above the threshold for 30 years**, not a debt to be retired as fast as possible.

> [!warning] Don't overpay the UK student loan as a reflex
> US-educated parents often tell students to "pay off the loan ASAP." This is usually *wrong* in the UK. Because the loan is written off, and because repayment is income-contingent, most graduates *never pay the full principal + interest*. Overpaying is equivalent to volunteering extra tax. Exception: if your expected lifetime income is very high (consulting, law, finance partners), you *will* pay it off anyway, and overpaying early saves interest.

## 6. Healthcare — NHS plus (optional) PMI

The **NHS** covers most healthcare free at the point of use for residents, including international students on a student visa (after paying the IHS — see International-Student Corner). Prescriptions in England have a flat charge (~£9.90 per item); prescriptions are **free in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland**. Hospital treatment and GP visits are free.

**Private Medical Insurance (PMI)** — optional top-up, usually offered as an employment benefit. Covers faster access to elective procedures, private rooms, wider specialist choice. Not a substitute for NHS; you use both.

No US-style deductibles, copays, or in-network/out-of-network distinctions. The word "insurance" is used very differently here.

## 7. Property and Mortgages

### Mortgage structure

UK mortgages are typically **fixed for 2, 3, 5, or occasionally 10 years**, then revert to the lender's **Standard Variable Rate (SVR)** — which is always higher than the current best fixed rates. Most homeowners **remortgage** (switch to a new fixed deal) before the SVR kicks in.

| Term | What it is |
|------|-----------|
| Fixed-rate | Rate locked for the fixed period (2/3/5/10 yr) |
| Tracker | Tracks Bank of England base rate + margin (e.g. "base + 0.75%") |
| SVR | Lender's variable default after fixed period ends |
| Interest-only | Pay interest monthly; principal due at end (common for buy-to-let) |
| LTV | Loan-to-Value ratio; lower LTV → better rates |

### Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT)

Tax on property purchases, paid by the buyer. Progressive bands from 0% up to 12%+. First-time buyer relief applies up to £425,000 (2024–25). Overseas buyers pay a 2% surcharge. A £500,000 purchase costs roughly £12,500–15,000 in SDLT depending on buyer status.

## 8. International-Student Corner

### Visa and healthcare

- **Student visa** replaced the old Tier 4 in 2020. Allows study and part-time work (usually up to 20 hours/week during term).
- **Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS)** — paid with the visa application, currently **£776/year** for students (2024 rate). This buys you full NHS access for the duration of your visa — much cheaper than any commercial equivalent.

### NI number and working

You cannot work in the UK without a **National Insurance number**. You can start work with a provisional record and apply for the NINo once in-country — the process takes 4–8 weeks. Your employer can pay you while the application is pending, but payroll is messy until the number is issued.

### Banking as a new arrival

UK banks accept **passport + BRP (Biometric Residence Permit) + proof of UK address**. The **proof of address** is often the bottleneck — a letter from your university admissions office usually works. Fintech banks (Monzo, Starling) are typically the fastest to open for new arrivals.

### Council Tax

Full-time students are **exempt from Council Tax** (the local government tax on residences). If you share a flat with non-students, be careful — the household may still owe Council Tax, but your share is typically excluded. Get a student certificate from your university to prove the exemption.
