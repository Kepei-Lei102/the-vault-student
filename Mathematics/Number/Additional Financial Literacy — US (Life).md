# Additional Financial Literacy — United States 美国金融常识

## What this card is

A **life card** supplementing [[Financial Literacy (Life)]]. Assumes you already know APR/APY, compounding, and fees. This is for the student arriving in the United States — what you meet in your first week (bank account), first month (health insurance, tax forms), and first year (credit score, retirement accounts you didn't know existed).

The US financial vocabulary is larger than China's or the UK's for three structural reasons: more of the system is *private* (especially healthcare), *state-level variation* adds a layer on top of federal rules, and *no single national ID* ties everything together the way 身份证 does in China — you juggle SSN, driver's license, bank account numbers, and a FICO score separately.

### 中文锚点

美国与国内差异最大的两点：**信用分数 (FICO)** 决定你能不能租房、办卡、有时决定能不能入职；**医疗保险术语** (deductible / copay / out-of-pocket max) 决定一次急诊账单是 $100 还是 $5000。所有条款都写在合同里，几乎没有人仔细读。

> [!info] Numbers in this card are 2024–2025
> Contribution limits, tax brackets, and deposit-insurance thresholds are inflation-adjusted annually. Values here are current through 2025. Check IRS.gov, FDIC.gov, and your plan documents for current-year figures before acting.

## 1. Banking

### Account types

| English | 中文 | What it is |
|---------|------|-----------|
| Checking account | 支票账户 / 活期账户 | Day-to-day spending, debit card attached, negligible interest |
| Savings account | 储蓄账户 | Companion to checking; slightly higher interest; sometimes transfer-limited |
| Money Market Account (MMA) | 货币市场账户 | Higher-yield savings with limited checking features; FDIC-insured |
| Certificate of Deposit (CD) | 定期存单 | = 定期存款; fixed term (3 mo – 5 yr), early withdrawal penalty |
| High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA) | 高息储蓄账户 | Online-bank savings; 3–5× brick-and-mortar rates |

### Key protections and identifiers

**FDIC insurance** — Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. Covers **up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category**. If the bank fails, you're made whole up to the limit. Credit unions have equivalent **NCUA** coverage.

**Routing number + account number** — every US bank account has two numbers. The 9-digit **routing number** identifies the bank (domestic equivalent of SWIFT); the **account number** identifies you within that bank. Both are printed on the bottom of a paper check. You need both to set up direct deposit, link a payment app, or receive a wire.

### Transfers and payments

| Method | Speed | Cost | Use case |
|--------|-------|------|----------|
| ACH | 1–3 business days | Free / low | Direct deposit, bill pay, Venmo cash-out |
| Wire transfer | Same day | $15–35 domestic, more international | Real estate closings, large one-offs |
| Zelle | Minutes | Free | Bank-to-bank P2P, built into most bank apps |
| Venmo / Cash App | Minutes | Free from bank, 3% from credit card | Social P2P, splitting bills |
| Apple Pay / Google Pay | Instant | Free | Contactless retail |

> [!warning] Overdraft and NSF fees — the single most common rookie bank mistake
> If a charge exceeds your balance, the bank may (a) **overdraft** the account, covering the charge but hitting you with ~$35, or (b) **decline** the charge and still charge an **NSF (non-sufficient funds)** fee of ~$35. Disable overdraft protection, or link it to your savings as a free fallback. Three overdrafts in a week can cost $100+ before you notice.

## 2. Credit — FICO and friends

Credit scores are the biggest single departure from the Chinese financial system. A **FICO score** (300–850) is computed from records at **three credit bureaus** — Experian, Equifax, TransUnion — and is visible to landlords, employers (sometimes), insurers, and every lender. No score = no apartment lease, no unsecured card, often no phone plan.

| English | 中文 | Note |
|---------|------|------|
| FICO score | FICO 信用分 | 300 (worst) to 850 (best); 670+ "good", 740+ "very good", 800+ "exceptional" |
| Credit report | 信用报告 | Itemized history at each bureau; **free annually** at annualcreditreport.com |
| Credit utilization | 信用使用率 | Current balance / total credit limit; **keep under 30%**, ideally under 10% |
| Credit history length | 信用历史长度 | Average age of open accounts; closing old cards hurts |
| Hard pull / hard inquiry | 硬查询 | Lender check during an application; small temporary drop |
| Soft pull | 软查询 | Pre-approval check, background; no score impact |
| Secured credit card | 抵押信用卡 | Backed by a cash deposit you pay; starter card for no-history applicants |
| Authorized user | 授权用户 | Added to someone else's card; inherits their history |

> [!warning] **"No credit" is worse than "bad credit"**
> An arriving student has *no* credit file. This is not neutral — most landlords will refuse to rent to a file that doesn't exist. Month 1–3 priority: open a checking account, apply for a **secured credit card** (Discover, Capital One, or a credit-union starter card), set one small recurring charge (a streaming service), autopay the full balance every month. After 6 months a FICO score appears; after 12 months you can qualify for a real unsecured card.

> [!tip] The three rules that build a score
> (1) **Pay on time, always** — payment history is 35% of the score. One missed payment drops you 60+ points. (2) **Keep utilization low** — under 10% is ideal, 30% is the red line. (3) **Don't close old cards** — history length matters; if an old card has no annual fee, keep it alive with one charge a year.

## 3. Healthcare — the critical one

Nothing in a Chinese student's prior experience prepares them for US health-insurance vocabulary. Six terms all simultaneously in play determine what a single visit actually costs.

| English | 中文 | What it does |
|---------|------|-------------|
| Premium | 保费 | Monthly fee to hold the policy — paid **whether or not** you use care |
| Deductible | 年度自付额 | What **you pay first** each year before insurance kicks in |
| Copay | 固定自付 | Flat fee per visit after deductible ($20 office, $100 ER, etc.) |
| Coinsurance | 共同保险比例 | **Percentage** split after deductible (typically 20% you / 80% plan) |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | 年度自付上限 | The **ceiling** — once you've paid this much in a calendar year, the plan pays 100% |
| Formulary | 药品目录 | The list of drugs the plan covers, organized by tier |

### Plan types

| Type | Full name | Tradeoff |
|------|-----------|----------|
| HMO | Health Maintenance Organization | Cheapest premium; must stay in-network; need referral for specialists |
| PPO | Preferred Provider Organization | Higher premium; see any doctor; partial coverage out-of-network |
| EPO | Exclusive Provider Organization | Middle; no referrals but in-network only |
| HDHP | High-Deductible Health Plan | Low premium, high deductible ($1,600+ single in 2024); **pairs with HSA** |

### In-network vs out-of-network

The plan has contracts with specific hospitals, clinics, and doctors (**in-network**). In-network: your deductible / copay / coinsurance apply at the advertised level. Out-of-network: the plan pays much less or nothing, and the provider can **balance-bill** you the difference between their charge and what the plan reimbursed.

> [!warning] Worked example — a "covered" ER visit that costs $2,500
> Student has a mid-tier PPO: **$2,000 deductible**, **20% coinsurance**, **$8,000 OOP max**. They go to the ER with a suspected fracture. Billed total: **$4,500**.
>
> - First $2,000 → **you pay** (deductible not yet met)
> - Remaining $2,500 → 20% coinsurance → **you pay $500**, plan pays $2,000
> - **Total you pay: $2,500**
>
> If the hospital was out-of-network, add another $800–1,500 in balance billing. "Insurance covered it" ≠ "it was free." Until you hit the OOP max, you pay a substantial share of every bill.

### HSA vs FSA

- **HSA (Health Savings Account)** — pairs only with an HDHP. **Triple tax advantage**: contributions pre-tax, growth tax-free, withdrawals tax-free for qualified medical expenses. Rolls over forever. Functions as a stealth retirement account if you don't spend it.
- **FSA (Flexible Spending Account)** — use-it-or-lose-it annual allowance for medical expenses; pre-tax but forfeit unspent balance at year end.

## 4. Retirement and Tax-Advantaged Accounts

Four-layer system: Social Security (public, automatic), employer-sponsored (401(k) / 403(b)), individual (IRA), and taxable brokerage. Tax-advantaged accounts have annual **contribution limits** that reset each January.

| Account | Purpose | Limit (2025) | Tax treatment |
|---------|---------|---------------|---------------|
| 401(k) | Employer-sponsored | $23,500 employee | Pre-tax now, taxed on withdrawal |
| Roth 401(k) | Post-tax variant | Same $23,500 | Taxed now, tax-free withdrawal |
| Traditional IRA | Individual retirement | $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+) | Pre-tax now, taxed on withdrawal |
| Roth IRA | Post-tax individual | Same $7,000 | Taxed now, **tax-free growth and withdrawal** |
| HSA | Health (HDHP only) | $4,300 single / $8,550 family | Triple tax-free |
| 529 plan | Education savings | No federal limit; state varies | Tax-free for qualified education expenses |

### Employer match — the clearest free money in your life

If your employer offers a **401(k) match**, they contribute a percentage of *your salary* to *your 401(k)* in addition to your own contribution. A typical match is "100% of the first 3% of salary, then 50% of the next 2%" — a 4% match cap. Contributing enough to capture the full match is a silent 4% raise; not contributing is forfeiting that raise.

> [!tip] Roth vs Traditional — which to pick as a student
> Grad student with low current income but a likely high future income → **Roth is usually right**: pay low taxes now, withdraw tax-free after you're a senior engineer earning $300k. Traditional is better for high earners now who expect to retire at a lower bracket.

## 5. Taxes

Taxes are filed annually, due **April 15** for the prior calendar year. Most workers have taxes **withheld from each paycheck** throughout the year and reconcile via a return (paying the shortfall or receiving a refund).

| Form | What it is |
|------|-----------|
| W-2 | Annual wage statement from your employer (arrives in January) |
| W-4 | Form **you** fill out so your employer knows how much to withhold |
| 1099 | Income from non-employer sources (dividends, contract work, interest, brokerage) — many subtypes |
| 1040 | The main annual tax return |
| 1040-NR | Non-resident alien version (F-1 students in first 5 years) |

### Identifiers

- **SSN (Social Security Number)** — 9 digits, issued to citizens, green-card holders, and work-authorized visa holders (F-1 with on-campus work, H-1B, etc.). Used for taxes, banking, credit, employment.
- **ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number)** — 9 digits starting with 9, issued to people who must file taxes but don't qualify for an SSN (some F-1 students with scholarships, certain dependents, non-resident investors). Tax-only; can't be used for employment.

### Payroll taxes — FICA

**FICA** = Social Security (6.2% up to a wage cap, ~$168,600 in 2024) + Medicare (1.45% uncapped + 0.9% surcharge on wages > $200k). Employers match these. Withheld automatically. **F-1 students are exempt from FICA for their first 5 calendar years** — a common payroll error; if you see FICA on your F-1 paycheck, ask payroll to correct it and refund.

### Income tax layers

- **Federal** — progressive brackets (10 / 12 / 22 / 24 / 32 / 35 / 37% in 2025)
- **State** — varies wildly: 0% in Texas / Florida / Washington / Nevada; 9–13% in California / New York / New Jersey
- **Local** — some cities (NYC, Philadelphia) add 1–4%

### Deductions

Take the **standard deduction** ($14,600 single in 2024, rises with inflation) or **itemize** (mortgage interest, state and local taxes capped at $10k under SALT, charity, medical > 7.5% of AGI). Since 2018 the standard deduction has been high enough that most filers don't itemize.

## 6. Mortgage

| Feature | Standard US | What this means |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Term | **30-year fixed** (globally unusual) | Rate locked for three decades; most countries' "fixed" means 2–10 years |
| Shorter alternative | 15-year fixed | Higher monthly payment, roughly half the total interest |
| Adjustable | ARM (5/1, 7/1, 10/1) | Fixed for first $N$ years, then annually adjustable |
| Down payment | 20% standard | Below 20% triggers PMI |
| PMI | Private Mortgage Insurance | Paid monthly until equity reaches 20%; protects the lender, not you |
| Points | Discount points | Pay 1% of loan upfront to reduce rate by ~0.25% |
| Escrow | 履约账户 | Monthly payment includes 1/12 of annual property tax + homeowners insurance, held by the lender and paid on your behalf |

## 7. International-Student Corner

### Visa and tax residency

- **F-1 students** are **non-resident aliens** for tax purposes for their **first 5 calendar years**, filing **Form 1040-NR**. Non-residents don't get the standard deduction (with treaty exceptions — the **US–China tax treaty Article 20** allows a $5,000 wage exemption for F-1 students), can't claim many tax credits, and are exempt from FICA.
- After 5 calendar years, F-1 students typically become **resident aliens** for tax purposes under the **Substantial Presence Test**, and file the regular 1040.

### SSN or ITIN

Get an SSN if you take on-campus employment, RA/TA work, CPT, or OPT — the work authorization unlocks SSN eligibility. Without any work authorization, apply for an **ITIN** when you file your first 1040-NR (typically to reclaim withholding on scholarship income).

### Credit history from zero

F-1 students arrive with no SSN initially and no US credit file. Day-1 strategy:

1. Open a checking account at a bank that accepts passport + I-20 (Bank of America, Chase, TD Bank usually do; some small banks don't).
2. Apply for a **secured credit card** — Discover's program accepts ITIN or passport; Capital One has similar paths. Deposit = credit limit. Builds credit from day 1.
3. After getting an SSN, apply for an **unsecured student card** (Discover it Student, Capital One Quicksilver Student).
4. Never carry a balance. Never miss a payment. Keep utilization under 10%. After 12 months your FICO score enters normal range.

### In-state vs out-of-state tuition

Public universities charge **in-state** residents a fraction of the **out-of-state** rate (UC Berkeley: ~$14k vs ~$47k per year in 2024). International students pay **out-of-state** regardless of how long they live in the state. Private universities charge a single rate to everyone.
