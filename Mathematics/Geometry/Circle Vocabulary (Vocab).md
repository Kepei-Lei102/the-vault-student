# Circle Vocabulary 圆的术语

## Definition

A **circle** (圆 yuán) is the set of all points at a fixed distance from a centre point. Every term below names a specific part of the circle or a line related to it. You need these words to state and prove Circle Theorems.

### 中文锚点

圆 = 到圆心距离相等的所有点的集合。半径 = 圆心到圆上的线段，直径 = 穿过圆心的弦（最长的弦），弦 = 连接圆上两点的线段，弧 = 圆上的一段弧线，扇形 = 两条半径和弧围成的区域，弓形 = 弦和弧围成的区域，切线 = 与圆只有一个交点的直线。

## Key Vocabulary — Parts of a Circle

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---------|------|------------|
| **centre** | 圆心 (yuánxīn) | The fixed point; every point on the circle is equidistant from it |
| **radius** (pl. radii) | 半径 (bànjìng) | Line segment from the centre to any point on the circle. Length $r$ |
| **diameter** | 直径 (zhíjìng) | Line segment through the centre with both endpoints on the circle. Length $d = 2r$ |
| **circumference** | 周长 (zhōucháng) | The perimeter of the circle. $C = \pi d = 2\pi r$ |
| **chord** | 弦 (xián) | Line segment joining any two points on the circle |
| **arc** | 弧 (hú) | A portion of the circumference |
| **minor arc** | 劣弧 (lièhú) | The shorter arc between two points (less than half the circle) |
| **major arc** | 优弧 (yōuhú) | The longer arc between two points (more than half the circle) |
| **semicircle** | 半圆 (bànyuán) | Exactly half the circle; bounded by a diameter |
| **sector** | 扇形 (shànxíng) | The "pizza slice" region between two radii and an arc |
| **segment** | 弓形 (gōngxíng) | The region between a chord and the arc it cuts off |

> [!warning] Sector vs segment — the classic confusion
> A **sector** is bounded by two **radii** and an arc (like a pizza slice — straight edges go to the centre). A **segment** is bounded by a **chord** and an arc (like the crust cut off by a straight knife — the straight edge doesn't go to the centre). Think: **sec**tor has the **c**entre; **seg**ment doesn't.

> [!warning] A diameter is a chord — the longest one
> Every diameter is a chord (it joins two points on the circle). Not every chord is a diameter (most don't pass through the centre). The diameter is the **longest possible chord**.

## Key Vocabulary — Lines Related to a Circle

| English | 中文 | Definition |
|---------|------|------------|
| **tangent** | 切线 (qiēxiàn) | A straight line that touches the circle at exactly **one** point (the point of tangency 切点) |
| **secant** | 割线 (gēxiàn) | A straight line that cuts the circle at **two** points |
| **normal** | 法线 (fǎxiàn) | The line perpendicular to the tangent at the point of tangency; passes through the centre |

> [!tip] Tangent and secant — you've seen these names before
> In [[Trigonometric Functions]], we learned that "tangent" comes from Latin *tangere* ("to touch") and "secant" from *secare* ("to cut"). The same words, the same meanings: a tangent *touches* the circle, a secant *cuts* through it. The trig functions were originally defined as lengths of these lines on the unit circle.

## Key Properties (preview of Circle Theorems)

These are stated here as vocabulary; the proofs come in [[Circle Theorems I]] and [[Circle Theorems II]]:

- A tangent is **perpendicular** to the radius at the point of tangency.
- The perpendicular from the centre to a chord **bisects** the chord.
- Equal chords are **equidistant** from the centre.

## Exam Notes

**9260 G8 / 0580 E4.6.** Knowing these terms is a prerequisite for Circle Theorems questions. Examiners use precise vocabulary ("the tangent at $P$", "the chord $AB$", "the arc $BC$") and expect you to know exactly what each means. Confusing "sector" with "segment" will lose marks even if your method is correct.

> [!note] $\pi$ is irrational
> The ratio $C/d = \pi$ is the same for every circle in Euclidean geometry — but $\pi$ cannot be expressed as a fraction (proved by Lambert, 1761) or as a root of any polynomial (proved by Lindemann, 1882, settling the 2000-year-old problem of "squaring the circle"). Its decimal expansion never terminates and never repeats: $3.14159265\ldots$ This is why you always leave $\pi$ in exact answers rather than writing $3.14$.

## Beyond the Syllabus

**Circles in non-Euclidean geometry.** On a sphere, the ratio $C/d$ is less than $\pi$ (great circles are "too short" relative to their diameter). In hyperbolic space, $C/d > \pi$. The value of this ratio tells you about the curvature of the space you're in — a key idea in general relativity.
