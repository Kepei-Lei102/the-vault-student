# Plans and Elevations 平面图与立面图 · 三视图

## Definition

A **plan** is the view of a solid from directly above. A **front elevation** is the view from directly in front, and a **side elevation** the view from directly one side, usually the right. Each is a flat drawing, made as if the solid were photographed square-on from that direction with all perspective removed, so lengths in the view are the solid's true lengths and can be measured with a ruler. In a view, a line is drawn along every edge you would see from that direction, including the edges where the visible surface steps up or down; edges hidden behind the solid are left out, or drawn dashed when a question asks for them. Together the three views pin down the solid; any one of them alone does not.

### 中文锚点

站在一栋房子正前方拍一张照片。再绕到侧面拍一张，然后放一架无人机直直飞上去，把屋顶也拍下来。每张照片都丢掉了一个方向：正面那张说不出房子有多深，侧面那张说不出有多宽，屋顶那张说不出有多高。三张放在一起，这栋房子就被完全定住了；少了一张，两栋很不一样的房子可能拍出同样的两张照片。平面图和立面图说的就是这件事：一个立体的东西，从正上方、正前方、正侧方各正对着看一眼，画成三张平面的图，看得见的表面哪里有高低起伏，就在哪里画一条线。建筑师、工程师、做拼装家具的人画的正是这种图，因为施工的人需要能用尺子量出来的尺寸，而一张透视的速写里没有一个尺寸是可以直接量出来的。

## The three views of one solid

![[plans-and-elevations-three-views.svg|960]]

Seven cubes, drawn once in three dimensions and then three times flat. Read the front elevation against the solid: the column of three at the left, the step down to two, then to one. Read the plan: the three-in-a-row with one cube sticking out at the front. The horizontal lines inside the front elevation are not decoration; each marks a place where the surface facing you steps back, and a marker will look for them.

## Key Vocabulary

| English | 中文 | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| plan (plan view) | 俯视图 (fǔshìtú) | from directly above; Chinese textbooks say "从上面看" |
| front elevation | 主视图 / 正视图 (zhǔshìtú) | from directly in front; Chinese textbooks say "从正面看" |
| side elevation | 左视图 / 右视图 (zuǒshìtú / yòushìtú) | from one side; English questions say which side ("from the right"), Chinese textbooks default to the left |
| elevation | 立面图 (lìmiàntú) | any view from the side, horizontally; the word also means "height above sea level" and "angle of elevation", which are unrelated |
| three views | 三视图 (sānshìtú) | the Chinese curriculum's name for the set; no single English word for the trio |
| orthographic projection | 正投影 (zhèng tóuyǐng) | the method: project every point straight onto the drawing plane, no perspective |
| isometric drawing | 等轴测图 (děngzhóucètú) | the 3D-looking drawing on triangular dotted paper; **not** a plan or elevation |
| hidden edge | 虚线 / 看不见的棱 | drawn dashed when required; most IGCSE questions ask only for visible edges |
| true length | 实长 (shícháng) | a length in a view equals the length on the solid, because there is no perspective |

> [!warning] Language trap: "elevation" three ways
> In this topic an elevation is a *view from the side*. In trigonometry the *angle of elevation* is the angle you look up through, and on a map *elevation* is height above sea level. An exam that says "draw the front elevation" wants a drawing, not an angle. The Chinese 立面图 has no such ambiguity, and 仰角 is the trigonometry word.

> [!warning] Language trap: which side, and where the views sit
> Chinese textbooks draw the 三视图 in a fixed arrangement (the 主视图 top left, the 左视图 to its right, the 俯视图 below), and the side view is the *left* view. English questions name the side, usually "from the right" or with an arrow on the diagram, and accept the views drawn anywhere as long as each is labelled. The drawings are the same; the words and the layout are not. If a question gives an arrow, draw the elevation as seen by someone standing at the arrow looking along it.

> [!tip] Exam phrasing
> "On the grid, draw the plan of the solid." "Draw the front elevation from the direction of the arrow." "The diagram shows the plan and front elevation of a solid made from centimetre cubes. Draw a 3D sketch of the solid on the isometric grid." (That last one is the reverse direction: rebuilding the solid from its views, which is where the ambiguity below bites.) Views are drawn on squared paper at full size unless a scale is stated; count squares.

## Why three, and not fewer

![[plans-and-elevations-two-solids.svg|960]]

Solid A and solid B have the same plan and the same front elevation. Only the side elevation separates them. A view squashes out one direction, so two views can agree while the solids differ in exactly the direction both views threw away. This is the whole reason engineering drawings carry three views (and, for awkward shapes, more): a builder handed a plan and a front elevation of solid A could build solid B in good faith. When a question gives you two views and asks for the solid, look for the cube that could be present or absent without changing either view; if there is one, the question will have given you the third view or a count of cubes to settle it.

## How to draw them, and how they are marked

1. **Decide the directions.** Front is the face the arrow points at or the face named in the question; right side is what you see standing to the right of that front, looking at the solid; plan is from above with the front at the bottom of the drawing.
2. **Draw the outline** of the silhouette from that direction, counting squares, full size.
3. **Add the visible edges** inside the outline: every place where the surface facing you changes level. In the plan, that is every place the height changes; in an elevation, every place the depth changes.
4. **Do not add** edges you could only see from another direction, and do not shade, unless asked.

A marker gives a mark for the correct outline and a mark for the correct internal lines, and takes off for extra lines that are not edges. The commonest lost mark is the internal line: a plan drawn as a plain rectangle when the solid has a step.

## Exam Notes

### OxAQA 9260

**Syllabus ref:** G11. Core: "interpret plans and elevations of 3D shapes". Extension: "construct and interpret plans and elevations of 3D shapes". So a Core question hands you the views and asks which solid, or asks a question about it; an Extension question asks you to draw them, on a grid, from a 3D sketch or from a solid built of cubes. 9260 is the only board in this vault that examines the topic.

### Where this is *not* examined

Cambridge 0580 and 0606 have no plans-and-elevations row: 0580's only "elevation" is the angle of elevation in E6.2, and its solids work (E4.x, E5.x) is about properties, nets, surface area and volume. Cambridge 9709 has none. The Chinese junior-secondary curriculum examines it as 投影与视图 (人教版 九年级下; 北师大版 九年级上 第四章), which is why the vocabulary above runs both ways.
