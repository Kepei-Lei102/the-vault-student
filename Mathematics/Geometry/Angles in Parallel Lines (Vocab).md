# Angles in Parallel Lines 平行线中的角

## Definition

When a straight line (called a **transversal** 截线) crosses two **parallel** lines, it creates eight angles. The relationships between these angles have specific names that examiners expect you to use.

### 中文锚点

平行线被一条截线所截，产生三种特殊角对：同位角（相等）、内错角（相等）、同旁内角（互补，和为 $180°$）。

---

## Key Vocabulary

| English | 中文 | Relationship | How to spot them |
|---------|------|-------------|-----------------|
| **corresponding angles** | 同位角 (tóngwèi jiǎo) | **Equal** | Same position at each intersection — like copies. F-shape. |
| **alternate angles** | 内错角 (nèicuò jiǎo) | **Equal** | Opposite sides of the transversal, between the parallel lines. Z-shape (or reverse Z). |
| **co-interior angles** (allied) | 同旁内角 (tóngpáng nèijiǎo) | **Sum to $180°$** | Same side of the transversal, between the parallel lines. C-shape (or U-shape). |
| **transversal** | 截线 (jiéxiàn) | — | The line that crosses the parallel lines |

> [!tip] The letter shapes — F, Z, C
> Rotate the letter to match what you see in the diagram. The F can be forwards or backwards. The Z can be an N. The C can be a U. These aren't rules — they're visual hints to help you spot the pattern. The actual rule is always about the **position** relative to the transversal and the parallel lines.

---

## WHY They Work

### Corresponding angles are equal

Slide one intersection along the transversal until it sits on top of the other. Because the lines are parallel (same direction), the two intersections are identical — just translated. Translation preserves angles, so corresponding angles must be equal.

### Alternate angles are equal

From the diagram: an alternate angle and its partner are both related to the same corresponding angle. One equals it directly (corresponding); the other equals it by vertically opposite. So they equal each other. **This is a two-step proof, not an axiom.**

### Co-interior angles sum to $180°$

One co-interior angle and the alternate angle on the other side are supplementary (straight line). But the alternate angle equals its partner. So the co-interior pair sums to $180°$.

> [!important] Only one of these three needs to be assumed
> In Euclid's geometry, only the corresponding-angles rule is taken as an axiom (or equivalently, the parallel postulate). Alternate and co-interior follow by proof. This is a common exam "show that" chain.

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Using these rules when lines aren't parallel.** All three relationships require parallel lines. If the question doesn't state or mark the lines as parallel, you cannot use these rules.
2. **Confusing co-interior with alternate.** Alternate angles are on **opposite** sides of the transversal (and are equal). Co-interior are on the **same** side (and sum to $180°$). The Z vs C shapes help.
3. **Not naming the rule in "give reasons" questions.** Writing "alternate angles" or "co-interior angles" is required for full marks.

---

## Exam Notes

**9260 G3 / 0580 E4.2.** "Understand and use the angle properties of parallel lines." Questions often combine these with angles on a straight line or vertically opposite to find unknown angles in multi-step problems. Always state which property you use at each step.

---

## Beyond the Syllabus

**Euclid's parallel postulate** is the axiom that makes all of this work. For 2000 years, mathematicians tried to prove it from the other axioms and failed. In the 19th century, Lobachevsky and Riemann showed you can build consistent geometries where parallel lines *do* meet (spherical geometry — think great circles on Earth) or where there are infinitely many parallels through a point (hyperbolic geometry). These **non-Euclidean geometries** turned out to be exactly what Einstein needed for general relativity: spacetime is curved, and the angles of a triangle don't always sum to $180°$.
