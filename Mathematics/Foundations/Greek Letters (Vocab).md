# Greek Letters 希腊字母

## Definition

Mathematics borrows the Greek alphabet whenever the Latin one runs out — or when a quantity deserves a symbol that will not be mistaken for an ordinary variable. The letters are not random: most were chosen because the Greek letter **sounds like the first letter of the English word**, and that pattern makes almost the whole set guessable rather than memorisable.

Two habits are worth having from the start. **Capital Greek letters usually stand for operations or large objects** ($\sum$ sum, $\prod$ product, $\Delta$ change); **lowercase Greek letters usually stand for quantities or parameters** ($\sigma$ deviation, $\mu$ mean, $\theta$ angle).

### 中文锚点

数学里的希腊字母不是随便选的：多数是因为**希腊字母的读音 = 那个英文单词的首字母**。$\sum$ = Sum（求和），$\mu$ = Mean（平均），$\Delta$ = Difference（差）。

**大写**通常表示运算或整体（$\sum$ 求和、$\prod$ 连乘、$\Delta$ 变化量）；**小写**通常表示某个量或参数（$\sigma$ 标准差、$\theta$ 角度、$\lambda$ 波长）。

英文考卷会直接把字母名念出来（"sigma x", "delta y", "theta"），所以**读法要会**——听不懂 "mu" 指的是 $\mu$，整道题就断在第一句。

> [!warning] The same letter does different jobs in different subjects
> Greek letters are recycled ruthlessly. The symbol does **not** carry the meaning — the *context* does, and every exam question tells you which one it means.
>
> - $\sigma$ — standard deviation (statistics), electrical conductivity (physics), stress (materials).
> - $\mu$ — population mean (statistics), the prefix **micro-** = $10^{-6}$ (units), coefficient of friction (mechanics), permeability (magnetism).
> - $\lambda$ — wavelength (waves), decay/arrival rate (Poisson, radioactivity), eigenvalue (matrices), an anonymous function (lambda calculus in computing).
> - $\Delta$ — "change in" (all subjects), but the **discriminant** $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ in quadratics.
> - $\rho$ — density (physics), correlation coefficient (statistics).
>
> When you meet a Greek letter cold, ask what subject you are in before you ask what the letter means.

> [!tip] Say them out loud — the exam will
> Written papers are safe, but teachers, videos and oral explanations pronounce these letters by name: *"sigma x over n"*, *"delta y by delta x"*, *"lambda equals v over f"*. A symbol you can read but cannot **hear** is a symbol you will lose in a lesson. The pronunciation column above is the one part of this card worth rehearsing aloud.

> [!warning] $\sum$ is an instruction, not a number
> $\sum x$ does not mean "sigma times x". The capital sigma is a **command**: *add up every value of $x$*. Similarly $\Delta x$ is not "delta times x" — it is one quantity, "the change in $x$", and you cannot cancel the $\Delta$ from top and bottom of $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$.

## Exam Notes

- No syllabus lists "Greek letters" as a topic; they arrive unannounced inside other topics, which is exactly why they are worth meeting once on purpose.
- **First encounters, in rough order:** $\pi$ and $\theta$ in IGCSE geometry and trigonometry; $\sum$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ when statistics starts; $\Delta$ in gradients and rates of change; $\lambda$, $\omega$, $\rho$ and $\tau$ across A-Level physics; $\Phi$ in the normal distribution; $\xi$ for the Cambridge universal set.
- **Write them legibly.** Markers cannot award a mark for a symbol they cannot identify — the usual casualties are $\mu$ against $u$, $\nu$ (nu) against $v$, $\rho$ against $p$, and a lowercase $\sigma$ against a $6$. Where confusion is likely, examiners' reports advise writing the quantity's name in words alongside the first use.
