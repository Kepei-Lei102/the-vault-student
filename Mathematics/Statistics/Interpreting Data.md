# Interpreting Data 解读数据

## Definition

### Formal

**Interpreting data** means reading information from tables, charts, and statistical summaries, then drawing conclusions, making comparisons, and communicating findings using precise mathematical language. This card focuses on the **language** of data interpretation — the command words, comparison vocabulary, and inference phrasing that examiners expect.

### Intuitive

You've already learnt how to *draw* charts, *calculate* averages, and *construct* cumulative frequency curves. This card is about the other half: **reading them back** and writing about what you see — in the specific way the examiner expects.

International students often lose marks not because they don't understand the maths, but because they don't use the **exact phrasing** the mark scheme rewards. This card is your phrasebook.

> [!tip] 解读 vs 解释 — nuance matters
> 解读 (jiědú) means "to interpret / decode" — extracting meaning from data. 解释 (jiěshì) means "to explain" — giving reasons. The exam uses both: "interpret" asks *what does this show?*, while "explain" asks *why does this happen?* Knowing which Chinese word maps to which English command word helps avoid the classic trap of explaining when you should only be stating.

## Command Words 命令词

Command words tell you **what the examiner expects** in your answer. Using the wrong approach for a command word is the fastest way to lose marks.

### The complete reference

| Command word | What it means | Working required? | Marks hint |
|-------------|---------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Calculate** | Work out from given facts, figures, or information | Yes — full working | Multi-mark |
| **Construct** | Make an accurate drawing (with ruler, compasses, protractor) | Yes — precise drawing | 0580 only |
| **Describe** | State the points of a topic / give characteristics and main features | No calculation, but specific detail | 2–3 marks |
| **Determine** | Establish with certainty | Yes — working expected | Multi-mark |
| **Explain** | Set out purposes or reasons / make relationships clear / say **why** and support with evidence | Reasoning, not just facts | 2+ marks |
| **Give** | Produce an answer from a given source or recall | Minimal or none | 1 mark |
| **Plot** | Mark point(s) on a graph | Accurate plotting | 1–2 marks |
| **Show (that)** | Provide structured evidence that leads to a given result — essentially a **proof** | Yes — every step, arrive at the stated answer | Multi-mark |
| **Sketch** | Make a simple freehand drawing showing key features | Approximate shape, labelled | 2–3 marks |
| **State** | Express in clear terms | No working needed | 1 mark |
| **Verify** | Confirm a given statement/result is true | Substitute and check | 0606 only |
| **Work out** | Calculate from given facts, figures, or information | Yes — full working | Multi-mark |
| **Write** | Give an answer in a specific form | As specified | 1 mark |
| **Write down** | Give an answer **without significant working** | No — just the answer | 1 mark |

### Syllabus-specific differences

| Word | 0580 | 0606 | 9260 |
|------|------|------|------|
| Construct | Yes — "make an accurate drawing" | No | No |
| Verify | No | Yes — "confirm a given statement/result is true" | No |
| All others | Shared across both Cambridge syllabi | Shared | Uses AQA conventions (no formal list) |

### Critical pairs students confuse

**State vs Explain**

| | State | Explain |
|-|-------|---------|
| What they want | A fact or value — no reasoning | A reason — *why* something happens |
| Working | None | Must give cause/evidence |
| Example | "State the modal class" → $10 < x \leq 20$ | "Explain why the mean is not suitable" → "Because there is an extreme outlier (98) that skews the mean" |
| Marks | Usually 1 | Usually 2+ |

> [!warning] The "Explain" trap for ESL students
> Many Chinese students write "The mean is not suitable because it is not good" — this is circular and scores 0. An explanation must give a **specific reason**: *what* feature of the data causes the problem, and *how* it affects the measure.

**Show (that) vs Work out**

"Show that" is the exam's way of asking for a **proof**. The answer is handed to you — your job is to build a logical chain from what's given to the stated conclusion, with no gaps.

| | Show (that) | Work out |
|-|-------------|----------|
| Answer given? | Yes — the result is stated in the question | No — you must find it |
| What they check | Every intermediate step is present and correct — a complete logical chain | The final answer (with working for method marks) |
| Danger | Skipping steps or working backwards from the answer | Arithmetic errors with no partial credit |
| Mindset | Think of it as writing a **proof**: given → step → step → conclusion | Think of it as solving a puzzle: find the unknown |

> [!warning] "Show that" = write a proof, not just confirm the answer
> If the question says "Show that the median is 45", you must demonstrate *how* to get 45 — building a logical argument from the data to the conclusion. Every step must be visible. If you skip a step, you lose the mark even though you have the right answer. Working backwards (starting from 45 and showing it fits) is risky — examiners prefer a forward chain of reasoning.

> [!tip] How to structure a "Show that" answer
> 1. **State what you're given** — write down the relevant information from the question
> 2. **Build forward** — apply definitions, formulas, or logical steps in order
> 3. **Arrive at the stated result** — conclude with the exact value or expression given in the question
>
> This is the same structure as a mathematical proof: premises → reasoning → conclusion. Practising "Show that" at IGCSE builds the proof-writing skills you'll need at A-Level, IB, and university.

**Write down vs Calculate**

| | Write down | Calculate |
|-|-----------|-----------|
| Working | None expected — just write the answer | Full working expected |
| Marks | 1 (usually) | 2+ (method + accuracy) |
| Implication | The answer should be obvious or directly readable | The answer requires processing |

## Comparison Vocabulary 比较词汇

When an exam asks you to **compare** two distributions (e.g. two box plots, two sets of data), you must make **two types of statement**: one about an **average** (central tendency) and one about **spread** (consistency).

### The comparison framework

Every comparison answer needs:

1. **A statement about average** — which group has a higher/lower typical value
2. **A statement about spread** — which group is more consistent/varied
3. **Context** — use the actual variable names, not just "data set A"

### Vocabulary for averages

| What you observe | How to write it |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Group A has higher mean/median | "On average, [Group A] scored higher than [Group B]" |
| Group A has lower mean/median | "The typical value for [Group A] is lower, suggesting..." |
| Medians are similar | "Both groups have similar typical values (median ≈ ...)" |

### Vocabulary for spread

| What you observe | How to write it |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Group A has larger IQR/range | "[Group A] shows more variation / is less consistent" |
| Group A has smaller IQR/range | "[Group A] is more consistent / less spread out" |
| Similar spread | "Both groups show similar levels of consistency" |

### Vocabulary for correlation (scatter diagrams)

| Pattern | Description |
|---------|------------|
| Points rise left to right | "Positive correlation — as [x] increases, [y] tends to increase" |
| Points fall left to right | "Negative correlation — as [x] increases, [y] tends to decrease" |
| No pattern | "No correlation — there is no clear relationship between [x] and [y]" |
| Tight cluster around line | "Strong [positive/negative] correlation" |
| Loose scatter around line | "Weak [positive/negative] correlation" |

> [!warning] Correlation does not imply causation
> "There is a positive correlation between ice cream sales and drownings" does **not** mean ice cream causes drowning. Both increase in summer (the lurking variable is temperature). Always say "tends to" — never "causes".

### Vocabulary for skewness

| Shape | How to describe it |
|-------|-------------------|
| Symmetric | "The distribution is approximately symmetric — mean ≈ median" |
| Positive skew (tail to right) | "The distribution is positively skewed — the mean is greater than the median, pulled up by high values" |
| Negative skew (tail to left) | "The distribution is negatively skewed — the mean is less than the median, pulled down by low values" |

## Inference and Conclusion Phrasing

### Drawing conclusions from data

Mark schemes reward specific, **hedged** language. Never claim certainty from a sample.

| Instead of... | Write... |
|--------------|----------|
| "This proves that..." | "This suggests that..." |
| "Boys are better at maths" | "On average, boys scored higher in this sample" |
| "There is a correlation so X causes Y" | "There is a positive correlation, suggesting a relationship, but this does not prove causation" |
| "The data shows everyone..." | "The data suggests that most..." |

### Limitations of data

Examiners often ask about **reliability** and **limitations**:

| Limitation | Example phrasing |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Small sample size | "The sample of 20 is too small to draw reliable conclusions about the whole population" |
| Biased sample | "The sample only includes students from one school, so may not represent all students" |
| No control group | "Without a comparison group, we cannot attribute the improvement to the new method" |
| Correlation ≠ causation | "Although a correlation exists, other factors may explain the relationship" |

## Misconceptions

### 1. Writing "the data is consistent" without specifying *what measure* shows this

"Consistent" is vague. The mark scheme wants you to cite a specific statistic.

**Fix:** "The IQR of Class A (8) is smaller than Class B (15), so Class A is more consistent."

### 2. Comparing without using context

Writing "Group 1 has a higher median than Group 2" scores partial marks at best. The mark scheme almost always requires the variable name.

**Fix:** "The median test score for Class A (67%) is higher than for Class B (54%), suggesting Class A performed better on average."

### 3. Using "average" without specifying which one

"The average is higher" — which average? Mean? Median? Mode? The mark scheme usually specifies or expects you to name it.

**Fix:** Always name the measure: "The *median* time for girls (12.3 s) is lower than for boys (14.1 s)."

### 4. Confusing "describe the trend" with "explain the trend"

"Describe" = say what you see (the pattern). "Explain" = say why it happens (the reason).

**Fix:**
- Describe: "The scatter diagram shows a positive correlation between hours studied and test score."
- Explain: "Students who study more hours tend to score higher because they have more time to practise and consolidate the material."

### 5. Forgetting the "in context" requirement

Many 9260 and 0580 questions end with "...in context" or "...with reference to the data". This means you must use the actual variable names (e.g. "height in cm", "number of goals") — not just abstract language.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 — Comparing two box plots (9260 S6 / 0580 E9.2)

Two classes took the same test. Their results are shown in box plots:

| | Class A | Class B |
|-|---------|---------|
| Minimum | 32 | 25 |
| Q1 | 48 | 42 |
| Median | 65 | 58 |
| Q3 | 74 | 76 |
| Maximum | 92 | 95 |

**Compare the two distributions.**

The median score for Class A (65) is higher than for Class B (58), suggesting Class A performed better on average.

The interquartile range for Class A is $74 - 48 = 26$, compared to $76 - 42 = 34$ for Class B. This means Class A's scores were more consistent (less spread), while Class B had greater variation in performance.

---

### Example 2 — Describing a scatter diagram (0580 E9.5 / 9260 S7)

A scatter diagram shows the relationship between temperature (°C) and number of hot drinks sold.

**Describe the relationship shown.**

There is a negative correlation between temperature and the number of hot drinks sold. As the temperature increases, the number of hot drinks sold tends to decrease. The correlation appears to be strong, as the points are clustered closely around a line of best fit.

---

### Example 3 — "Explain why" question (9260 / 0580)

A data set of employee salaries has mean = £48,000 and median = £31,000.

**Explain why the median is a more suitable average than the mean for this data.**

The mean (£48,000) is much higher than the median (£31,000), which suggests the data is positively skewed — a few very high salaries are pulling the mean upwards. The median is not affected by these extreme values, so it gives a more representative measure of the typical salary.

## Exam Notes

### 9260

- **S2:** "Read, interpret and draw inferences from tables and statistical diagrams" — this is the core interpretation skill.
- **S6:** "Compare sets of data using tables, graphs, and statistical measures" — compare averages *and* spread in context.
- **S7:** "Appreciate restrictions on drawing conclusions from given data" — limitations, sample size, correlation ≠ causation.
- No formal command word list — follows AQA conventions. In practice, the same command words as 0580/0606 appear.

### 0580 (E9.2)

- "Read, interpret and draw inferences from tables and statistical diagrams."
- "Compare sets of data using tables, graphs and statistical measures" — e.g. compare averages and ranges between two data sets.
- "Appreciate restrictions on drawing conclusions from given data."

### 0606

- The same command words appear but in more algebraically demanding contexts.
- "Verify" is unique to 0606: you must substitute a value and confirm the result matches what's claimed.
- Statistics interpretation is less prominent in 0606 (which focuses on pure functions and calculus), but the command words apply across all topics.

### Mark scheme patterns

| Question type | What the mark scheme expects |
|--------------|------------------------------|
| "Compare..." (2 marks) | One statement about average + one about spread, both in context |
| "Describe the correlation" (1 mark) | Type (positive/negative/none) + strength (strong/weak) |
| "Explain why..." (2 marks) | A reason + supporting evidence from the data |
| "State..." (1 mark) | A single clear answer — no explanation needed |
| "Give a reason why the mean is not suitable" (1 mark) | Identify the specific feature (outlier/skew) that distorts the mean |

## Beyond Syllabus

### AP / IB / A-Level depth

**Statistical inference and hypothesis testing**

At higher levels, "interpreting data" formalises into **inferential statistics** — using sample data to make statements about populations. Key ideas that build on IGCSE interpretation:

- **Confidence intervals:** Instead of "the average is about 65", you say "we are 95% confident the true population mean is between 62 and 68."
- **Hypothesis testing:** Instead of "there seems to be a difference", you calculate a p-value to determine whether the observed difference is statistically significant or could have occurred by chance.
- **Effect size:** Beyond "is there a difference?", you ask "how big is the difference?" — a crucial distinction in research.

The vocabulary of hedging you learn at IGCSE ("suggests", "tends to", "on average") is directly preparing you for the formal language of statistical inference, where certainty is always qualified by probability.

### Beyond high school — University

**The replication crisis and statistical literacy**

Understanding how to properly interpret data is one of the most important skills for any field. The "replication crisis" in psychology and medicine revealed that many published findings were based on misinterpretation: small samples, p-hacking (testing many hypotheses and reporting only the significant ones), and confusing correlation with causation.

The critical thinking skills in this card — hedging conclusions, acknowledging limitations, insisting on context — are the foundation of scientific literacy. A student who truly internalises "correlation does not imply causation" and "a small sample limits generalisability" is better equipped than many published researchers who forgot these basics.
