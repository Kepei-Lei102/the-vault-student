# Layers 图层

> *The moon is perfect. The trees are perfect. Now move through the trees without making the moon grow. A surprising amount of visual history is hidden inside that request.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Louis Daguerre (1787–1851)** — gives a photographic process his name; the subject must cooperate with its clock.
- **Oscar Rejlander (1813–1875)** — assembles a photograph from more than thirty negatives, before anybody has a computer to blame.
- **Eadweard Muybridge (1830–1904)** — makes a galloping horse hold still in pieces; his scientific achievement does not excuse his killing of a man.
- **Georges Méliès (1861–1938)** — a magician who discovers that a camera can be another kind of stage machinery.
- **Lotte Reiniger (1899–1981)** — builds worlds with scissors, jointed silhouettes and light.
- **Ub Iwerks (1901–1971) and Bill Garity (1899–1971)** — engineers who give separately painted planes separately controllable movement.
- **Thomas Knoll (born 1960) and John Knoll (born 1962)** — a programmer and a visual-effects artist; brothers with a darkroom in their childhood.

## Act I — The world must sit still (1839 →)

Before the photographer could rearrange the world, the world had to stop moving long enough to become a photograph.

A daguerreotype is an image on a polished, silver-coated copper plate. The early process demanded exposures of roughly three to fifteen minutes; improvements in chemistry and lenses soon pushed them below a minute. That difference is the distance between an experimental marvel and a portrait business. The customer's face is part of the apparatus: if it moves during the exposure, the plate accumulates the movement too. [Library of Congress: the medium](https://www.loc.gov/collections/daguerreotypes/articles-and-essays/the-daguerreotype-medium/)

Try holding a delighted expression while somebody slowly counts to sixty. The delight begins to look like a dental emergency.

But do not turn that joke into a universal explanation of solemn Victorian faces. Exposure times changed; habits of formal portraiture mattered too. The safe claim is narrower and more interesting: **a recording technology can require a particular kind of performance from the person it records.** The plate does not merely document the pose. Its limitations help choose the pose.

In October or November 1839, Robert Cornelius stood outside his family's Philadelphia business and made the surviving self-portrait now held by the Library of Congress. His hair is untidy; he looks back at the apparatus. It is an appealing beginning for the photographic self-portrait: a person finding out whether he can make himself remain on a surface after he has walked away. [The Cornelius portrait](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2004664436/)

The result is wonderfully detailed. It is also stubborn. Whatever shares that exposure shares that plate. You cannot ask the finished object to move the sitter three centimetres to the left and preserve the wall behind him.

That sounds like an inconvenience. For the next century and a half, it will be a business opportunity.

## Act II — A photograph of something that never happened (1857)

Oscar Rejlander wanted photography to make the kind of elaborate allegory painters made.

His *The Two Ways of Life* offers a theatrical choice between vice and virtue: a crowd of bodies, gestures and moral alternatives spread across one great composition. Getting all its parts into one satisfactory exposure was not his solution. He photographed figures and background sections separately, then combined more than thirty negatives into a single print. [Princeton University Art Museum](https://artmuseum.princeton.edu/art/collections/objects/18132)

Each component could be prepared on its own. The final picture made them appear to inhabit one space.

This is **combination printing**: expose selected portions from different negatives onto one photographic print, controlling which regions receive each contribution. Think of a darkroom as a very patient compositor. A finished picture need not correspond to a single moment in front of a single lens.

There is a sharp joke in the reception. Rejlander was trying to promote photography into high art by borrowing the language of painting. Yet photographed nudity provoked controversy despite its familiarity in paintings. The camera's apparent closeness to reality made the same subject feel different. [Princeton's account of the controversy](https://artmuseum.princeton.edu/art/collections/objects/18132)

The new medium inherited a credibility its maker was already learning to exploit.

**Photography did not spend a century innocent and then fall into sin when Photoshop arrived.** Constructed photographs belong near the beginning of its history. What later tools change is the cost of construction, the ease of revision and the number of people allowed to try.

## Act III — The horse refuses the painting (1878)

Leland Stanford wanted an answer about a horse's gait. Muybridge had to make something the eye could not comfortably inspect available for inspection.

The solution was a row of cameras: divide a rapid event into successive exposures. In 1878, twelve cameras captured a running horse with all four hooves off the ground. The suspended phase did not look like the familiar stretched-out flying horse: the hooves came under the body. The eye had been supplied with something it could stop and argue with. [Smithsonian: the motion studies](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/photographer-who-captured-humans-and-animals-motion)

The Library of Congress holds the *Sallie Gardner* sequence carrying the date 19 June 1878. The separate pictures are the important thing. You can point to one. Compare it with its neighbour. Reorder them, or display them successively to make motion visible again. [The original photographic sequence](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/97502309/)

This is not yet a layer stack. It is a related liberation: **time has become a set of addressable pieces**. Film will make that operation routine, and animation will reverse it—make the pieces first, then manufacture the motion.

Before turning Muybridge into an uncomplicated hero, keep the other event in view. In 1874 he shot and killed Harry Larkyns, who was involved with his wife, Flora. A jury acquitted him in 1875 on a justification that accepted the killing. It was not evidence that he had not done it. The photographs deserve attention; the violence deserves plain naming, not a charming aside about an eccentric genius. [UCR Arts: Muybridge's history](https://ucrarts.ucr.edu/california-stories/161-muybridge/)

The scientific achievement remains a lovely reversal. A machine humans built to preserve appearances showed humans that a familiar appearance was wrong.

## Act IV — The magician employs himself seven times (1896–1900)

A camera that can leave out time can perform a substitution. Stop it. Change something. Start it again. Remove the interruption from what the audience sees, and an ordinary replacement becomes an extraordinary transformation.

Méliès later told the perfect origin story: his camera jammed while he filmed traffic near the Paris Opéra; once it ran again, the resulting sequence made an omnibus appear to become a hearse. **It is his retrospective account, not a securely established certificate of invention.** Darragh O’Donoghue’s historical account calls the revelation possibly apocryphal. The value of the story is the decision it captures: a malfunction suggests a repeatable trick. [Senses of Cinema: Méliès](https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2004/great-directors/melies/)

Nor did cinema have to wait for him to discover substitution. The Edison company's *The Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots* used an actor-to-dummy replacement in 1895. There is no need to award Méliès every first in order to recognise his imagination. [Library of Congress: the 1895 film](https://www.loc.gov/item/00694120/)

The next freedom is stranger. Instead of putting different events *after* one another, put separately photographed performances **beside one another inside the same frame**.

A simple way to understand the trick is to cover part of the image during an exposure, rewind, then expose a different part while shielding what has already been recorded. These covers are **mattes**. The camera must stay registered: a slight shift can betray the join. The performance must also fit the space assigned to it. This is composition by constraint, not a button that makes seven people materialise.

In *L'Homme-orchestre*—*The One-Man Band* (1900)—Méliès multiplies into seven musicians. One performer becomes the ensemble. The Cinémathèque québécoise aptly treats the film as a metaphorical self-portrait: he was already doing an extraordinary number of jobs in his own productions. [Cinémathèque québécoise](https://www.cinematheque.qc.ca/fr/publications/no10-propos-sur-les-vues-animees/)

![[layers-melies-comic.png|900]]

*An imagined comic inspired by the film; the captions are a joke, not quotations or a reconstruction of its shots.*

The method makes a delicious demand on the actor. He has to play with somebody who is not there yet—or who was himself a few minutes ago. Leave room. Keep the timing. Do not casually walk through the region reserved for your other self.

Méliès's theatre has acquired an invisible backstage inside the image.

There is a cost to this magic. In-camera exposure is committed to a physical strip of film. The separately performed parts are not automatically preserved as an editable project. A later mistake can spoil earlier work. **Separating the act of making is not yet the same as preserving the right to revise.**

## Act V — The girl with the scissors, and the moon that should not grow (1926–1937)

Lotte Reiniger's childhood fascination with silhouettes and fairy tales became her adult working method. She cut figures, articulated their joints, moved them a little, photographed them, then moved them again. In *The Adventures of Prince Achmed* (1926), that patient work becomes a feature-length world. Its survival makes it the oldest surviving animated feature, not the first animated feature ever made. [Library of Congress: Reiniger](https://blogs.loc.gov/inside_adams/2025/04/lotte-reiniger/)

Paper-cutting was not a childish interest she had to outgrow before becoming an artist. It was the instrument she learned to play with greater and greater precision.

Reiniger and her collaborators used layers of glass to organise the image in depth. The frame could contain distinct planes rather than one inseparable surface. Their work belongs in the history of the multiplane camera before Disney's famous machine. [ACMI: *Prince Achmed*](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/100855--die-abenteuer-des-prinzen-achmed-the-adventures-of-prince-achmed/)

Now return to the moon and the trees.

Imagine painting both on one sheet. Bring the camera closer. Everything grows together: trunk, branch, moon. But a walk through a wood should make nearby branches sweep past while the distant moon barely changes. Your painting has coupled two things the shot needs to control separately.

Put the moon on a distant plane, trees on a nearer one, and foreground leaves nearer still. Move the planes by different amounts. **Parallax**—the different apparent movement of objects at different distances—becomes a set of mechanical instructions. The depth is staged with flat artwork; the camera records the combined view. [Walt Disney Family Museum: how the camera works](https://www.waltdisney.org/sites/default/files/030114_MultiplaneGuideFINAL.pdf)

Ub Iwerks built his own multiplane apparatus in 1933, after leaving Disney to run his own studio; its construction used mechanisms from a Chevrolet. Bill Garity developed the Disney studio's celebrated system, used in *The Old Mill* (1937). The familiar corporate name covers a history of artists, engineers and earlier experiments. [The museum's inventor histories](https://www.waltdisney.org/sites/default/files/030114_MultiplaneGuideFINAL.pdf)

And the machine did not remove the labour. It organised it.

For *Pinocchio* (1940), Garity developed an expanded horizontal arrangement for the elaborate village camera move. The museum's account describes a whole-room apparatus and the work of dozens of people. The audience sees a camera wandering through a town. The studio sees many separately prepared things that must behave as one. [Walt Disney Family Museum: *Pinocchio*](https://www.waltdisney.org/blog/machine-imagination-walt-disneys-pinocchio-and-multiplane-camera)

That is the peculiar triumph of a good composite: the more effort spent coordinating its parts, the less the audience notices the parts exist.

## Act VI — Two brothers bring the darkroom upstairs (1987–1994)

Thomas Knoll remembers receiving a camera at about eleven and learning film development in the family basement darkroom. Programming became another interest. Years later, as a graduate student working on computer vision, he wrote image-processing utilities for his Macintosh. His brother John, working in visual effects at Industrial Light & Magic, wanted the utilities gathered into one usable application.

Thomas recalls John’s excitement at converting film into editable numbers: **“We’d have complete power.”** It is a brother’s remembered remark, not a technical promise.

The childhood darkroom returned as an engineering resource. Adjusting digital tones reminded Thomas of changing chemistry, paper and controls under the enlarger. The past was not discarded; its operations were translated. [Thomas Knoll's first-person account](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2015/02/18/photoshop-turns-25-qa-with-thomas-knoll)

The two brothers supplied different kinds of knowledge. Thomas could build the operation. John could recognise what an image-maker would want to do with it. That is more useful than the romantic picture of a programmer receiving one complete idea in a flash.

Their program, initially called *Display*, grew into Photoshop. A scanner company sold an early version with its equipment; Adobe's Russell Brown helped champion the software, and Adobe shipped Photoshop 1.0 in 1990. **Layers arrived in Photoshop 3.0 in 1994**, not in the original release. [Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/makesoftware/exhibit/photoshop/), [Adobe's timeline](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2025/02/19/celebrating-35-years-of-creativity-community-innovation-with-adobe-photoshop)

This was not the invention of compositing, or of keeping artwork on separate planes. It brought a very powerful form of editable composition to the desktop.

Consider a poster: a person, a city behind them, a title across the top. If you paint everything into one bitmap, moving the person means deciding what replaces the pixels left behind. The computer cannot reveal the original city from those pixels merely because you regret covering it.

Keep the person and city separately, however, and moving the person reveals the city layer that was there all along. Keep the title as editable text, and a spelling correction need not become a painting job.

A **layer mask** adds another separation: the image data can remain intact while a separate map controls which parts show. Hiding a sleeve with a mask need not delete the sleeve. Paint the mask differently and the sleeve returns. Adjustment layers similarly preserve colour changes as adjustable operations rather than requiring direct replacement of the original pixels. [Adobe: nondestructive editing](https://helpx.adobe.com/ie/photoshop/using/nondestructive-editing.html)

The important asset is no longer only the finished picture. It is the **working arrangement that can make several finished pictures**.

You still have to preserve that arrangement. Saving a layered project and exporting a flattened image are different actions. A JPEG of the poster is not a backup of its editable text, hidden city or masks. And layers do not make every operation reversible: you can still erase pixels destructively or discard the originals.

The new freedom is conditional, but enormous. Rejlander could combine separately made parts. The desktop project can keep those parts available for the next request.

## Act VII — “Can we just change the background?”

Here is where this history earns its keep in an ordinary afternoon.

You make a school-concert poster. The singer looks good, the date is right, the background is blue. Somebody wants green. Then a square version for a group chat. Then the singer on the other side so the title has room.

If the parts remain separate, these are changes to an arrangement. If all you have is the flattened output, several become reconstruction problems. The deadline has not changed. The way you stored the work decides whether the request is trivial or miserable.

The same distinction appears in video: a subtitle can remain separate from footage, and a foreground performance can be combined with a background filmed elsewhere. Effects artists may organise operations in **node graphs** rather than a vertical layer list. The interface changes; the useful question remains: *which parts must stay independently controllable?*

This is the image-side partner of [[One Take, Many Tracks]]. A recorded voice can be moved relative to the drums; a photographed person can be moved relative to a city. Both freedoms depend on keeping useful parts apart until you choose how to combine them.

But the histories do not form one straight ladder. An exposure mask, a pane of painted glass and a software layer are different mechanisms. They solve overlapping problems, and earlier techniques keep working beside later ones. A puppet has not become obsolete because a polygon exists.

The consequences for evidence are equally concrete. Rejlander's assembly announced itself as art. A composite presented as an unaltered record of an event makes a different claim. The problem is not that editing necessarily lies; it is that a convincing image does not tell you which operations produced it.

You need the source, the context and, where the claim requires it, the original material—not merely an absence of visible seams. [[Ethics and Ownership]] supplies the wider question of what creators owe the people who trust their work.

The moon can stay exactly where you want it now.

Remember that somebody wanted it there.

## Hands-on — Keep the right to change your mind

**No software needed:** draw a sky on paper. Put a person on one transparent sheet or clear document sleeve and a tree on another. Stack them over the sky. Move the person without moving the tree; swap the upper sheets and watch the overlap change. Slide the nearby tree farther than the distant scenery to suggest camera movement. You have given different parts different controls.

**In an image editor with layers and masks:**

1. Use your own photos or drawings to build a three-part poster: background, foreground subject and text.
2. Hide part of the subject with a mask. Restore it by editing the mask. Compare that with erasing pixels on a duplicate.
3. Save the editable project. Export a flattened copy.
4. Close and reopen both. Try changing only the wording, then revealing what was behind the subject.
5. Explain which information each file retained. The visual result can match while the available next actions differ completely.

The success test is not whether the poster looks professional. It is whether you can make a new decision **without reconstructing work you already did**.

## Cultural ripples

- **The vocabulary remembers the workshop:** cutting, splicing, mattes, masks, layers, dodge and burn. Digital tools inherit operations from scissors, opaque covers, glass and controlled exposure.
- **The project becomes part of the artwork's future.** A finished export may preserve what the audience saw while losing what another maker needs to revise it.
- **Credit hides behind the composite.** The smooth camera move does not announce the engineer; the company logo does not name every inventor; the finished frame conceals separate performances and labour.
- **Reality and construction were neighbours from the beginning.** The useful distinction is the claim an image makes and the evidence supporting that claim.
