# The Bernoulli Family 伯努利家族

> *"Eadem mutata resurgo."* — *"Although changed, I rise again the same."* (Jacob Bernoulli's epitaph for the logarithmic spiral, 1705. The stonemason carved an Archimedean spiral instead.)
>
> *Eight mathematicians, three generations, two centuries. A family in which mathematics ran like a hereditary illness, transmitted from father to son and brother to brother — alongside, in equal measure, a hereditary capacity for spectacular feuds about who deserved the credit.*

![[bernoulli-family-tree.svg|697]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Niklaus Bernoulli the Elder** (1623–1708) — Basel spice merchant, patriarch. Forbade his sons from doing mathematics. They did it anyway.
- **Jacob Bernoulli** (1654–1705) — eldest son, the founder. Studied theology to please his father, mathematics to please himself. First to prove $\lim_{n \to \infty}(1 + 1/n)^n$ exists (1683), gave us the **Bernoulli numbers** and **Bernoulli's inequality**, made the *logarithmic spiral* his lifelong obsession, founded **probability theory** in *Ars Conjectandi* (posthumous 1713).
- **Johann Bernoulli** (1667–1748) — Jacob's younger brother. Taught by Jacob, then surpassed him, then spent twenty years in print warfare with him. Threw the **brachistochrone challenge** at Newton in 1696 and got humiliated in twelve hours. Sold his theorems to a French marquis under the name **L'Hôpital**. Father of Daniel; teacher of Euler.
- **Niklaus I Bernoulli** (1687–1759) — Jacob's nephew, the family fixer. Edited *Ars Conjectandi* posthumously. Posed the **St. Petersburg Paradox** to Daniel in 1713. Quiet but real.
- **Niklaus II Bernoulli** (1695–1726) — Johann's eldest son. Recruited to St. Petersburg by Catherine I in 1725. Died of fever there eight months later. He was thirty-one.
- **Daniel Bernoulli** (1700–1782) — Johann's middle son, the polymath. *Hydrodynamica* (1738), the **Bernoulli equation** of fluid mechanics, eight Paris Academy prizes, founder of statistical mechanics. Won the 1734 prize *jointly with his father* and got kicked out of the house.
- **Johann II Bernoulli** (1710–1790) — Johann's youngest son, less famous.
- **Johann III Bernoulli** (1744–1807) — Johann II's son, astronomer. Berlin Royal Astronomer at 19.
- **Jacob II Bernoulli** (1759–1789) — Johann II's son. Drowned in the Neva river at age thirty.
- **Guillaume François Antoine, Marquis de l'Hôpital** (1661–1704) — French nobleman, mediocre mathematician, very rich. Paid Johann a salary in 1694 to publish Bernoulli's results under his own name. The first calculus textbook (1696) was Bernoulli's work with L'Hôpital's name on it.
- **Catherine I of Russia** (1684–1727) — Peter the Great's widow. Founded the St. Petersburg Academy 1724 and recruited the Bernoullis to staff it.
- **Leonhard Euler** (1707–1783) — Johann's greatest student. Sent to St. Petersburg in 1727 as Daniel's replacement. Stayed fourteen years. The story arc you already met in [[Stories/The Hidden Number]].

## 中文锚点

**伯努利家族**（bónǔlì jiāzú）：从 1654 到 1789 年，一个 Basel（瑞士巴塞尔）的家族产出了**八位重量级数学家**。横跨三代人，跨越 135 年。

家族母线（Jacob → Johann → Daniel）的故事尤其精彩：

- **第一代**：父亲 Niklaus 是商人，禁止儿子学数学。Jacob 偷偷自学，后来教弟弟 Johann。
- **第二代**：兄弟 Jacob 与 Johann，本来是亲密合作者，后来在公开期刊上互相攻击二十年。1696 年 Johann 向牛顿挑战 brachistochrone 问题（最速降线），被牛顿一夜解出。1694 年 Johann 把自己的定理卖给法国侯爵 L'Hôpital，于是"洛必达法则"其实是 Bernoulli 法则。
- **第三代**：Johann 的儿子 Daniel 写了《流体动力学》(1738)，**伯努利方程** $P + \tfrac12 \rho v^2 + \rho gh = \text{const}$ 是现代流体力学的基础。**父亲 Johann 嫉妒儿子的成就**：1734 年 Daniel 跟父亲共享巴黎奖时，Johann 把他赶出家门。1739 年 Johann 出版《流体力学》（*Hydraulica*）并**伪造日期为 1732 年**，企图压过 Daniel 的优先权。

故事的精神：**数学天才常常不是孤立现象，而是家族传承现象**。比 Bach 家族（音乐）还要密集，比 Curie 家族（物理）更早。但天才的传承也带来了天才的内斗。

- [[Stories/The Hidden Number|《隐藏的数》]]：Jacob 1683 年算 $\lim_n(1+1/n)^n$ 那一幕，就是 $e$ 故事的第四幕。
- [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute|《微积分优先权之争》]]：兄弟俩是 Leibniz 阵营在牛顿争议中的主力。Brachistochrone 战役是这场战争的重要一仗。
- [[Stories/The Argument for i|《i 的辩护词》]]：18 世纪复分析的形成有 Bernoulli 家族的贡献，特别是在级数和复变函数发展中的早期工作。

## Act I — Basel, 1670s: The Forbidden Mathematics

The Bernoullis came from money. Their grandfather had fled Antwerp during the 1583 Spanish Fury, settling in Basel with the silver and the spice contracts intact. By the 1650s the family was Swiss patrician — wealthy, Calvinist, respectable. Mathematics was not in the household plan.

**Niklaus the Elder** wanted his sons to enter respectable professions. Jacob, the eldest, was to be a Protestant minister. Johann, the youngest of the surviving children, was to take over the family spice trade.

Jacob obeyed his father's nominal wish — he enrolled in theology at the University of Basel, took his master's degree in 1671, and was on track for ordination — while secretly teaching himself mathematics from Descartes's *Géométrie* and the new Leibniz calculus papers in his spare time. By the time he completed the theology training, he had already read all the available mathematical literature in Europe, learned French and Italian to read more, and was conducting an active correspondence with mathematicians twice his age. His thesis was on the comet of 1680.

He never preached a sermon. By 1683 he was lecturing on mechanics at Basel, and in 1687 the chair of mathematics opened. He took it.

Meanwhile, twelve-year-old **Johann** had been watching all of this with rapt attention. When his father tried to send him to commercial apprenticeship, Johann refused. He would instead study medicine. (Medicine, in 17th-century Basel, was the academic discipline most permissive of mathematical work — more so than theology, which was a fight; more so than law, which was rigid.) Johann took his medical degree in 1690 with a thesis on the action of muscles, full of mathematical analysis of fermentation.

Jacob, eight years his senior, became Johann's tutor in mathematics. They studied Leibniz's recently-published *Nova Methodus* (1684) — the first paper introducing differential calculus to the wider European audience — together. Within five years they had jointly mastered the new calculus more thoroughly than its inventor. *(That date matters: see [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]] for the parallel story of why Newton, who had developed equivalent methods in 1665–66, watched Leibniz publish first and would spend the next forty years trying to undo it. The Bernoullis became Leibniz's most fluent disciples — and would later be drawn into the priority war on Leibniz's side.)*

For about a decade, Basel had two of the most active calculus practitioners in Europe, and they happened to be brothers, working in close collaboration, exchanging letters and ideas almost daily. They published together. They learned together. *They were, briefly, a unit.*

This was the calm before the storm.

## Act II — The Brothers' Rise (1683–1696)

### Jacob and the constant that wasn't named

In 1683, working on the limit of compound interest at frequency $n \to \infty$, Jacob proved that

$$\lim_{n \to \infty}\!\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{n}\right)^n$$

exists and lies between 2 and 3. He didn't compute it precisely; he didn't connect it to Saint-Vincent's hyperbolic-area constant; he didn't name it. (In 1727 Euler would write $e$ for it; in 1748 Euler would tie all the threads together.) Jacob just proved the limit was finite. *That* was the first mathematical milestone.

This is the moment from [[Stories/The Hidden Number]]'s Act IV. Jacob died in 1705 not knowing his number was the same number Saint-Vincent had found in hyperbolic-logarithm work, the same number Mercator had named "natural" in 1668, and the same number Leibniz wrote as "$b$" in private letters. Five paths to one constant. Jacob walked one of them.

### The L'Hôpital paid contract (1694)

In autumn 1691, Johann travelled to Paris on a kind of mathematical concert tour. He gave private lectures on the new differential calculus to Parisian aristocrats and academicians, who hadn't yet caught up with what the brothers in Basel had been doing.

His most enthusiastic student was **Guillaume François Antoine, Marquis de l'Hôpital** — a 30-year-old French nobleman with a passion for mathematics, considerable wealth, and (this is the load-bearing detail) very limited talent. L'Hôpital paid Johann handsomely for private tutoring throughout 1691–1692.

In 1694, after Johann had returned to Basel, L'Hôpital made him an offer that was, even by 17th-century standards, *unusual*. The letter survives:

![[the-bernoulli-family-lhopital-letter.png|620]]

> *"I shall give you with pleasure a pension of three hundred livres, beginning with the first day of January of this year... I am not so unreasonable as to demand all your time for this; but I shall ask you to give me at intervals some hours of your time, to work on what I shall ask you, and also to communicate to me your discoveries, asking you not to mention any of them to others. I beg you also to send me neither M. de Varignon nor anyone else any copies of those writings you have already given me; if they should be published, that would not please me at all. Reply to me regarding all this..."*
>
> — L'Hôpital to Johann Bernoulli, March 17, 1694.

Translated: *I'll pay you to give me your discoveries privately, and you must not share them with anyone else, and I'll publish them under my name.*

Johann accepted. He needed the money. (He was 27, recently married, no academic position.) For two years he sent L'Hôpital his current research by post — including the rule that for a $0/0$ limit, you can take derivatives of numerator and denominator until the indeterminacy resolves. (See [[L'Hôpital's Rule]] for the math.)

In 1696, L'Hôpital published *Analyse des infiniment petits pour l'intelligence des lignes courbes* — **the first calculus textbook in any language**. It contained, alongside other Bernoulli material, the rule we now know as L'Hôpital's Rule. The preface offered a ceremonial nod to "Mr. Bernoulli the younger" but credited no specific results.

The book was a sensation. It dominated calculus pedagogy in Europe for fifty years. L'Hôpital's name became synonymous with the rule. Bernoulli's name was forgotten.

After L'Hôpital's death in 1704, Johann began hinting in correspondence that he had written the actual content of the textbook. Nobody believed him. By the 1740s he was openly furious in published papers. In 1742, in his collected works, he published a complete catalogue of "his own theorems that L'Hôpital had used." The community shrugged. The rule was already known by L'Hôpital's name and would stay that way.

**Vindication came in 1922** — 228 years after the contract, 218 years after L'Hôpital's death — when Bernoulli's notebooks were rediscovered, including correspondence with L'Hôpital that confirmed every claim. By then it was much too late.

The rule is still called L'Hôpital's. The textbooks still teach it that way. *That's how mathematical credit works in practice.* The story is filed under "cautionary tale" in [[L'Hôpital's Rule]] and is the inaugural example of what mathematicians now call **the Stigler's Law of Eponymy**: *no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer.*

### The brachistochrone challenge (1696)

In June 1696, Johann published in the *Acta Eruditorum* a public mathematical challenge:

> *"I, Johann Bernoulli, address the most brilliant mathematicians in the world. Nothing is more attractive to intelligent people than an honest, challenging problem, whose possible solution will bestow fame and remain as a lasting monument. Following the example set by Pascal, Fermat, etc., I hope to gain the gratitude of the whole scientific community by placing before the finest mathematicians of our time a problem which will test their methods and the strength of their intellect..."*
>
> *Given two points A and B in a vertical plane, what is the curve traced out by a point acted on only by gravity, which starts at A and reaches B in the shortest time?*

He set a deadline of six months: end of 1696. He wanted to embarrass the British, particularly Newton, whom he and his Leibnizian friends regarded as a failed challenger to Leibniz's calculus.

After six months, only one solution had arrived: from Leibniz. Johann extended the deadline by a year and re-published the challenge with a personal note explicitly directing it at "the acutest mathematicians who now flourish on this earth."

In January 1697, Newton was 54 and serving as Warden of the Royal Mint. He came home from work one evening to find Johann's challenge waiting in the post. By his niece's account, he sat down at four in the afternoon and didn't get up until four the next morning. He had the solution. He sent it back to Johann anonymously, by way of the Royal Society.

Johann opened the letter. Read the solution. Recognised the handwriting *and* the elegance of the method. Wrote back to a friend the famous Latin sentence:

> ***Tanquam ex ungue leonem.***
>
> *(I recognise the lion by its paw.)*

![[the-bernoulli-family-lions-paw.png|520]]

Five solutions arrived altogether: Newton's, Leibniz's, Jacob's, Johann's own, and L'Hôpital's (probably with Johann's help). All five solutions converged on the same curve: the **cycloid** — the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling circle.

![[brachistochrone-race.svg|697]]
*The race, simulated. Three balls released from rest at the same height, sliding under gravity along three curves to the same endpoint. The straight line is the **shortest path**; the cycloid is the **longest path**; and the cycloid arrives first. Bernoulli's challenge in one picture.*

Johann's solution used Fermat's principle of least time and a refraction analogy from optics. Jacob's solution invented an entirely new branch of mathematics: **the calculus of variations**. (When you're solving a problem that asks "find the curve that minimises *something*," you've discovered variational calculus. Jacob got there first; Euler systematised it 1733; Lagrange formalised it 1755.)

The brachistochrone challenge went down in history as one of the most important problems in the history of mathematics. It also went down as the moment when Newton, who had been keeping his calculus quiet for decades, demonstrated unmistakable mastery of the new differential techniques. *He was awake all night to do it. He won.*

## Act III — The Brothers' Fall (1697–1705)

The brachistochrone is also where the brothers' partnership broke.

Both Jacob and Johann had solved it. Both wanted credit. Jacob's calculus-of-variations approach was deeper; Johann's optical approach was cleverer. Each thought his own solution was the better one. Each spent the next two years arguing in print that the other's was inferior.

The 1697 issue of *Acta Eruditorum* is, in retrospect, the moment the brothers stopped being a unit. Jacob's article was titled (politely) *"Solution of the problems posed by my brother."* Johann's reply article was titled (less politely) *"Curvature of a beam against my brother's analysis."* This was the opening volley.

For the next eight years, they fought in print over priority, methods, generalisations, who-taught-whom, and whether Johann was capable of original work or merely Jacob's plagiarist. The journals at the time published these exchanges enthusiastically — they sold copies. Mathematicians across Europe took sides. **Leibniz, embarrassed at having two of his strongest disciples destroy each other in public**, tried unsuccessfully to mediate.

In 1705, after eight years of feud, Jacob died of tuberculosis, age 50. Johann succeeded him in the chair at Basel and would hold it for the next 43 years.

Jacob asked, in his will, that his tombstone bear a **logarithmic spiral** — the curve $r = a e^{b\theta}$, which he had loved his entire mathematical life because it has the property of remaining the same shape under any scaling, any rotation, any inversion. ("Although changed, I rise again the same.") He had written 22 papers about it. He published his proof that it is the unique self-similar curve in 1692.

The stonemason in Basel, looking at Jacob's diagram, didn't know the difference between a logarithmic spiral and an Archimedean one. He carved an **Archimedean spiral** — the simpler curve $r = a\theta$, which has none of the self-similarity properties Jacob loved.

The error is still on the tombstone in Basel Münster cloister. You can visit it. The Latin epitaph *Eadem mutata resurgo* sits next to a curve that resurges, but does not remain the same.

*Mathematical immortality, at every stage, betrayed by the people doing the actual work.*

### Posthumous *Ars Conjectandi* (1713)

Jacob had been working for over a decade on a treatise on probability — the first systematic treatment of the subject. He died with it unfinished. His nephew **Niklaus I Bernoulli** spent eight years editing the manuscript and published it in 1713 as *Ars Conjectandi* — "The Art of Conjecturing."

The book is the **founding document of mathematical probability theory**. It contains:

- The first proof of the **Law of Large Numbers** (Bernoulli's theorem): if you flip a fair coin many times, the proportion of heads converges to $\tfrac12$ in a precise sense. Jacob's proof took twenty pages and is still readable today.
- A complete theory of **combinations and permutations**, including what we now call the **Bernoulli trials** for repeated independent experiments.
- The **Bernoulli numbers** $B_n$, originally introduced for computing sums of $k$-th powers, later turning up everywhere from the Riemann zeta function to topology.

Three centuries later, *Ars Conjectandi* remains in print. Probability theory still uses Jacob's framework as its first chapter.

## Act IV — Daniel and Johann: The Cruellest Generation (1700–1782)

When Jacob died in 1705, his younger brother Johann was 38 and already a fierce competitor. By the 1720s, Johann was 60, securely the most prominent living mathematician in Europe, and increasingly bitter. The bitterness found a target in his own son.

**Daniel Bernoulli** was born in 1700, the second of Johann's three sons. From childhood, Daniel was the most talented mathematician in the family since his uncle Jacob — and Johann knew it. Johann tried to steer Daniel into business. Daniel refused. Johann tried to steer him into medicine. Daniel did the medical degree (Basel 1721) but kept doing mathematics. By 25, Daniel had published his first mathematical work, *Exercitationes Mathematicae*, which solved problems in differential equations and probability that Johann himself had not been able to solve.

In 1725, Daniel and his elder brother **Niklaus II** were both invited to the new **St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences** by Catherine I — the dowager empress of Russia, Peter the Great's widow, who was building Russia's first scientific institution. They sailed together. (Niklaus II would die there of fever in 1726, eight months after arrival, age 31. Daniel never quite recovered.)

Daniel stayed in St. Petersburg for eight years. During that time he did the work that founded modern fluid dynamics, kinetic theory, and statistical mechanics — *Hydrodynamica*. Wrote it in St. Petersburg 1725–1733; published Strasbourg 1738.

The book contains the **Bernoulli equation**:

$$P + \dfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant along a streamline}.$$

This single equation explains why airplanes fly (lift on a wing), why a curveball curves (Magnus effect), why blood flows through narrow arteries faster than wide ones (continuity), why a shower curtain billows inward in a hot shower (the moving water entrains air, lowering pressure outside the curtain). Modern aeronautical engineering, hydraulics, weather modelling, and arterial blood-flow analysis all start with this equation.

It also, separately, contains the **kinetic theory of gases** in roughly the form it would have a hundred years later — Daniel modelled gas pressure as the result of molecular collisions, anticipating Maxwell and Boltzmann by 120 years.

He wrote it while watching his uncle Jacob's Basel chair pass to his father.

### The 1734 Paris Academy prize

In 1734 the Paris Academy posed the prize question: explain the inclination of the planetary orbits. Daniel submitted an answer. *His father Johann also submitted an answer.* The judges decided to split the prize — both Bernoullis had submitted solid work. They were declared joint winners.

Johann was *furious* at having to share with his son. According to multiple accounts:

> *"He kicked Daniel out of the house."*

The breach never fully healed. Daniel returned to Basel in 1733 and took the chair of medicine (Johann still held the mathematics chair). Father and son lived in the same small Swiss city for the remaining 15 years of Johann's life and corresponded mainly through Euler.

### The *Hydraulica* forgery (1739)

The cruellest move came after Daniel's *Hydrodynamica* appeared in 1738.

In 1739 — just months later — Johann published a book called **Hydraulica**. The book covered substantially the same fluid-dynamics material as Daniel's, with substantially the same equations, framed in Johann's own notation.

*Johann backdated the book to 1732* — claiming, in the preface, that he had written it before Daniel's. The forgery was that of an old man trying to claim priority over his son's life work.

Modern analysis of letters and surviving manuscripts has confirmed: the *Hydraulica* was written 1738–1739, after Daniel's book had been in print for months. The 1732 date was false. Johann was 71. He carried out a full-scale academic forgery to claim priority over his son.

Daniel never recovered emotionally. He wrote letters to Euler describing the experience as if it were a betrayal by a stranger. He continued his work — won the Paris prize **eight more times**, for problems on tides, magnetism, ocean currents, ship stability, and elastic bodies — but his correspondence is permeated with a quiet melancholy about his father.

When Johann died in 1748, age 80, Daniel attended the funeral but seems not to have written about it. He outlived his father by 34 years and died at Basel in 1782, the most-decorated mathematician of his generation, a quiet bachelor.

## Act V — St Petersburg, Euler, and the Dynasty's Persistence (1727–1789)

When Daniel left St Petersburg in 1733, the Academy needed a replacement. Daniel recommended his **closest friend** in mathematics — a 20-year-old Basel graduate named **Leonhard Euler**.

The Euler-Daniel friendship is one of the great friendships in the history of mathematics. Euler had been **Johann's most diligent student** at Basel from age 14 — but Johann was 60 by then and had thirty years of accumulated bitterness; Daniel was Euler's age and could engage as a peer rather than a deity. They studied together in Basel through Euler's late teens. When Daniel went to St Petersburg in 1725, Euler stayed in correspondence; when the Academy job opened, Daniel pulled the strings to bring his friend over. Euler arrived in 1727. They worked side-by-side for six years until Daniel left in 1733, then continued the correspondence by post for the next half-century until Daniel's death in 1782. Surviving Euler-Daniel letters — over 200 of them — show *the warmth that's missing from Johann-Daniel correspondence*. Daniel found in Euler the intellectual companion his father refused to be.

![[the-bernoulli-family-daniel-and-euler.png|640]]

Euler stayed at St Petersburg for fourteen years, then moved to Berlin (1741), then back to St Petersburg (1766). (See [[Stories/The Hidden Number]] Acts V–VII for what he did with the time.) He became, in effect, the heir of the Bernoulli mathematical tradition — **Johann's pupil, Daniel's friend, working in the institution Daniel had helped build**. The torch had passed not to a Bernoulli son but to a Bernoulli adopted son.

The Bernoullis themselves persisted into a third generation. Johann's youngest son **Johann II** (1710–1790) was a competent if unspectacular mathematician at Basel. *His* son **Johann III** (1744–1807) became Royal Astronomer at the Berlin Academy at age 19 — that's the third generation. Johann III's brother **Jacob II Bernoulli** (1759–1789) showed real promise, won a Petersburg Academy chair at 27, and **drowned in the Neva river at age 30**. The dynasty's run of premature Russian deaths (Niklaus II 1726, Jacob II 1789) is one of the small statistical curiosities of 18th-century mathematics.

By 1800, the family's mathematical line had effectively faded. Other Bernoullis would publish in the 19th and 20th centuries — including a *Daniel Bernoulli II* who studied at Basel — but the dynasty's centre of gravity had moved to other families and other countries. The Lagrange–Cauchy–Riemann arc was beginning. The Bernoullis had handed off the torch to Euler, and Euler's torch went on to Lagrange.

But the run was extraordinary while it lasted: **eight major mathematicians from one family across three generations**, contributing the foundations of probability theory, fluid dynamics, calculus of variations, kinetic theory, and parts of analytical mechanics. Compared to the Bach family in music — which produced six generations of musicians but only one transcendent composer (J.S.) — the Bernoullis were *denser*. Multiple major contributors per generation. A statistical anomaly that the modern field of *behavioral genetics* still finds difficult to explain.

## Cultural ripples

**Bernoulli's equation in everything that flies, swims, or pumps.** Daniel's $P + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho gh = \text{const}$ is on every aerospace-engineering reference card, in every fluid-dynamics undergraduate textbook, in every cardiology-trained doctor's mental model of blood flow. Three centuries old, never superseded.

**Probability theory as a mathematical subject.** Jacob's *Ars Conjectandi* established that probability could be a *mathematical* discipline, not just a recreation for gamblers. The chain Jacob → Laplace → Pearson → Fisher → Kolmogorov → modern statistics is unbroken. Every clinical trial, every A/B test, every actuarial table starts with Jacob's Law of Large Numbers.

**Calculus of variations as a method.** Jacob's brachistochrone solution invented an entirely new branch of mathematics. Three centuries later: Lagrangian mechanics, optimal control theory, machine-learning-as-functional-optimisation, the path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics. All variational. All descended from Jacob's 1697 paper.

**The L'Hôpital rule as a cautionary fable.** Stigler's Law — *"no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer"* — has L'Hôpital's Rule as its founding example. Variants of the same injustice appear in: **Stigler's Law itself** (invented by Robert Merton, not Stigler), the **Pythagorean theorem** (known to Babylonians a millennium before Pythagoras), **Pascal's triangle** (Yang Hui in China three centuries before Pascal), the **Gaussian distribution** (de Moivre 1733, Gauss 1809). The pattern is universal. The first time anyone explicitly noticed it as a pattern was when Bernoulli got crossed off the rule that bears L'Hôpital's name.

**The brachistochrone problem as a teaching vehicle.** Every undergraduate mechanics course in the world teaches the brachistochrone in week one or two as the introduction to variational methods. The cycloid solution remains memorable, surprising, and exactly the right level of mathematical content for the entry point.

**Newton's "lion's paw" line.** *Tanquam ex ungue leonem* is the most-quoted sentence from the entire Newton-Bernoulli correspondence, and one of the most-quoted lines in the history of mathematics. It captures something essential about how mathematical mastery is recognised across centuries — by *style* and *signature*, not by signed name.

**The pattern of genius and fragile self-esteem.** Reading this card alongside [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]], an uncomfortable pattern emerges. Newton fought Leibniz over calculus, Hooke over optics and inverse-square gravitation, and Flamsteed over Greenwich Observatory data. Johann fought Jacob, then Daniel, then L'Hôpital's heirs. *Brilliance and paranoid territoriality recur together.* These are people whose self-worth was load-bearingly tied to specific intellectual achievements, and whose response to anyone they perceived as encroaching on those achievements was lifelong, public, often vindictive. **Mathematical greatness does not correlate with personal generosity.** It is one of the small disappointments of reading 17th- and 18th-century mathematical biography: the most penetrating minds of an era often spent their final decades writing attack pamphlets about who-said-what-first. The work was greater than the people, and the people knew it, and that's part of what made them the way they were.

- [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]] — the brothers were Leibniz's strongest disciples; the brachistochrone was the moment Newton publicly outranked Bernoulli. Companion drama.
- [[Stories/The Argument for i]] — 18th-century complex analysis owes Bernoulli-family preliminary work, especially in series and analytic-function development.
- [[L'Hôpital's Rule]] — the deep card on the rule. The history footnote there points here for the full story.
- [[Differentiation Rules]] — Bernoulli's role in establishing the chain and product rules in their modern form.
- [[Mean Value Theorem]] — connections to Cauchy's MVT and Jacob's earlier mean-value ideas in the *Acta Eruditorum*.
- [[Differential Equations]] — many specific ODEs (the **Bernoulli equation** as a 1st-order ODE, distinct from the fluid-dynamics one) are named after one or another Bernoulli.
- [[Probability Basics|Probability]] — *Ars Conjectandi*, the founding text.
- [[Discrete Random Variables]] — Jacob's binomial trial framework is the basis.
- [[Normal Distribution]] — distant heir; de Moivre 1733 took Jacob's framework to the limit.
- [[Heptadecagon]] — Gauss in 1796 was working at the same Petersburg Academy that hosted Daniel and then Euler. The mathematical family of European 17th–19th-century academies is small.

## Receipts

**Primary sources:**
- L'Hôpital to Johann Bernoulli, March 17, 1694 — the paid-contract letter. Reproduced in: Pierre Costabel, ed., *Bernoulli–L'Hôpital correspondence*, Bibliothèque de l'Institut, Paris.
- Johann Bernoulli, "Problema novum ad cuius solutionem Mathematici invitantur" — the brachistochrone challenge — *Acta Eruditorum*, June 1696, p. 269.
- Newton's anonymous solution and Bernoulli's *"Tanquam ex ungue leonem"* — *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, 19 (1697): 384.
- Jacob Bernoulli, *Ars Conjectandi* (Basel, 1713), edited posthumously by Niklaus I Bernoulli.
- Daniel Bernoulli, *Hydrodynamica* (Strasbourg, 1738).
- Johann Bernoulli, *Hydraulica* (Strasbourg, 1739, *backdated to 1732*).
- L'Hôpital, *Analyse des infiniment petits pour l'intelligence des lignes courbes* (Paris, 1696).
- Bernoulli's notebooks, rediscovered 1922 — confirmed L'Hôpital priority dispute. Held at the University of Basel.

**Secondary — biographical:**
- Karl Pearson, *The History of Statistics in the 17th and 18th Centuries Against the Changing Background of Intellectual, Scientific and Religious Thought*, edited by E.S. Pearson (London: Charles Griffin, 1978). Best single survey of *Ars Conjectandi*'s reception.
- C. Truesdell, *The Rational Mechanics of Flexible or Elastic Bodies, 1638–1788* (Zürich: Orell Füssli, 1960). Authoritative on Daniel and Johann's hydrodynamics dispute.
- John Henry, "Why Newton was bashful, and why we should be too: Newton, Bernoulli, and the brachistochrone" — *Annals of Science* 70 (2013): 197–217. Reconstructs the dating of Newton's solution night.
- Stephen M. Stigler, *Statistics on the Table: The History of Statistical Concepts and Methods* (Harvard, 1999), Chapter 14 — the original "Stigler's Law" essay, with the L'Hôpital story as a founding example.
- Florian Cajori, *A History of Mathematics* (Macmillan, 1893). Chapter on the Bernoullis is dated but vivid.

**Secondary — scholarly editions:**
- *The Collected Works of Johann Bernoulli*, ed. Karl Pearson, 4 vols., 1742 — Johann's own anti-L'Hôpital catalogue is here.
- *Die Werke von Jakob Bernoulli*, multiple volumes, 1969 onward, Birkhäuser. The standard scholarly edition.

**Online:**
- MacTutor History of Mathematics archive — biographies of all Bernoullis, with cross-referenced timelines and family trees. Accessed 2026-05-10.
- Wikipedia — *Bernoulli family*, *Brachistochrone curve*, *L'Hôpital's rule* — accessed 2026-05-10 for date verification.
