# The Bookbinder's Apprentice 装订工学徒

> The most consequential physicist of the nineteenth century could not do algebra. He was a blacksmith's son who left school at thirteen, learned his science from the books other people paid him to bind, and got his break by gift-wrapping a great man's own words and handing them back to him. He was made to eat with the servants on the very tour of Europe where he met the age's greatest minds. And when he finally found the thing he had hunted for ten years — magnetism turned into electricity — he drew what he could not calculate: curved lines in empty space that every mathematician of his day politely despised, and that every physicist since has lived inside. This is the story of Michael Faraday, who invented the **field** — arguably the deepest idea in physics — because he was too poor to be taught the wrong one.

## Act I — The university in the bindery (1791–1812)

Michael Faraday was born over a stable in Newington Butts, third child of a sickly blacksmith who could not always work; there was a season when the family's ration was one loaf of bread per child per week. School taught him "the rudiments of reading, writing and arithmetic" — his words — and released him at thirteen to run errands for George Riebau, a bookbinder, delivering newspapers that had to be collected back from each customer because nobody could afford to keep one.

At fourteen he was apprenticed to the binding trade, and here the story turns on its hinge: **a bookbinder's apprentice spends all day holding books.** Faraday read what he bound. An *Encyclopædia Britannica* volume came through the shop and he read its long article on electricity. Jane Marcet's *Conversations on Chemistry* came through — a book written for girls, because girls were barred from the lecture halls — and taught chemistry to a boy barred by class instead. He saved pennies for zinc discs and built his own voltaic pile on the shop bench. His brother, a blacksmith like their father, paid the one-shilling entry so Michael could attend evening science lectures at the City Philosophical Society, where tradesmen taught each other what no university would teach them.

Riebau, to his lasting credit, encouraged all of it — and would later point proudly at the shop as the place where *that* Faraday had learned. The lesson the vault refuses to let go: **his university was the reading, not the room.**

## Act II — The job application he manufactured (1812–1813)

In 1812 a customer of the shop, William Dance, gave the earnest apprentice tickets to the season's great spectacle: Sir Humphry Davy's farewell lectures at the Royal Institution. Faraday sat in the gallery and took notes so complete they were nearly transcripts. Then he did the one professionally distinctive thing he knew how to do: **he bound them** — nearly four hundred pages, with hand-drawn diagrams, in fine leather — and sent the volume to Davy with a letter asking for any job in science at all.

It is, read plainly, one of the great job applications in history: *here are your own words, sir, returned to you as a finer object than you spoke them — judge from the workmanship whether I might be useful.* Davy's reply was kind but deflating: science, he warned, was "a harsh mistress" that poorly rewarded her servants; stay in the trade. Then fate intervened twice. An explosion of nitrogen trichloride (Davy was fearless with dangerous chemistry, which is to say reckless) temporarily damaged Davy's eyes, and he remembered the young man with the beautiful handwriting, hiring him for a few days as an amanuensis. Months later the Royal Institution's laboratory assistant was dismissed for brawling, and Davy sent for the bookbinder. In March 1813, at twenty-one, Faraday became a **bottle-washer** at twenty-five shillings a week, with two rooms at the top of the building. He would live inside the Royal Institution for most of the next fifty years.

## Act III — The valet's grand tour (1813–1815)

Within months Davy swept his new assistant onto an eighteen-month scientific tour of a Europe still at war — Napoleon had granted the celebrated chemist a special passport. For young Faraday it was Cambridge, Oxford and Harvard rolled into one carriage: he watched Davy identify iodine as a new element in Paris, met Ampère, and in Italy met the elderly Volta whose pile he had once copied in pennies and zinc.

But Davy's valet had refused to travel, and Faraday was pressed into double duty: assistant *and* servant. Lady Davy enforced the distinction with relish — Faraday rode outside the carriage in the weather, and when company was present he ate with the staff. His letters home confess he came close to abandoning science entirely, and the humiliation was the sharper for the company: on the same afternoon, the same young man might discuss electrochemistry with the finest minds in Europe and then be reminded he was not fit to sit at his employer's table. He endured it. The vault notes, without sentimentality, *how* he endured it: by deciding that the science was worth more than the seating arrangement.

## Act IV — The first motor, and the rift (1821–1830)

Oersted's 1820 discovery — a current nudges a compass — set every laboratory in Europe hunting (the story [[Lorentz Force]] tells from the physics side). In 1821 Faraday found something no one else had: he made a current-carrying wire **circle endlessly around a magnet** — electromagnetic rotation, the first conversion of electric current into continuous motion, the ancestor of every motor. He was thirty, and it made his European name.

It also nearly broke him. William Wollaston had earlier speculated aloud about making a wire *spin on its own axis* — a different effect — and had tried and failed in Davy's lab. When Faraday published without a careful enough acknowledgment, whispers of plagiarism ran through London's scientific society, and Davy — his own mentor — let them run, even fed them. The wound reopened in 1824 when Faraday was proposed for the Royal Society: the president of the Society at the time was Humphry Davy, and lore records exactly **one vote against** the election. Posterity hands Davy the line that his greatest discovery "was Michael Faraday" — a quote so perfect it is almost certainly polished legend (see Receipts) — but the record of those years shows the uglier, more human truth: the mentor who opened the door could not bear watching the apprentice walk through it.

Faraday's response is the most Faraday thing in this story: he simply *left the field* for most of a decade — glass, chlorine, steel alloys, dutiful commissioned work — while a single sentence sat in his notebook, written in 1822: ***"Convert magnetism into electricity."***

## Act V — Ten days in autumn (1831)

He came back to the sentence when Davy was dead. On **29 August 1831** he wound two separate coils onto an iron ring — one wired to a battery through a switch, the other to a galvanometer, no electrical connection between them — and saw the thing that everyone hunting a *steady* effect had walked past for a decade: the needle **kicked at the instant he closed the switch, died to zero, and kicked the other way at the instant he opened it.**

![[faraday-iron-ring.svg|697]]

Steady current, steady magnetism: nothing. *Change*: everything. Within weeks he had the whole family of effects — a magnet thrust into a coil, a coil moved over a magnet, and finally a copper disc spun between magnet poles delivering *continuous* current: the first generator, the machine [[Electromagnetic Induction]] builds into every power station on Earth. Ten years of a written-down question; ten days of answers.

And then the deeper move — the one that outlived the machines. Faraday could not follow the mathematicians' formalism of forces acting at a distance across empty nothing; he had never been taught it. So he trusted what iron filings showed him and *drew*: curved **lines of force** filling the space around magnets and currents, denser where the action is stronger, physically real, pushing and tensing like elastic threads. Cambridge's mathematicians found the picture charmingly naive. The picture was the future. The modern name for that space full of structure is **the field**, and there is a serious argument that it — not the dynamo — is Faraday's greatest invention: physics since is field theory all the way down.

## Act VI — The translator, and the refusals (1845–1867)

The vindication arrived politely, from Scotland. In 1856 a Cambridge prodigy in his twenties, James Clerk Maxwell, published *On Faraday's Lines of Force*, taking the old man's drawings seriously as mathematics — and over the following decade turned them into the equations that carry his name ([[Maxwell's Equations]]), out of which light itself falls as a prediction. Faraday, over seventy, wrote to Maxwell that he was "at first almost frightened" to see such mathematical force brought to bear on his pictures — and then asked, with perfect self-awareness, whether the mathematics could not be translated back "into common language" for people like him. Maxwell's later verdict on those untutored drawings: Faraday "was in reality a mathematician of a very high order." The apprentice's pictures had been righter than the establishment's calculus.

The last act is a study in refusals, all of them rooted in his plain-living Sandemanian faith. He declined a knighthood — resolved, as the accounts have it, to remain "plain Michael Faraday to the last." He declined the presidency of the Royal Society. Twice. Asked during the Crimean War about producing poison gas for the siege of Sebastopol, he replied that it was certainly *feasible* — and that he would have nothing whatever to do with it. He gave forty years of **Christmas Lectures** for children at the Royal Institution (founded 1825, still running every December), including the most famous science lectures ever delivered, on the chemistry of a single candle. Meanwhile his memory crumbled through his final two decades — the notebooks fill with apologies for forgetting — until he could no longer lecture at all; Prince Albert gave him a grace-and-favour house at Hampton Court, and he died quietly in his study chair in 1867. He had declined, in advance, a grave in Westminster Abbey. He lies under a plain Sandemanian headstone in Highgate; the Abbey settled for a plaque near Newton.

## Honest edges

- **The Wollaston affair was a real misstep.** Faraday's 1821 paper genuinely under-acknowledged the prior speculation he knew about; he spent the rest of his career being scrupulous to a fault about credit, which reads like a man who never forgave himself.
- **Davy stays complicated.** He plucked Faraday from the bindery, taught him, carried him across Europe — and then obstructed him, fed a plagiarism whisper, and cast the one vote against him. Keep both halves; the story is worthless with either one removed.
- **The mathematical illiteracy was real,** not false modesty: essentially no algebra, ever. That the field concept came *from* this limitation — he had to see because he could not compute — is the story's deepest lesson, but it cost him too: he could not follow the theory built on his own foundations.
- **The faith cut both ways.** Sandemanian humility powered the refusals the world admires — and the sect once suspended him from eldership, by the sternest accounts for the sin of dining with the Queen when duty said Sunday meeting. He submitted to the discipline. Devotion that admirable is also that unbending.
- **The apocrypha are flagged in Receipts** — the "greatest discovery" line, and the beloved "one day you may tax it" retort, are both too good to be verifiable.

## Cultural ripples

- **The farad** — the unit every [[Capacitors]] problem is denominated in — and the **Faraday cage**, the **Faraday constant**, and the Faraday disc: a bookbinder's apprentice with more units and effects to his name than any formally trained contemporary.
- **Einstein kept three portraits** on his study wall: Newton, Maxwell — and Faraday. The field concept those three passed hand to hand is the frame of both relativity and quantum field theory.
- **The Christmas Lectures still run** every December, two centuries on — the institution that says teaching children is not what you do when you retire from real science; it *is* real science.
- **His last request of Maxwell** — that the mathematics be translated back *"into common language"* for people like him — is the standing charter of every honest teacher and every textbook worth binding: rigour that cannot be told as a story is rigour half-finished.
- **Anno 1800 players have built this story's ending:** the game's electricity age — power plants lighting a metropolis, whole industries waking up — is the industrial afterlife of the 1831 iron ring, rendered as gameplay. Every power plant you place is Faraday's spinning disc at city scale.
- He is the patron saint of every self-taught learner: the standing proof that the entry ticket to deep science is neither money nor pedigree, but appetite — and somebody, like Riebau or brother Robert, willing to hold the door.

## Receipts

- **Bread ration, errand-boy start, Riebau apprenticeship, Marcet and the Britannica, the penny voltaic pile, City Philosophical Society**: standard in the major biographies (Bence Jones's *Life and Letters*; L. Pearce Williams; James Hamilton).
- **The bound lecture notes sent to Davy (1812) and the "harsh mistress" warning**: documented; the bound volume survives in the Royal Institution's collection.
- **NCl₃ eye injury, the brawling assistant's dismissal, the March 1813 appointment at 25s/week**: Royal Institution records.
- **The valet humiliations on the 1813–15 tour**: from Faraday's own travel correspondence; Lady Davy's treatment is attested by him and by later biographers.
- **1821 rotations, the Wollaston controversy, the single vote against at the Royal Society (1824)**: the vote count is lore transmitted by early biographers — Davy's opposition is documented, the exact tally is tradition.
- **"The greatest of all my discoveries was Michael Faraday"**: attributed to Davy in Victorian retellings; no primary source — quote as legend, never as fact.
- **Notebook resolve "convert magnetism into electricity" (1822), the 29 August 1831 iron-ring entry, the make/break kicks**: Faraday's laboratory notebooks, published in facsimile.
- **Faraday–Maxwell letters (1857): "almost frightened" and the plea for common language; Maxwell's "mathematician of a very high order"**: published correspondence and Maxwell's *Treatise*-era essays.
- **Declined knighthood, declined RS presidency (twice), the Sebastopol poison-gas refusal, the Sandemanian suspension, Hampton Court, the declined Abbey burial**: standard biographies; the suspension's exact cause varies by account — the Queen-dinner version is the strongest tradition.
- **"One day, sir, you may tax it"** (to a minister asking the use of electricity): first appears decades later, secondhand — almost certainly embroidered; the sentiment, at least, was his.
