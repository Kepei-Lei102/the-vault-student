# Stigler's Law of Eponymy 斯蒂格勒命名定律

> *"No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer."*
> — **Stephen Stigler, 1980**, formulating his eponymous law.
>
> Robert K. Merton formulated the same observation about 15 years earlier, in the 1960s sociology literature on scientific reward systems. Stigler dedicated his 1980 paper *"Stigler's Law of Eponymy"* to Merton, with full self-awareness of what he was doing. **Stigler's Law is the most flawless example of Stigler's Law.**

![[stiglers-law-obeys-itself-comic.png|560]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Stephen Stigler** (b. 1941) — University of Chicago statistician, historian of statistics, son of Nobel-laureate economist George Stigler. Formulated the eponymous law as a "tongue-in-cheek tribute" to Merton, knowing the joke would land.
- **Robert K. Merton** (1910–2003) — Columbia sociologist of science; coined "self-fulfilling prophecy" and "role model"; the *real* originator of the eponymy-law observation, embedded in his 1965 book *On the Shoulders of Giants* (a 280-page tracing of Newton's "shoulders of giants" line back through 12 centuries of attributions). Pre-empted his own credit by writing about how often this happens.
- **The wronged (sampled)** — Tartaglia (whose cubic formula Cardano signed his name to), Johann Bernoulli (whose rule l'Hôpital published as his own), Yang Hui / Al-Karaji / Khayyam (whose triangle Pascal got named after), Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II (whose equation Pell never touched), Pingala (whose sequence Fibonacci re-derived 1400 years later), Bunyakovsky (whose inequality Cauchy did first, then Schwarz did again, then a hyphen rescued one of them), James Gregory (who had Taylor series 40 years before Taylor), Simon Newcomb (whose logarithm-table observation Frank Benford got named after).
- **The credited (sampled)** — Pascal, l'Hôpital, Cardano, Pell, Fibonacci, Cauchy and Schwarz, Taylor and Maclaurin, Benford, Pythagoras, Higgs.

## 中文锚点

**斯蒂格勒命名定律**：任何科学发现都不是以最早发现者的名字命名的 (Stigler, 1980)。这条定律本身也是它自己的例子——Merton 在 1960 年代就已经写过同样的观察。

这条定律不是抱怨，而是社会学观察。**一项发现归到谁名下，往往取决于：**
- 谁把它写进了流行的教科书 (Pascal, 不是 Yang Hui)
- 谁的国家有更多读者 (Pell, 不是 Brahmagupta)
- 谁第一个把它讲清楚到全世界都懂 (Maclaurin, 不是 Stirling)
- 谁还活着的时候有政治力量打压对手 (Newton vs. Leibniz)

中国数学史本身就给了一个温暖的反例：**杨辉三角** 在中文世界里就叫杨辉三角，不叫 Pascal 三角。这是少数 Stigler 定律没有得逞的情况——杨辉 1303 年的著作《详解九章算法》在中国本土有教学传承，所以中文教材没有跟着西方走。但翻开任何英文课本，它都还是 Pascal's Triangle，尽管 Yang Hui 早了 350 年、Al-Karaji 早了 500 年、Halayudha 早了 700 年。

## The Three Mechanisms

How does a discovery end up named after the wrong person? Stigler argued there isn't *one* answer — there are at least three distinct mechanisms, each well-documented across the vault's existing case studies.

### Mechanism 1 — Textbook author wins

Whoever writes the *most-read* book on the topic gets the name. The discovery is often older than that book, sometimes by centuries, but the textbook is what students learn from, and they call the discovery by whatever name appears in the textbook.

**Case A — l'Hôpital's Rule** (the cleanest example in the vault).

Johann Bernoulli, the second of the [[Stories/The Bernoulli Family|Bernoulli brothers]], discovered the rule in 1694. He signed a contract with the Marquis Guillaume François Antoine de l'Hôpital, a French nobleman who wanted to be a mathematician, exchanging mathematical results for a yearly stipend. In 1696 l'Hôpital published *Analyse des Infiniment Petits pour l'Intelligence des Lignes Courbes* — the first calculus textbook in any European language — with Bernoulli's rule appearing as one of its centrepieces. **L'Hôpital credited Bernoulli generously in the preface**, but the textbook was readable, popular, and French. Generations of European students learned the rule from it. The name stuck. When Bernoulli protested after l'Hôpital's death in 1704, the mathematical community shrugged: *"Mais c'est dans le livre de l'Hôpital."* It is in l'Hôpital's book.

The full receipt — including the 1922 discovery of the Bernoulli-to-Bernoulli correspondence that finally vindicated Johann — lives in [[L'Hôpital's Rule]] §"Why is this called l'Hôpital's Rule?".

**Case B — Pascal's Triangle.**

The arithmetical triangle that bears Blaise Pascal's name (1665 treatise) was published in China by **Yang Hui in 1303**, in Persia by **Al-Karaji around 1000**, and in India by **Halayudha around 975 CE** in his commentary on Pingala's *Chandaḥśāstra*. Omar Khayyam wrote about it around 1100. Pascal's contribution was real — he was the first to apply it systematically to combinatorial and probabilistic questions, and his treatise introduced the now-standard arithmetic-table layout. **But he wasn't the discoverer.** China keeps the historically correct name; the rest of the world goes with the more-readable book.

**Case C — Cardano's Formula.**

The cubic formula in [[Stories/The Argument for i|the Argument for i]] is named after Gerolamo Cardano, who published it in his 1545 *Ars Magna*. Cardano did not discover it. **Niccolò Tartaglia did**, in 1535, after winning a public mathematics duel against Antonio Fior. Tartaglia revealed his method to Cardano under an oath of secrecy. Cardano kept the oath for six years, then learned that an even earlier mathematician (Scipione del Ferro) had also discovered the formula thirty years before Tartaglia. Cardano decided the oath was void, published in 1545 with credit to both del Ferro and Tartaglia in the text — and the world remembered only Cardano's name on the cover. Tartaglia spent the rest of his life writing increasingly bitter polemics. The textbook won.

### Mechanism 2 — Nationality and publication venue win

Whoever publishes in the *right* language and the *right* country gets the name. The discovery may have happened centuries earlier in a different mathematical tradition, but if it didn't reach the people who write the histories, it doesn't count.

**Case D — Pell's Equation.**

The Diophantine equation $x^2 - ny^2 = 1$ was systematically solved by **Brahmagupta** in India in 628 CE (the chakravala method), perfected by **Bhāskara II** in 1150 CE. Both worked out the full theory of continued-fraction solutions a thousand years before any European touched the problem.

In the 17th century, Fermat reposed the equation as a challenge. Lord Brouncker and John Wallis worked out an English solution. Euler, reading their work in 1730 and translating it into Latin for continental Europe, **misattributed the English solution to John Pell**, an English mathematician who had nothing to do with it. *Pell never worked on this equation.* Euler was just confused about the names in the English correspondence. The misattribution stuck. Today every textbook calls it Pell's equation. Brahmagupta and Bhāskara are restored in the historiography of the last 50 years, but the name does not change.

**Case E — Heaviside Cover-Up.**

The cover-up shortcut for [[Partial Fractions]] decomposition — the trick where you "cover up" a factor and substitute the root to extract the coefficient — was used systematically by Euler, Lagrange, and others in the 18th century. **Oliver Heaviside**, the great Victorian electrical engineer, applied it heavily in his operator calculus and made it famous. His applied-mathematics audience attached his name to a technique that had been a standard piece of European calculus for a century. *English-speaking electrical engineers won the naming.* Continental European mathematics texts still don't always call it Heaviside's method — they just call it the technique.

**Case F — Newton's First Law.**

The principle that bodies in motion stay in motion was clearly stated by **Galileo** in his 1632 *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* and even earlier (in less developed form) by **Jean Buridan** in the 14th century. Newton wrote it down again as the first of his three laws in the 1687 *Principia*. Because the *Principia* swept European physics, "Newton's First Law" became the name. The full restoration of Galileo's priority is now standard in every history-of-physics textbook, but every introductory physics class still calls it Newton's First Law — *including* the [[Newton's Laws of Motion]] card in this vault.

### Mechanism 3 — Readability and the popular explanation win

The discovery existed; even the discoverer's *name* was attached to it in the technical literature. But then someone else wrote the explanation that everyone could understand, and over time the credit migrated.

**Case G — Maclaurin and Taylor Series.**

The Taylor series, named after Brook Taylor (1715), was used in essentially its modern form by **James Gregory** in 1671 and by **Newton** in his unpublished 1665 manuscripts. The Maclaurin series, named after Colin Maclaurin (1742), was already in **Stirling's 1717 *Methodus Differentialis***. In both cases the names attached to whoever's textbook made the technique *teachable*, not to the first person who actually used it. Taylor and Maclaurin were both excellent expositors; Gregory and Stirling were not. **Both eponymous series are case studies in their own Stigler corollary**: the case where the credited person did real work (Taylor's 1715 *Methodus Incrementorum* did have new theorems) but not the foundational work the name suggests.

Full treatment lives in [[Maclaurin Series]] §"Stigler's Law strikes again".

**Case H — The Fibonacci Sequence.**

The recursion $a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$ was studied systematically by **Pingala** around 200 BCE in his *Chandaḥśāstra*, a treatise on Sanskrit poetic metres. (The number of distinct $n$-syllable metres in a given long/short pattern follows the Fibonacci recurrence.) Indian mathematicians Virahanka (c. 700 CE), Gopāla (c. 1135), and Hemachandra (c. 1150) all wrote about the sequence before Leonardo of Pisa rediscovered it in his 1202 *Liber Abaci* via a rabbit-breeding problem. Leonardo (nicknamed "Fibonacci," meaning *son of Bonacci*) wrote in Latin and addressed a European audience; Pingala wrote in Sanskrit. The European name won.

**Case I — Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality** (and the missing Bunyakovsky).

The inequality $\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \leq \lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert$ was proven by **Augustin-Louis Cauchy** in 1821 for finite sums. **Viktor Bunyakovsky** extended it to integrals in 1859. **Hermann Schwarz** independently rediscovered the integral case in 1888, ignorant of Bunyakovsky's work because Russian mathematics was not yet on most Western readers' radar. The English-language name **Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality** credits Cauchy (correctly) and Schwarz (the later European rediscoverer) but skips Bunyakovsky entirely. The Russian-language name is **Bunyakovsky's inequality**. Both names are used in different parts of the world for the same theorem.

**Case J — Benford's Law.**

The observation that the leading digit of "natural" datasets follows $\log_{10}(1 + 1/d)$ — so 1 appears as the leading digit about 30% of the time, 9 only about 4.6% — was made by **Simon Newcomb** in 1881 from worn pages of logarithm tables (the early pages, indexing numbers starting with 1, were much more thumbed than the later pages). Newcomb wrote a two-page note and moved on. **Frank Benford** rediscovered the pattern in 1938, did extensive empirical verification across 20,000 datasets, and published the full paper. Benford got the name. Newcomb is restored in the modern literature but most textbooks don't even mention him.

**Case K — The Abel–Ruffini Theorem** (and the vanished Ruffini).

That the general quintic has no solution in radicals is usually credited to **Niels Henrik Abel**, who gave a complete proof in 1824. But **Paolo Ruffini** published a proof *twenty-five years earlier*, in 1799 — over 500 pages of it. It carried one genuine gap (a lemma about "natural irrationalities" that Abel later closed) and it went almost entirely unread; Cauchy was among the very few who took it seriously. The fuller name *Abel–Ruffini* restores half the credit, but in everyday use the result is simply "Abel's theorem," and the man who saw it first is a hyphen most people skip. (Abel's own tragedy — dead at twenty-six, his masterpiece mislaid by Cauchy — is [[Stories/Abel the Other Boy Who Died Young|its own story]].) The deeper question — *which* equations are solvable, and why — belongs to a third figure the establishment was even slower to read: [[Stories/Galois at Twenty|Évariste Galois]], whose founding papers sat unread for eleven years.

## The honest count

Stigler's Law is **not** the claim that the credited person did nothing. In most cases the credited person did *real* mathematical work — sometimes very deep. The claim is more subtle: **the name reflects who explained it well or whose tradition won the naming war, not who first discovered the result.**

| Maclaurin Series | Colin Maclaurin | Stirling | Readability |

**The case where Stigler's Law fails is informative too.** In the vault: **Newton's three laws of motion as a unit** are correctly attributed to Newton — he did synthesise N1 (from Galileo) + N2 (his own contribution, F=dp/dt) + N3 (his own, action-reaction) into a single coherent system that *was* genuinely new in 1687. The *first* law alone gets the partial-credit asterisk; the *triple-laws synthesis* is genuinely Newton's. Similarly, [[Stories/Newton vs Hooke|Newton's inverse-square law of gravity]] gets the asterisk on the *idea* (Hooke had the inverse-square guess) but not on the *derivation* (only Newton's calculus could prove that an inverse-square central force produces elliptical orbits). **Stigler's Law catches discoveries, not derivations.**

**And the *inverse* case — a tribute, not a misattribution.** It is worth keeping one counter-example on file precisely because it looks like a Stigler case and isn't. The **Boltzmann constant $k$** and the form $S = k\log W$ were both written down by **Max Planck**, not Boltzmann — Planck introduced the constant in 1900 and *named it after Boltzmann.* That sounds like the usual eponymy mischief, but it runs the other way: the discovery (entropy as the logarithm of the microstate count) is unambiguously Boltzmann's, and Planck — the formalizer, and Boltzmann's former opponent on the statistical view — deliberately attached his rival's name to the constant out of respect, even though he had his own constant $h$ in the very same papers. Here the eponym is *more* deserved than usual, not less: the name honours the right person for the right idea, supplied by someone who could easily have kept the credit. The full story is in [[Stories/Boltzmann's Tombstone]]. Stigler's Law is about credit going *astray*; this is the rarer, happier case of credit being *given*.

There is a second honest case worth filing beside it: **"abelian."** The commutative groups are named for [[Stories/Abel the Other Boy Who Died Young|Niels Henrik Abel]], who really did open the world they live in — and the name is carved so deep into working mathematics (abelian group, abelian integral, abelian variety) that it has lost its capital letter entirely. When credit goes right, it can go *all the way* right: an eponym so absorbed into the language that no one thinks of it as a name at all.

## Cultural ripples — why this matters

Three reasons the naming question keeps being interesting in 2026:

1. **Citation and search engines.** A theorem named after the wrong person is *findable under the wrong name*. Anyone Googling "Pell's equation" is led away from Brahmagupta's original work; the search-engine reinforces the misattribution. The 21st century's version of "the textbook wins" is "the SEO wins."

2. **AI training data and the eponymy of LLMs.** Language models are trained on the textbooks Stigler is critiquing. They learn the misattributed names *with high confidence*, because that's what their training corpus says repeatedly. Asking GPT-4 "who invented the Pascal triangle?" still risks getting "Blaise Pascal" as the answer unless you prompt carefully. The model is repeating the textbook bias at scale.

3. **The honest correction is now standard.** Modern history-of-mathematics textbooks (Boyer-Merzbach, Stillwell, Katz) restore the original discoverers in their narrative chapters but still use the eponymous names because the alternative would be unreadable. *You can't write a calculus text that says "the rule sometimes called l'Hôpital's but really Johann Bernoulli's." You write "l'Hôpital's rule" and footnote the truth.* The naming is now self-aware. That is the modern equilibrium: the names stay wrong, the histories get told right, and Stigler's Law remains correct.

## The deep methodological point

> *"Who discovered it first?"* and *"Whose name does it carry?"* are answers to **different questions.** Both can be correctly answered. They just often answer differently.

This is the load-bearing reason Stigler's Law isn't just a complaint. It's a methodological reminder: **the name on the theorem encodes a sociology of mathematical reception, not a history of mathematical invention.** Treating the name as *historical* leads to all the misattributions above. Treating the name as *sociological* — as a record of who explained it best, in the right language, in the right century — keeps the credits straight.

The same principle applies anywhere a name persists past its discovery: brand names, citation conventions, scientific units (the curie, the röntgen, the boltzmann), even programming languages (Pascal the language, named after Pascal the mathematician who didn't discover his triangle). **Every name is a sediment of who explained the thing, deposited over the rock of who found it.** Stigler made that sediment legible.
