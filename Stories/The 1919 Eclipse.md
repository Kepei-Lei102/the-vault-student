# The 1919 Eclipse 1919年的日食

> *"Through clouds. Hopeful."*
> — Frank Dyson, Astronomer Royal, decoding the cable from Príncipe, **3 June 1919**.

> *"Then I would feel sorry for the dear Lord. The theory is correct."*
> — Albert Einstein, asked what he would do if Eddington's result had come out *against* general relativity.

> *"Dear Mother, do not worry, whatever happens I shall not be shot."*
> — Arthur Eddington to his mother, 1918, during the tribunal that nearly sent the conscientious objector to a labour camp instead of to Príncipe.

## Cast of Characters

- **Arthur Stanley Eddington** (1882–1944) — Plumian Professor of Astronomy at Cambridge from 1913; Director of the Cambridge Observatory; *Quaker* and pacifist; the man with the photographic plates on Príncipe. Eddington was the first English-language scientist to fully read and accept Einstein's 1915 papers — at a time when the field equations had crossed almost no other desks outside Berlin.
- **Frank Watson Dyson** (1868–1939) — Astronomer Royal from 1910. Organised the 1919 expedition, talked the Admiralty into funding it during postwar austerity, and — quietly, crucially — *saved Eddington from a labour camp* by writing to the appeals tribunal that Britain needed this Cambridge astronomer in May 1919, not breaking rocks in a Welsh quarry.
- **Andrew Crommelin** (1865–1939) — Royal Observatory Greenwich; led the parallel expedition to **Sobral, Brazil**. (Two stations, two continents — to hedge against weather.) Quiet, careful, good at logistics; the man who actually delivered the cleanest data set.
- **Charles Davidson** (1875–1970) — Greenwich photographer; ran the Sobral plates.
- **Albert Einstein** (1879–1955) — Berlin, 1915 general relativity, 1916 prediction. Pretty much penniless and politically suspect (German, Jewish, anti-war) until 11 a.m. on 7 November 1919. Then world-famous before lunch.
- **Erwin Finlay-Freundlich** (1885–1964) — Berlin astronomer who would have tested Einstein's prediction in 1914, six years earlier, on a Crimean eclipse. He was *en route to Crimea on 1 August 1914* when the First World War began; Russian troops interned him on the German-Austrian side; his equipment was confiscated. Had Freundlich measured the deflection in 1914, he would have measured **only Einstein's 1911 wrong-by-half value** — Einstein hadn't yet finished general relativity. *History dodged a bullet.*
- **John Soldner** (1776–1833) and the ghost of **Henry Cavendish** (1731–1810) — both had calculated the Newtonian deflection (0.87'') a century earlier, treating light as corpuscles travelling at $c$ past a gravitating mass. Cavendish never published; Soldner published in 1801 and was forgotten for 120 years.

## 中文锚点

**1919年的日食观测**是物理学史上最戏剧性的一次单一实验。**爱因斯坦广义相对论** (1915) 预测：星光经过太阳附近时会被引力弯曲，偏转角应为 **1.75 角秒** —— 整整是牛顿引力理论 (将光看作粒子) 预测的 **0.87 角秒** 的两倍。这个差距非常小，但**一次完整的日全食**会让太阳完全被月亮遮住，露出旁边的星星，于是就能拍下来精确比较。

牛顿用了 200 年的物理学，是否会在这一张照相底片上被推翻？

英国天文学家 **爱丁顿 (Eddington)** —— 一个贵格会教徒、反战主义者，几乎差点被英国政府送进劳改营 —— 在战争结束半年后率队前往非洲西海岸的 **普林西比岛 (Príncipe)**；另一队由 **克罗梅林 (Crommelin)** 带队前往巴西的 **索布拉尔 (Sobral)**。1919年5月29日日全食发生时，普林西比天上有云，索布拉尔晴朗。爱丁顿的底片只有几张可用 —— 但够了。

测量结果：
- 普林西比 (爱丁顿)：$1.61 \pm 0.30$ 角秒
- 索布拉尔 4 英寸镜头：$1.98 \pm 0.18$ 角秒
- 索布拉尔天体照相镜头：被作为"系统误差污染"剔除 (注意这一步)

两个保留的结果都更接近爱因斯坦的 1.75 角秒，远离牛顿的 0.87 角秒。**1919年11月6日**，皇家学会和皇家天文学会的联合会议正式宣布：广义相对论得到证实。第二天《泰晤士报》头版：「**科学革命**」；几天后《纽约时报》：「**星光在天空中歪了**」。爱因斯坦从一个 40 岁的瑞士专利局前职员变成了世界名人，整个过程不到 24 小时。

这个故事还有一条暗线 —— **1980 年的重新分析**质疑爱丁顿是不是"挑选"了符合广义相对论的数据。这是一个测量精度的故事 (见 [[Accuracy vs Precision]])，所以这张卡片同时是 Foundations 测量三部曲的故事伴侣。

## Act I — Two Predictions for the Same Photograph (1801, 1911, 1915)

The question is older than most people realise. **John Soldner**, a Bavarian astronomer, published the calculation in 1801: if light is corpuscular and travels at $c$, then by Newton's universal gravitation it should be deflected by a passing mass. Plug in the Sun and you get $0.87''$ at the limb. Soldner's paper was promptly forgotten — light became a wave in the 19th century and the question of corpuscular deflection went into the drawer.

The drawer reopened in **1911**. Einstein, working from special relativity plus the equivalence principle (acceleration is locally indistinguishable from gravity), recomputed the deflection and got — almost embarrassingly — the *same* Newtonian value: $0.87''$. He published this in *Annalen der Physik* and Freundlich began planning the 1914 Crimean expedition.

Four years later, in November 1915, Einstein finished general relativity. The new theory said spacetime *itself* curves around mass — and this geometric curvature *doubles* the photon's deflection compared to the 1911 calculation. The full GR prediction is

$$\theta = \frac{4GM}{c^2 R} = 1.75''$$

at the Sun's limb (with $M$ the solar mass and $R$ the solar radius). Half of this is the "Newtonian" piece (treating light as falling) and half is the genuinely curved-spacetime piece. Add them up and you get the *factor of two* that became the entire experimental crux.

> [!info] Why a *factor of two* is decisive
> Recall from [[Accuracy vs Precision]] that the worst case in measurement is to be *precise but inaccurate* — you confidently report the wrong answer. If Einstein's prediction were $1.0''$ versus Newton's $0.87''$, a 15% disagreement would be lost in the noise of 1919 photography. **But $1.75''$ is *exactly twice* $0.87''$.** The factor of two is a categorical claim: the universe either does or does not curve spacetime; the answer cannot be "kinda." This is what made the 1919 measurement a *yes/no* test rather than a *how-much* test. The whole expedition was designed around that binary.

## Act II — The Quaker Who Almost Didn't Go (1916–1918)

Eddington was a Quaker by upbringing and pacifist by conviction. When the British government introduced military conscription in 1916, Eddington — now Plumian Professor at Cambridge — refused to serve. The Cambridge tribunal granted him an exemption on academic grounds, but the case was appealed by the local recruiting officer, and Eddington was summoned to a national appeals tribunal in 1918.

The tribunal would have sent Eddington either to the front, to a non-combatant role, or to a labour camp. By any of these routes, the 1919 eclipse would have happened without him.

**Frank Dyson intervened.** The Astronomer Royal wrote to the tribunal arguing that Eddington's specific skills — long-exposure photographic astrometry, plate measurement, the mathematics of general relativity — would be needed for an expedition in *May 1919* to test "the most important physical theory of the new century." Dyson all but promised the tribunal that the eclipse expedition was a matter of national scientific prestige, and that Eddington was uniquely positioned to lead it.

The tribunal agreed. Eddington's exemption was upheld on the condition that he carried out the eclipse work. *In the literal contract Eddington signed, going to Príncipe was the price of not going to prison.*

This is part of why the result hit so hard in 1919. The expedition wasn't an abstract test of curved spacetime; it was a Quaker, who had refused to fight a war between Britain and Germany, going to Africa to confirm the theory of a *German* physicist. **The act itself was anti-nationalist science** — a deliberate, public demonstration that physics outranked passports. Dyson, who organised the expedition, was perfectly aware of this framing and worked it into every press release.

## Act III — Príncipe and Sobral (March–May 1919)

To hedge against weather, the Joint Permanent Eclipse Committee sent two parties.

- **Príncipe**, a small island in the Gulf of Guinea off the coast of West Africa. **Eddington and Edwin Cottingham.** Two telescopes; one Cooke 13-inch astrographic.
- **Sobral**, Brazil. **Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson.** Two telescopes; one 4-inch lens, one astrographic.

Both teams sailed in March 1919. The instruments were the standard astrographic plates that the British Astronomical Association had used to map the sky for the *Carte du Ciel* project — known apparatus, known photographic emulsion. The plan was the same at both stations:

1. Photograph the eclipse field during totality. Record stellar positions through the deflected light.
2. Months later, photograph the *same* star field at night (no Sun present). Record the undeflected positions.
3. Subtract. The difference, scaled to the Sun's limb, is the deflection.

The hard part was not the eclipse photograph. The hard part was *the night comparison plates* — taken months later in different temperatures, with subtly different focus, on different photographic emulsion batches. Every step is a systematic-error trap.

**29 May 1919.** Príncipe began cloudy. Eddington wrote in his observing log: *"Through clouds. Hopeful."* The Sun broke through near the start of totality; Eddington exposed 16 plates over six minutes; only 6 of them showed usable stars. Sobral had perfect weather. Crommelin and Davidson exposed 27 plates.

![[the-1919-eclipse-principe-clouds-comic.png|560]]

Of the Sobral plates, two telescopes captured the eclipse on the same field. Here is where the story gets uncomfortable.

- **Sobral 4-inch lens**: 7 plates, sharp images, reduced cleanly. Result: $1.98 \pm 0.18''$.
- **Sobral astrographic lens**: 19 plates, but the lens's *focus had drifted* during totality, almost certainly because of thermal contraction as the Sun was occluded and the metal cooled. The stellar images were defocused; the astrometric scale was unstable. Reduced as-is, the astrographic plates gave $0.93 \pm 0.30''$ — *suspiciously close to Newton, far from Einstein*.

What do you do with the astrographic plates?

## Act IV — Throwing Out Data (the Foundations question)

This is the moment the 1919 expedition becomes a measurement story rather than a relativity story.

The Sobral astrographic data set was *not* random noise scattered around a number — it had **a clear, identifiable, physical cause of systematic distortion** (thermal focus drift, visible as defocused star images on the plates). The 4-inch lens did not share the problem; its plates were sharp. Eddington and Dyson together made the call: discard the astrographic data set as systematically compromised, keep the 4-inch Sobral set and the Príncipe set.

This is exactly the diagnostic move from [[Accuracy vs Precision]]: identify a *systematic* error source, *calibrate it out* — and where calibration is impossible, *exclude the affected data*. Averaging the bad astrographic plates with the good ones would have been the wrong move (averaging eats *random* noise; it cannot fix bias).

The 1919 reported result:

![[eddington-deflection-1919.svg|720]]

*Two prediction lines — Newton 0.87'' (left) and Einstein 1.75'' (right) — and the three Sobral/Príncipe data sets. The two kept measurements straddle Einstein; the discarded Sobral astrographic data set is shown in amber, with an open marker, near Newton.*

The two retained data sets straddle Einstein and lie well clear of Newton. **Both error bars exclude Newton at better than $3\sigma$.** Both error bars include Einstein within $1\sigma$. By 1919 measurement standards, this is conclusive.

> [!warning] The 1980 controversy — was the astrographic rejection *legitimate*?
> In 1980 the historians **John Earman and Clark Glymour** published a re-analysis arguing that Eddington had effectively *cherry-picked* — discarded the astrographic data not because of an independent physical fault but because it disagreed with the theory. Their charge was severe: that the 1919 announcement was sociologically driven (Einstein's theory was already fashionable in Cambridge), and the data alone did not warrant the headline.
>
> The modern verdict — established by **Daniel Kennefick**'s 2007 and 2019 re-analyses — is that *Eddington was right.* The focus drift on the astrographic plates is documented in the photographs themselves, independently of the theoretical comparison; the 4-inch plates show no such drift; the rejection was justified by the data's own properties, not by which prediction it agreed with. When all three Sobral data sets are *included* in a modern reduction (with proper handling of the astrographic plates' lower weight), the combined result is **closer to Einstein than Eddington's 1919 report stated** — the cherry-picking charge runs in the *wrong direction*.
>
> But the controversy itself is instructive. **Throwing out data is the most dangerous move an experimenter can make**, and the *only* defence is to do it on grounds that are visible to a competent outside reader — focus, plate fogging, temperature logs, an instrument malfunction noted in the observing log *before* the reduction was attempted. Eddington's defence passes that test; many experimenters' do not.

## Act V — 7 November 1919, Burlington House

Eddington returned to England in late August 1919. The plate reductions took two months. On **6 November 1919** the Joint Permanent Eclipse Committee announced the result; the *formal* announcement was at a joint meeting of the **Royal Society** and the **Royal Astronomical Society** the following day, 7 November.

J. J. Thomson, Royal Society President — *discoverer of the electron, conservative Newtonian* — chaired the meeting. His closing remarks have become legend:

> *"This is the most important result obtained in connection with the theory of gravitation since Newton's day, and it is fitting that it should be announced at a meeting of the Society so closely connected with him... If it is sustained that Einstein's reasoning holds good — and it has survived two very severe tests — then it is the result of one of the highest achievements of human thought."*

Thomson then conceded — visibly, in the room — that the result *had* held. The man whose career was built on Newtonian electron mechanics declared, in 1919, the end of the unconditional Newtonian universe. *That* is what gave the announcement its electric charge: the Newtonian establishment, in Newton's own learned society, formally yielding.

![[the-1919-eclipse-burlington-house-comic.png|560]]

The *Times of London* led the next morning with **"Revolution in Science / New Theory of the Universe / Newton's Ideas Overthrown."** Three days later the *New York Times*, working from telegrams, got the headline wrong in the most famous way the press has ever served physics: **"Lights All Askew in the Heavens / Men of Science More or Less Agog Over Results of Eclipse Observations / EINSTEIN THEORY TRIUMPHS."**

By the end of November, Einstein — who had been an obscure 40-year-old Berlin professor on the morning of 6 November — was the most famous scientist on Earth. He would remain so for the rest of his life.

## Act VI — What the Eclipse Did Outside Physics

Three downstream consequences, in increasing order of strangeness.

**(1) Einstein the public figure.** Before 1919, Einstein was known inside physics. After 1919, he was a celebrity in the modern sense — interviewed, photographed, asked about politics, asked about God. The press image of *Einstein as scientific genius* — wild hair, tongue out, gnomic remark about the universe — dates from after November 1919, not before. The 1919 eclipse did not just confirm a theory; it manufactured the 20th century's most recognisable scientific face.

**(2) German-British scientific reconciliation.** This was Dyson's quiet political achievement. The eclipse expedition was funded and run by the British scientific establishment to test a *German* theory, *eighteen months after* the Armistice ended a war that had killed a million British soldiers. The announcement was framed as a *triumph of international science* — physics doesn't care which side of the trench you were on. This framing reopened Anglo-German scientific exchange more quickly than would otherwise have been possible. It is one reason quantum mechanics — built in Göttingen, Copenhagen, Munich, Cambridge — could be a transcontinental project as early as the mid-1920s.

**(3) Eddington and the popularisation of relativity.** Eddington wrote the first English-language popular books on relativity (*Space, Time and Gravitation* 1920; *The Mathematical Theory of Relativity* 1923). He invented many of the explanations that physics teachers still use — the rubber-sheet analogy for curved spacetime, the ant on the balloon for expanding cosmology, the "two-dimensional flatlander" thought experiment for curvature. The Hawking-and-Sagan tradition of cosmologist-as-public-explainer descends from Eddington.

## Where this surfaces in the vault

The 1919 eclipse is, at its heart, a measurement story. The Foundations trio is its natural pedagogical home; this Story card carries the human drama that wouldn't fit those cards.

- **[[Accuracy vs Precision]]** — the systematic-vs-random factorisation is exactly what Eddington had to apply to the Sobral plates. The astrographic rejection is the canonical worked example of *"identify systematic error → calibrate or exclude → don't average it out."* Average could not have saved the focus-drift data.
- **[[Error Propagation]]** — Eddington's quoted uncertainties ($\pm 0.30''$ Príncipe, $\pm 0.18''$ Sobral 4-inch) were derived by propagating plate-measurement uncertainties through the entire reduction pipeline (relative star positions → plate scale → projected deflection at the limb). The pipeline involved subtractions of nearly-equal quantities — the case where *absolute* errors add and you cannot escape into percentages.
- **[[Significant Figures]]** — the reported "$1.61$" and "$1.75$" both carry three significant figures because the *uncertainty* is on the order of $0.3''$ and $0.2''$ respectively. Reporting "$1.612$" would have been a calculator-vomit honesty violation; reporting "$1.6$" would have been throwing away resolution Eddington actually had. The trio is in equilibrium with the data, exactly as [[Significant Figures]] §"weakest-link arithmetic" demands.
- **[[Newton's Laws of Motion]]** — the Newton column in the Act I prediction table is what 200 years of universal gravitation said the answer should be. The 1919 measurement is the moment that prediction, in the gravitational regime, was experimentally distinguished from Einstein's. (Newton's laws survive everywhere $v \ll c$ and gravitational fields are weak; they are not *wrong*, they are *incomplete*. The same way [[Hooke's Law for Springs]] is the small-displacement limit of a more general elasticity, Newton's gravitation is the weak-field limit of GR.)
- **[[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]]** and **[[Stories/Newton vs Hooke]]** — the *previous* Newton priority stories. The 1919 eclipse closes the long Newton arc in this vault: Newton fought to ensure his name on calculus and the inverse-square law in his lifetime; 192 years after his death, the inverse-square law was demoted to the leading-order term of a deeper theory by a measurement performed in his learned society. The vault's Newton arc has now told the inauguration (calculus), the dress rehearsal (Hooke), and the eventual quiet supersession (Einstein).

## Cultural ripples — the 1980 controversy in more detail

It is worth slowing down on the 1980 Earman-Glymour critique because it is the *teaching* case for what makes a data-exclusion decision *legitimate*.

The Earman-Glymour argument, distilled:
1. The Sobral astrographic data set agreed with Newton, not Einstein.
2. Eddington had a strong prior commitment to Einstein's theory.
3. Eddington excluded the astrographic set "because of focus drift."
4. Steps 2 and 3 may be causally linked; the focus-drift justification may have been confabulated *post hoc* to legitimise an exclusion the experimenter wanted on theoretical grounds.

The reply — assembled by Kennefick and others over 2007–2019 — passes through *contemporaneous documentation*:
- Eddington's own 1919 observing log notes the focus instability of the astrographic apparatus *before* the plate reduction was complete.
- The physical mechanism (thermal contraction of the telescope tube during totality) is independently documented for that instrument design; later eclipse expeditions using the same lens design had similar problems.
- The plates themselves show the defocus on visual inspection; this is not a property of the deflection calculation but of the imaging.
- A 1979 *re-reduction* of the original Sobral astrographic plates by the Greenwich team, using modern image-analysis software, *recovered Einstein's value* within error once the focus distortion was properly modelled. The plates were not bad data; they were data with a known correction that was beyond Eddington's 1919 toolkit.

The lesson is exactly what the Foundations cards say: a systematic-error exclusion is legitimate when it has an *independent physical cause* visible in the apparatus, the observing log, or the raw data — not when it is justified solely by which prediction the included data favours. Eddington's exclusion passes; many published exclusions in less careful experiments do not. **The 1980 critique was the immune system of science working correctly; the 2007–2019 vindication was the same immune system reaching the right verdict on the second pass.**

> [!tip] Sociology of confirmation
> A subtler point lurks underneath. **Confirmation is not symmetric with falsification.** Had Eddington reported $0.87''$ in 1919, the result would have been read as *"Einstein wrong, Newton vindicated"* — a single eclipse killing GR. In fact a single negative eclipse would not have killed GR; the theory's mathematical coherence and the precession-of-Mercury success would have demanded more eclipses, better instruments, decades of follow-up. Confirmation in 1919 was loud and instant *because* the result was expected by the (still-small) community of relativity insiders. **The press headline reflected the social cost of *not* believing, not the bare statistical weight of one expedition.**
>
> Subsequent eclipses (1922 Wallal Australia, 1929 Sumatra, 1947 Brazil, 1973 Mauritania) all confirmed the GR value within their respective uncertainties. By the 1960s radio-astronomical measurements of solar deflection of quasar signals achieved $\sim 1\%$ precision; modern Very Long Baseline Interferometry has confirmed $\theta_{GR}$ to better than $0.02\%$. The 1919 eclipse was the inauguration, not the proof. The proof is the *century of agreement* that followed.

## The lesson

Three takeaways, in order of generality.

1. **A single measurement, well executed, can shift a paradigm — but the execution is the entire story.** Eddington's $1.61'' \pm 0.30''$ is *one number from one expedition*. It carried the announcement at Burlington House because every step — the *a priori* prediction with a factor-of-two difference, the dual-station design hedge, the documented systematic-error exclusion, the open publication of plate quality and reduction methods — was visible and checkable. Strip any of these and the result would not have been load-bearing. **Headlines like "*Newton overthrown*" require boring measurement discipline. The drama is downstream of the discipline.**

2. **Throwing data out is the most dangerous move in experimental physics, and the *only* defence is independence.** The reason to exclude a data set must be a property the data set has *as data* — instability, malfunction, contamination — and not a property of how the included data set compares to a theoretical prediction. The 1919 expedition passes this test; many famous experiments do not, and the litigation lasts decades. See [[Accuracy vs Precision]] §"Misconception 3 — averaging removes systematic error" for the canonical version of this rule.

3. **The 20th century's scientific image of itself was made in 6 minutes of totality on 29 May 1919.** Not because relativity needed those 6 minutes — Einstein's Mercury-perihelion calculation in 1915 had already converted insiders — but because the *public* needed a clean dramatic moment to mark the end of the unconditional Newtonian world. Burlington House on 7 November gave it to them. The vault treats this honestly: the eclipse was *necessary* for the cultural transition, *sufficient* for the experimental case, *and* the experimental case was harder and slower than the cultural memory recalls. Both are true.
