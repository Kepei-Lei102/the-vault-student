# Wolfgang Pauli and the Number 137 沃尔夫冈·泡利与数字 137

> *He was the conscience of physics — the man whose verdict "not even wrong" could kill a theory, godson of the philosopher who insisted you never speak of what you cannot see. And he spent the second half of his life chasing dreams with Carl Jung, and one number — 137 — that he could neither explain nor let go of. When he died, it was in hospital room number 137.*

![[wolfgang-pauli-two-threads.svg|700]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Wolfgang Pauli** (1900–1958) — Austrian-Swiss physicist; the **exclusion principle**, the **neutrino**, "the conscience of physics." Nobel Prize 1945.
- **Ernst Mach** (1838–1916) — physicist-philosopher, arch-**positivist** ("Have you ever seen one?"), Boltzmann's great anti-atomist adversary — and **Pauli's godfather**.
- **Arnold Sommerfeld** (1868–1951) — Pauli's doctoral teacher in Munich; the era's great trainer of theorists.
- **Niels Bohr** — mentor and sparring partner; willing to abandon energy conservation in β-decay where Pauli would not.
- **Ralph Kronig, George Uhlenbeck & Samuel Goudsmit** — electron **spin** (Pauli talked Kronig out of it; the other two published and got the credit).
- **Enrico Fermi** — built the theory of β-decay on Pauli's ghost particle and gave it its name: *neutrino*.
- **Carl Jung** (1875–1961) — psychoanalyst; Pauli's analyst, then his thirty-year correspondent on physics and the psyche.

## 中文锚点

**沃尔夫冈·泡利**（Wolfgang Pauli, 1900–1958）：量子力学的奠基人之一，"物理学的良心"。他提出**不相容原理**（Pauli exclusion principle）——同一原子中没有两个电子能拥有完全相同的量子态，这一条禁令直接解释了**整张元素周期表**的结构、物质为何有体积、你的手为何穿不过桌子。他还在 1930 年大胆假设了一种几乎无法探测的中性粒子（费米后来命名为**中微子** neutrino）来挽救 β 衰变中的能量守恒。

但这位最严苛的理性主义者——他的教父正是**逼迫玻尔兹曼**的反原子论者[[Stories/Boltzmann's Tombstone|马赫]]（Mach）——后半生却痴迷于荣格（Jung）的梦境分析、"共时性"，以及一个他无法解释的纯数字：精细结构常数 $\alpha \approx 1/137$。他死在苏黎世医院的 **137 号房间**。物理学的硬核内容（不相容原理、费米子、简并压）由 [[Pauli Exclusion Principle]] 卡片承载；这张故事卡讲的是这个人。

## The Story

### Act I — The godson of the skeptic (1900–1921)

He was born in Vienna in 1900, and his godfather was **Ernst Mach**. Hold that fact against the rest of the vault for a moment: Mach was the apostle of *"Haben Sie eines gesehen?"* — "Have you ever seen one?" — the positivist who tormented [[Stories/Boltzmann's Tombstone|Boltzmann]] for daring to build physics on atoms no one could observe. Mach gave the infant a silver cup, and the family a creed: **trust only what you can measure.** It is one of history's quieter ironies that the man who pressed that creed on the Pauli household stood godfather to the boy who would spend his life mapping the unobservable interior of the atom.

The boy was a prodigy of the frightening kind. He devoured Einstein's relativity as a schoolboy, and at **twenty-one** wrote a 237-page encyclopedia article on the theory so complete that Einstein himself reviewed it in disbelief: *"No one studying this mature, grandly conceived work would believe that the author is a man of twenty-one."* He took his doctorate under **Sommerfeld** in Munich and walked straight into the front rank of physics.

### Act II — No two alike (1924–1925)

In **1925** Pauli stated the principle that carries his name: in an atom, **no two electrons can share the same complete set of quantum numbers.** It sounds like a technicality. It is the reason the universe has chemistry. Electrons cannot all pile into the lowest energy level — the prohibition forces them to **stack into shells**, and the shape of that stacking *is* the periodic table, the valences, the bonds, the fact that matter takes up room. To make the bookkeeping work Pauli had to introduce a strange "two-valuedness not describable classically" — what we now call **spin**.

The honest edge: when **Ralph Kronig** suggested the electron *literally spins*, Pauli ridiculed the idea out of him, and Kronig never published. Months later **Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit** proposed exactly that — and got the credit. (Pauli's objection wasn't pure stubbornness: a classically spinning electron would have to whirl faster than light at its surface. He was wrong about the picture, right to be suspicious of it, and he came around — later writing the **Pauli matrices** that give spin its proper mathematics.) The Nobel Prize for the exclusion principle came in **1945**, with Einstein doing the nominating.

### Act III — The conscience of physics

Pauli was the most feared critic in physics, and he earned the title *das Gewissen der Physik* — the conscience of physics. His ultimate dismissal has outlived almost everything else he said: of one hopeless paper he reportedly snapped that it was *"not even wrong"* (**nicht einmal falsch**) — so untestable it failed to rise even to the dignity of being false. Listen to where that blade was forged: it is **Mach's** demand for the observable, sharpened into a weapon. The godfather's creed, in the godson's mouth.

And then the other side of him, which he wore with delight: the **Pauli effect** — the running law among physicists that delicate apparatus broke the instant Pauli entered the building. Otto Stern, who ran a sensitive experimental lab, banned him from it on principle. Equipment in Göttingen once failed for no reason, and the culprit was later "traced" to the exact hour Pauli's train had paused at the station. The most exacting rationalist alive cheerfully half-believed he could shatter glassware by walking past it. Both things were true of him at once. They always were.

### Act IV — The desperate remedy (1930)

Beta decay would not behave. When a nucleus emitted an electron, the electron came out with a *smear* of energies rather than the single sharp value that energy conservation demanded — and the books would not balance. **Bohr** was prepared to do the unthinkable and abandon conservation of energy itself.

Pauli refused. And to save the law he did something startlingly **un-Machian**: he postulated a new particle nobody had seen — electrically neutral, nearly massless, almost impossible to detect — to slip out of the nucleus carrying the missing energy and momentum. He announced it not in a paper but in a letter, dated **4 December 1930**, to a conference he couldn't be bothered to attend (he had a ball in Zürich). It opens, immortally: *"Dear Radioactive Ladies and Gentlemen…"* He called the particle a "neutron"; after Chadwick found the *real* neutron in 1932, **Fermi** renamed Pauli's ghost the **neutrino** — "little neutral one" — and built the modern theory of β-decay around it.

![[wolfgang-pauli-neutrino-letter-comic.png|560]]

Pauli was tormented by what he'd done: *"I have done a terrible thing. I have postulated a particle that cannot be detected."* He bet a case of champagne it never would be. He lost: in **1956**, Cowan and Reines caught the neutrino, and wired him the news. Pauli wired back, *"Everything comes to him who knows how to wait,"* and paid the champagne. He died two years later. The arch-empiricist's single greatest gift to physics was an act of **faith in a conservation law over the evidence of his own eyes** — and he was right.

### Act V — The crack (1927–1932)

The genius was coming apart at the same time. In **1927** his mother took her own life. His father soon remarried a much younger woman whom Pauli bitterly called "the wicked stepmother." In **1929** he made a hasty marriage to a cabaret dancer, Käthe Deppner; it dissolved within the year when she left him — for a chemist — drawing from Pauli the wounded, perfect quip: *"Had she taken a bullfighter, I could have understood — but a chemist…"* He was drinking heavily, picking fights, unravelling. The conscience of physics needed help, and he knew it.

### Act VI — The other half (1932–1958)

He went, of all places, to **Carl Jung**, in Zürich. Jung's circle recorded over a thousand of Pauli's dreams — the anonymous "dreams of a man of science" that fill parts of Jung's published work — and what began as therapy became one of the strangest collaborations in intellectual history: a **thirty-year correspondence** between the century's most rigorous physicist and its most mystical psychologist, on archetypes, the unconscious, and **synchronicity** (Jung's name for meaningful coincidence with no causal link), culminating in a book they wrote together in 1952.

And underneath it all, the obsession: the **fine-structure constant**, $\alpha \approx 1/137$ — the pure, dimensionless number that braids together the quantum, relativity, and electromagnetism into the strength of light's grip on matter. Pauli wanted to **derive 137 from first principles**; he was convinced that any true theory of the universe would have to *produce* it. The man who invented "not even wrong" spent his evenings on a question that was, by his own ferocious standard, not even wrong. This was not senility and not a lapse. It was him — the same man — holding rigor and the numinous in the same two hands and refusing, his whole life, to let go of either.

### Act VII — Room 137 (15 December 1958)

In December 1958 Pauli collapsed and was taken to the Rotkreuzspital, the Red Cross hospital in Zürich. His assistant Charles Enz came to visit. Pauli, agitated, asked him a single question: had he noticed the **number of the room**?

It was **137**.

He did not leave it alive. Pancreatic cancer took him within days; he was 58. The number he had chased his entire life — that he could not explain and could not stop reading as a sign — was the number on the door he died behind. There is no physics in that coincidence; it means nothing. But for a man who spent half his genius demanding proof and the other half listening for meaning, it is the perfect, unbearable last line.

## Honest edges

- **The room-137 story is real but easily over-told.** It comes through Pauli's assistant Charles Enz and is well attested; what's *not* warranted is reading any significance into it. It is a coincidence, full stop — and saying so plainly is more respectful of Pauli, who knew the difference between a sign and a number better than anyone, than turning his death into a mystical fable.
- **The Jung / synchronicity work is not science — and was genuinely important to Pauli.** By Pauli's own falsifiability standard, synchronicity is "not even wrong," and the vault does not present it as physics. But it was a real, sustained part of who he was, not a footnote or an embarrassment to be hidden. The point of the story is precisely that the *same* mind held both — sneering at it or endorsing it would each miss the man.
- **Pauli did not "steal" spin from Kronig**, and his skepticism had a defensible basis (the faster-than-light surface-speed problem). He discouraged a correct idea and that cost Kronig the credit — a real failure of nerve — but it belongs in the same drawer as a hundred other near-misses, not the villainy drawer.
- **The neutrino as "an act of faith" is a useful frame, not the whole truth.** Pauli's bet rested on the extraordinary track record of conservation laws, not on mysticism; calling it pure faith overstates it. The genuine tension is subtler: the apostle of the observable chose an *unobservable* to save a principle — and trusted the principle.
- **The displaced years.** Pauli's paternal family were Prague Jews (originally *Pascheles*) who had converted to Catholicism; under Nazi racial law he was "non-Aryan." After the 1938 *Anschluss* he was forced into unwanted German citizenship, failed to secure Swiss citizenship in time, and spent the war stranded at Princeton, isolated and unhappy, before returning to Zürich in 1946. The exclusion principle's author spent the war excluded.

## Cultural ripples

**The exclusion principle holds up the world.** It is not an atomic-physics curiosity — it is why there *is* a world of distinct, rigid, voluminous matter. It gives the periodic table its shape and chemistry its rules; it is the real reason your hand doesn't pass through the table (the repulsive "wall" in [[Internal Energy]] is Pauli exclusion in action); and it is what props **white dwarfs and neutron stars** against their own gravity — electron and neutron *degeneracy pressure*, with the **Chandrasekhar limit** marking where even Pauli's prohibition finally loses to gravity and the star collapses. The behaviour of electrons in metals and semiconductors — **Fermi–Dirac statistics**, the thing that makes transistors work — is the exclusion principle counted at scale. Strip it out and there is no chemistry, no solid matter, no us.

**The undetectable particle founded a science.** The neutrino Pauli apologized for went from "impossible to detect" to an entire field: solar-neutrino astronomy, supernova alarms, and a 2015 Nobel Prize for the discovery that neutrinos have mass. Roughly a hundred trillion of them stream through your body every second, leaving no trace — exactly as he feared, and exactly why we needed him to insist they were there.

**"Not even wrong" entered the language** as the gold-standard dismissal of a claim too vague to be tested — later the title of a famous critique of string theory. Pauli's blade outlived most of his equations.

**137 is still unexplained.** The fine-structure constant remains a number we *measure*, never derive. Feynman called it "one of the greatest damn mysteries of physics: a magic number that comes to us with no understanding." Pauli's question — *why this number?* — is still open. He never got his answer; neither has anyone else.

## Coda — holding the unfalsifiable lightly

Pauli's life leaves a question larger than Pauli: **what do you do with the ideas you can't test?**

His own weapon, "not even wrong," is Karl Popper's razor with a sharper handle — *falsifiability* is the line between **science** and non-science. But it was never the line between the **meaningful** and the worthless, and Pauli, of all people, is the proof. The things we cannot strictly falsify are many, and far from empty: whether the wavefunction truly *collapses* or merely *branches* (the Copenhagen and many-worlds readings of quantum mechanics make identical predictions — choosing between them is, so far, untestable); whether the universe is a **simulation** (the intuition that a video game renders only what the player looks at is a vivid, if loose, echo of the quantum measurement puzzle — *a possibility until it is observed* — and David Chalmers' *Reality+* argues that even a simulated world would be no less *real*, and that from the inside we likely could not tell); most of what **psychology** and Jung's archetypes try to name; and the meaning we cannot help reading into a coincidence.

None of these is science. Every one of them is worth thinking about. **The trap is not *entertaining* such an idea — it is *needing it to be true*.** Pauli had curiosity and rigor in equal, enormous measure; what hurt him was letting the curiosity curdle into a demand — that 137 *must* be derivable, that the coincidence *must* mean something. And the cure was sitting inside his own greatest work: the neutrino was an unobservable *maybe*, held honestly as a maybe — and twenty-six years later it became measurable after all. Today's unfalsifiable is sometimes tomorrow's experiment, which is exactly why the grown-up move is to neither swallow these ideas whole nor sneer them away.

Mathematics has taken this licence for four centuries. To solve certain real cubics you are *forced* through $\sqrt{-1}$ — an "impossible" number you ride through the calculation until the imaginary parts cancel and a real, checkable answer falls out ([[Stories/The Argument for i]]). The intermediate was unprovable nonsense by the standards of its day; it was legitimate anyway, because the procedure was consistent and the *output* was real. That is the licence we take every day — reasoning through an assumption, a model of someone's intentions, a next move we cannot prove — and letting the *result*, not the input, settle the account. The scaffolding need not be real; the conclusion must check out. (Pauli's error was never *using* 137 — it was refusing to let it cancel, mistaking the scaffolding for the building.)

So don't be obsessed with synchronicity. But when it happens — when the room really is numbered **137** — you are allowed to grin, think *hey, that's pretty cool*, and get back to work. Rigor and wonder were never enemies; Pauli simply never found the place to stand between them. The gift of his story is that we get to. (This is the kind of "what *status* does a claim have, and how tightly should you hold it?" question that [[Laws and Theorems]] is built around.)
