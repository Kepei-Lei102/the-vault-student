# Ibn al-Haytham and the Question of Seeing 伊本·海赛姆与看见的疑问

> *A room, a few lamps, and the awkward possibility that a very old explanation might be wrong.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Ibn al-Haytham (c.965–c.1040)** — Arabic-writing scholar associated with Basra and Cairo; known in Latin as Alhazen.
- **Al-Hakim (reigned 996–1021)** — Fatimid caliph of Egypt; the powerful patron in the later Nile-project accounts.
- **Euclid and Ptolemy** — ancient authorities whose geometrical optics provided formidable material to work with and argue against.
- **The unnamed readers, copyists and translators** — the people through whom an argument could outlive its author.

## Prologue — Your eyes are working perfectly

Switch off the bedside lamp in a genuinely dark room. Keep your eyes open. Look harder at the book.

No improvement.

This is an unusually cheap way to discover that *trying to see* and *having something available to see* are different activities. Looking feels active: you turn your head, direct your attention, search a face. It is easy to imagine that the eyes do something outward to the world.

But a theory must explain the dark room as well as the bright one.

Around a thousand years ago, Ibn al-Haytham made the relationship between light and sight the subject of an extraordinary investigation. His seven-book *Optics*, written in Arabic, brought geometrical reasoning into sustained contact with the physical eye. Its reach extended far beyond his own language and lifetime. [The modern edition's introduction](https://uolpress.co.uk/book/the-optics-of-ibn-al-haytham-books-iv-v/)

## Act I — When certainty comes in incompatible versions

Ibn al-Haytham's childhood is mostly beyond our reach. We do have an account of his intellectual development, written in 1027 and preserved by a later biographer. Looking back at his youth, he described encountering incompatible religious convictions, each firmly held. He eventually committed himself to philosophical and mathematical inquiry; Aristotle was a major guide. This was a scholar working deeply within inherited learning. [MacTutor biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Al-Haytham/)

The predicament is familiar. Two adults explain the same event. Both sound completely certain. Their accounts cannot both be right.

Which part of the certainty is evidence?

We cannot reconstruct the private thoughts of the young man from Basra. But his later work makes the question productive. Confidence, reputation and a beautifully organised argument can all be present while a premise is false.

The hard part is that this applies to your own argument too.

## Act II — The river story, with its uncertainty left attached

A later account by Ibn al-Qifti says that Ibn al-Haytham proposed regulating the Nile. Al-Hakim summoned him to Egypt; inspecting the river defeated the plan. An administrative appointment followed. Fearing the ruler, the scholar allegedly feigned mental illness and remained confined until the caliph's death. Other accounts disagree about the encounter and his subsequent movements. Most were written long after his lifetime. His autobiographical account does not settle them. [A. I. Sabra, *Dictionary of Scientific Biography*, opening biographical discussion](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Al-Haytham.pdf)

It is a magnificent plot: the engineer promises to tame a river, discovers the river has other arrangements, and must survive the man who paid for the promise.

That is precisely why it needs care.

We should not turn it into a witnessed prison scene, assign the *Optics* a convenient date of composition there, or make a dramatic escape the cause of every subsequent discovery. The surviving scientific arguments can carry their own drama.

A scholar who spends hundreds of pages giving readers reasons has left us something more interesting than an invitation to believe his legend.

## Act III — Give the lamps a chance to answer

The *Optics* describes lamps placed outside a dark room, their light entering through a small opening and making separate patches on the opposite wall. Their order reverses. The investigation concerns how light travels and how the contributions remain distinguishable after crossing. [Sabra, “Alhazen's Optics in Europe,” pp. 53–55](https://www.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/sites/default/files/migrated/p333.pdf)

Imagine setting it up with **two small LED lights**, a dark box, a pinhole and a white inner screen. There are no tiny photographs stored inside the hole. Yet the light makes a spatial arrangement on the far side.

![[ibn-al-haytham-lamp-paths.svg|850]]

*An idealised top view, with two sources at equal distances from the opening. The colours label the two paths; the lamps need not have different colours. Only the rays through the hole are drawn.*

Follow source A's light towards the opening. After passing through, it continues in the same straight line. It does not turn to stay on A's side of the room. Source B's light does the corresponding thing from the other side. Their paths cross, then separate.

The account explicitly describes screening a lamp and restoring its patch when the screen is removed. [Translated passage reproduced in this historical essay](https://www.firstscientist.net/3.pdf)

**Now change just one thing.** Slide an opaque card across A's route before it reaches the hole, leaving B's route clear.

A's patch vanishes. B's remains. Remove the card: A returns.

This is stronger evidence than standing in the room and remarking that the patches look rather lamp-like. You have made one source unavailable and watched what depended on it. An explanation must account for the change as well as the original scene.

For the ideal geometry shown, similar triangles give

$$\frac{\text{patch offset}}{\text{lamp offset}}
=\frac{\text{hole-to-screen distance}}{\text{lamp-to-hole distance}}.$$

The offsets are on opposite sides of the central line. Move the screen twice as far from the hole, with the sources fixed, and the separation between the patches doubles. That is a new prediction you can check, not a label to memorise.

A real pinhole has a width, so its patches are not mathematical points. Keep the hole small enough to separate them and the interior dark enough to see them. Use ordinary LEDs; this demonstration needs neither flames nor sunlight.

The charm is how little apparatus the question requires. The difficulty lies in deciding what to change and what to keep still.

## Act IV — Change the explanation, keep the geometry

Ancient theories of sight were diverse. Some used rays extending from the eye; theories in which something arrived from the object also existed. Ibn al-Haytham did not invent the bare possibility of incoming influence. He constructed a new synthesis: light from visible objects affects the eye, while geometry helps explain the spatial order of what is seen. [Sabra, “Optics: Doctrine of Light” and “Theory of Vision”](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DSB/Al-Haytham.pdf)

Think about the practical difficulty. Every visible point sends light in many directions. Much of it never enters your eye. The light that does arrive has to preserve enough information for you to distinguish a friend's face from the wall behind it.

“Light comes in” begins the explanation. It does not finish it.

Nor did Ibn al-Haytham possess our finished explanation. His account assigned visual sensitivity to the crystalline humour; it was **not** the modern account of a lens forming an image on the retina. Knowing a dark-room experiment does not automatically supply a correct model of an eye. [Sabra, “Alhazen's Optics in Europe,” p. 53](https://www.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/sites/default/files/migrated/p333.pdf)

This makes his achievement human-sized in the best sense. He could change an enormously consequential part of a theory and still be wrong about another part. Correctable work is work someone else can continue.

## Act V — An authority who asks to be questioned

He had already asked readers to discard an earlier work of his own. In the preface to the *Optics*, he rejected that previous treatment in favour of a more demonstrative approach. The demand for better evidence had reached his own bookshelf. [Sabra, pp. 54–55](https://www.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/sites/default/files/migrated/p333.pdf)

Imagine the cost of that sentence. You have finished a work. People may have read it. Your name is on it. Now you have to tell those people that your name was never enough.

In *Doubts on Ptolemy*, Ibn al-Haytham wrote:

> “Truth is sought for its own sake”

The passage challenges readers to examine arguments rather than trust an author's standing. This English wording is quoted in S. M. Razaullah Ansari's account; it is a translation, not the original Arabic. [Ansari, “Views on Authorities”](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/sm-razaullah-ansari-ibn-al-haythams-scientific-method)

There is a tempting way to ruin that sentence: print it beside a portrait, call the man the inventor of rational thought, and stop asking questions.

A better tribute would be to inspect his demonstration. Does the obstacle really block only one lamp? Is the room dark enough? Does the predicted patch return? Have we accepted the explanation because of the evidence, or because we like the person attached to it?

Even the comic deserves that test. Its scholar and assistant are fiction. Its apparatus makes a claim. Those are different kinds of things to trust.

## Cultural ripples — An argument travels farther than its author

The Arabic *Optics* entered Latin through a medieval translation, generally dated to the twelfth–thirteenth centuries. Readers knew its author as Alhazen. The work influenced later optical thinkers, including Kepler. The route involved translation and subsequent reworking, not a single untouched discovery passed from hand to hand. [University of London Press, historical introduction](https://uolpress.co.uk/book/the-optics-of-ibn-al-haytham-books-iv-v/)

Your phone camera offers a small daily encounter with the underlying physical problem. Light must reach its sensor in an organised way; cover the lens and software cannot recover the unobstructed live scene from light that never arrived. A lens and electronic sensor are major additions to a dark box with a hole. The shared question is still concrete: **what route made this part of the picture possible?**

The connection reaches beyond cameras. When a device fails and you unplug one component at a time, you are trying to give causes separate opportunities to reveal themselves. The result is useful only if your intervention isolates what you think it isolates.

Perhaps that is the scene worth remembering: someone moves a small screen, and a patch of light disappears.

A very large claim has agreed to answer a very small question.
