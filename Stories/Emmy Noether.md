# Emmy Noether 埃米·诺特

> *Every conservation law is a symmetry in disguise. The person who proved it was not allowed to lecture under her own name.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Emmy Noether (1882–1935)** — one of two women among 986 students at Erlangen; the algebraist who rebuilt algebra around structure instead of formulas; author of the 1918 theorem that turned "energy is conserved" from a mystery into a consequence.
- **Max Noether (1844–1921)** — her father, professor of mathematics at Erlangen, for whom she lectured unpaid when he was ill.
- **David Hilbert (1862–1943)** and **Felix Klein (1849–1925)** — the two men who ran Göttingen mathematics and invited her in 1915; Hilbert fought the faculty for her, and lent her his name when he lost.
- **Albert Einstein (1879–1955)** — lectured in Göttingen in the summer of 1915 on a theory that was not yet finished; its energy problem was the question she answered.
- **Hermann Weyl (1885–1955)** — colleague, admirer, and the man who spoke at her funeral.
- **Pavel Alexandrov (1896–1982)** — the Moscow topologist who became her close friend and called her *der Noether*.
- **The Noether boys** — van der Waerden, Deuring, Fitting, Witt, Grete Hermann and the rest: the students who turned her lectures into a subject.

## The Story

![[noether-timeline.svg|900]]

### Act I — One of two (Erlangen, 1900–1908)

Emmy Noether was born in 1882 in Erlangen, a small Bavarian university town, into a family of mathematicians and iron merchants. The girls' school taught her German, English, French, arithmetic and the piano; she liked dancing. At eighteen she passed the Bavarian state examination and became a certified teacher of English and French for girls' schools, with the grade "very good", her weakest mark being for classroom teaching. She never taught a language class in her life.

Instead she did the thing the university had been built to prevent. In 1900 women could not matriculate at Erlangen; two years earlier the Academic Senate had ruled that mixed-sex education would "overthrow all academic order". A woman could sit in on lectures if each professor gave his personal permission. Noether collected the permissions and sat in, one of two women among 986 students. In 1903 she passed the matriculation examination at Nuremberg, spent a semester listening to Hilbert, Klein, Minkowski and Schwarzschild in Göttingen, and when the rules changed in 1904 she matriculated at Erlangen as a proper student, in mathematics only.

Her doctorate came in 1907 under Paul Gordan, the "king of invariants", whose way of working was to calculate. The thesis computed a complete system of 331 invariants of a ternary biquadratic form, page after page of them, and was passed *summa cum laude*. Noether later called it, and the papers that followed it, "crap". She had already seen the other way of doing algebra, Hilbert's, in which you prove that the invariants exist and are finite in number without ever writing one down, and she spent the rest of her life on that side of the argument.

From 1908 to 1915 she worked at the Erlangen Mathematical Institute without pay, supervising doctoral students and taking her father's lectures when he was ill. She published, she was elected to the German Mathematical Society, she wrote to colleagues about algebra on postcards. Nobody offered her a post.

### Act II — "Not a bathhouse" (Göttingen, 1915)

In 1915 Hilbert and Klein invited her to Göttingen. Weyl recorded the reason without decoration: "to both Hilbert and Klein, Emmy was welcome as she was able to help them with invariant-theoretic knowledge." Hilbert wanted her because he was working on physics, and the physics was Einstein's.

Then Hilbert tried to get her a *Habilitation*, the licence to lecture under her own name, and ran into the philologists and historians of the Philosophical Faculty. Weyl told the story at her funeral twenty years later: Hilbert declared at the faculty meeting, "I do not see that the sex of a candidate is an argument against her admission as *Privatdozent*. After all, we are a university, not a bathing establishment." His exact words were not minuted, and Alexandrov later added that the opposition was not only to her sex but to her social-democratic politics and her Jewish family. Hilbert lost. The faculty would not license a woman.

![[emmy-noether-bathhouse-comic.png|640]]

So Hilbert cheated. For four years Noether's courses appeared in the Göttingen catalogue under his name: *Mathematical Physics Seminar: Professor Hilbert, with the assistance of Dr E. Noether, Mondays from 4 to 6, no tuition.* She wrote the courses, she gave them, and the university's records say Hilbert did. She was not paid. She was thirty-three.

### Act III — Hilbert's broken energy (1915–1918)

In June and July 1915 Einstein came to Göttingen and gave six lectures on the general theory of relativity, a theory he had not yet finished. "To my great joy," he wrote afterwards, "I completely succeeded in convincing Hilbert and Klein." In November both men reached the field equations, Einstein by his physical route and Hilbert by a variational principle, and that same month Noether wrote to a friend that Hilbert planned to lecture on "Einstein's differential invariants, and so we had better be ready." She began to study relativity.

She was good at it at once. Einstein, sent one of her papers, wrote to Hilbert: "Yesterday I received from Miss Noether a very interesting paper on invariant forms. I am impressed that one can comprehend these matters from so general a viewpoint. It would not have done the old guard at Göttingen any harm had they picked up a thing or two from her."

The problem Hilbert put to her was this. In every physical theory before 1915, energy was conserved in the plain sense: the energy in any region changes only by what flows in or out through its boundary. In [[General Relativity]] that statement went wrong. The quantity that should have been the energy density could be made to vanish or appear by changing coordinates, and nobody could say where the energy of a gravitational field was. Hilbert called it "the failure of the energy theorem" and guessed, in letters to Klein, that the failure was not a defect but a characteristic of the theory: such theories had only "improper" energy theorems. He asked Noether to make the guess precise. Klein, working the same question in 1916, reported to Hilbert that "Frl. Noether advised me continually throughout my work, and it was really only through her that I was led to the material presented in this letter," and that when he showed her his result she told him she had derived it a year earlier and written it up.

Her answer was *Invariante Variationsprobleme*, presented to the Göttingen Royal Society on 26 July 1918 by Felix Klein, because she was not a member and could not present it herself. It proves two theorems.

The first is the one every physicist knows as **Noether's theorem**. Take any system whose laws come from a principle of least action, which includes all of mechanics, electromagnetism and everything since. If the laws are unchanged by some continuous family of transformations, there is a quantity the system conserves, and the theorem tells you how to write it down. Shift the whole experiment sideways and nothing changes: momentum is conserved. Run it an hour later and nothing changes: energy is conserved. Turn it round: angular momentum is conserved. Before 1918 those three conservation laws were three separate facts about the world. After 1918 they were three instances of one fact, and the fact was about *symmetry*. Every conservation law in physics, including the ones for electric charge and the ones discovered in particle physics fifty years later, is a symmetry in disguise.

The second theorem is the one Hilbert had asked for. When the symmetry family is not a few knobs, like shifts and rotations, but infinitely many, like the freedom to relabel every point of space-time with whatever coordinates you please, the conservation laws become identities: true automatically, and therefore empty as physics. That is exactly the situation in general relativity. Noether proved that in any theory with that kind of freedom the energy theorem is "improper" in Hilbert's sense, and she closed the paper with a section titled, in her plain way, *A Hilbertian Assertion*: "From the foregoing, finally, we also obtain the proof of a Hilbertian assertion about the connection of the failure of laws of conservation of energy proper with general relativity." Nina Byers, who traced the history, calls this a notable understatement. Hilbert credited her in print in 1924, and general relativity has lived with improper energy ever since: gravitational energy has no address, only a total.

> `noether-symmetry-check.py`, in this folder, integrates two particles joined by a spring through three worlds and reports how far the total momentum and the total energy wander. With only the spring, no place and no moment is special, and both drift by less than one part in a billion. Put a hill under the particles and the world has a special place: momentum drifts by a factor of ten while energy stays put. Make the spring stiffen with time and the world has a special moment: energy drifts by a factor of two while momentum stays put. Each symmetry you break loses exactly its own conserved quantity and no other. That is the theorem's fingerprint, and the reason it feels less like a formula than like a law about laws.

### Act IV — *Der Noether* (1919–1932)

The Weimar republic changed the rules, and in 1919 she was allowed the *Habilitation* she had been refused in 1915; she chose the invariant paper as her thesis, noting in her application that it was "an outgrowth of my assistance to Klein and Hilbert in their work on Einstein's general theory of relativity." In 1922 the Prussian ministry granted her the title of *nicht beamteter ausserordentlicher Professor*, an extraordinary professor without a civil-service post, which is to say a professor without a salary. Only in 1923, at forty-one, did a teaching contract in algebra bring her first pay.

By then she had left physics. *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen* (1921) did to algebra what she had wanted to do since Gordan: it threw away the formulas and kept the structure. A ring, an ideal, a chain of ideals that must stop growing: from a handful of axioms she rebuilt the theory of numbers and polynomials at a level of generality that made the old calculations look like counting on fingers. Rings that obey her chain condition are called Noetherian to this day. Van der Waerden wrote her lectures up as *Moderne Algebra* (1930), the textbook that taught the twentieth century its algebra, and the young men who filled her seminar, the *Noether boys*, carried the method into every corner of mathematics.

She was, by every account, loud, untidy, generous and happy. Her lectures were not polished performances but thinking aloud, and students who wanted a tidy set of notes fled; those who stayed got the subject being made in front of them. She could not supervise doctorates in her own name, so more than a dozen were formally Landau's or others'. Alexandrov, who came from Moscow in 1923 and became her close friend, called her *der Noether*, and Weyl explained the joke at her funeral: "in Göttingen we jokingly, but reverentially, spoke of you in the masculine." She spent the winter of 1928–29 lecturing in Moscow, admired Soviet science out loud, and was evicted from her lodgings after student leaders complained of living with "a Marxist-leaning Jewess". In September 1932 she gave a plenary address to the International Congress of Mathematicians in Zürich, and shared that year's Ackermann–Teubner prize with Emil Artin. She was fifty, and at the top of the subject she had made.

### Act V — The seminar in her flat (1933)

The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service was passed on 7 April 1933. Within the month Noether, who was Jewish, was dismissed from Göttingen without pension or compensation. She wrote to Helmut Hasse on 10 May: "Many thanks for your dear compassionate letter! I must say, though, that this thing is much less terrible for me than it is for many others. At least I have a small inheritance (I was never entitled to a pension anyway) which allows me to sit back for a while and see."

Then she carried on. The students came to her flat, and the seminar on class field theory continued in her sitting room through the summer. One of them turned up in the brown uniform of the SA; she showed no sign of agitation and, by the accounts that survive, laughed about it afterwards. Weyl, who spent that "stormy summer" in Göttingen with her, said at her funeral what he had seen: "your heart knew no malice; you did not believe in evil, indeed it never occurred to you that it could play a role in the affairs of man."

![[emmy-noether-flat-comic.png|640]]

That is the choice the whole story turns on. The state had removed her name from the university, as the faculty had in 1915. Both times she kept doing the thing under whatever name, in whatever room, was left.

### Act VI — The men's university (1933–1935)

Bryn Mawr, a women's college outside Philadelphia, offered a one-year visiting professorship, funded by the Rockefeller Foundation and the committee set up to place dismissed German scholars. She sailed on the *Bremen* in October 1933. From February 1934 she also lectured weekly at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, and wrote to Hasse that "Princeton will receive its first algebraic treatment this winter, and a thorough one at that," while noting that she had "already driven a few of them away with my approach." Of Princeton University itself she said she was not welcome at "the men's university, where nothing female is admitted."

In the summer of 1934 she went back to Germany once, to see her brother and to visit Artin in Hamburg. Artin's wife remembered the ride on the Hamburg underground: Emmy and Artin talking algebra at full volume in a crowded carriage, and the words that kept coming out were *Ideal*, *Führer*, *Gruppe*, *Untergruppe*, every one of them a technical term and every one of them, in 1934, a political one. "The whole car suddenly started pricking up their ears. And I was frightened to death. But Emmy was completely oblivious, and she talked very loudly and very excitedly, and got louder and louder."

She returned to Bryn Mawr for a second year. In April 1935 doctors found a tumour; the operation seemed to go well, and on the fourth day she collapsed with a high fever and died. She was fifty-three. Einstein wrote to the *New York Times*: "In the judgment of the most competent living mathematicians, Fräulein Noether was the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced since the higher education of women began." Weyl, at the memorial: "in Göttingen we jokingly, but reverentially, spoke of you in the masculine." Her ashes are under the cloisters of the Bryn Mawr library.

## Cultural ripples

- **Physics stopped listing conservation laws and started deriving them.** After 1918 a physicist who finds a new conserved quantity asks what symmetry it comes from, and one who postulates a new symmetry knows what must be conserved. The conservation of electric charge is a symmetry; the quantum numbers of particle physics are symmetries; the gauge theories that describe every force but gravity were built by choosing a symmetry group and reading off the rest. Noether's theorem is the grammar of that whole enterprise, and it came from a woman answering a question about energy that Hilbert could not settle.
- **Mathematics learned to argue from structure.** The style of *Moderne Algebra*, in which you name a few axioms and prove everything from them, is now simply what mathematics looks like. Bourbaki, category theory and the abstract turn of the twentieth century all descend from her seminar. The name "Noetherian" is attached to rings, modules, spaces and schemes, and a working algebraist uses it several times a day without thinking of a person.
- **The exclusion cost more than it saved.** The faculty that would not license her in 1915 delayed nothing but her salary. Göttingen's mathematics, the best in the world in 1932, was gone within a year of her dismissal, along with Courant, Landau, Weyl and most of the rest; the men who stayed inherited an empty building. Hilbert, asked at a dinner by the Nazi education minister how mathematics at Göttingen was now that it had been freed of Jewish influence, is reported to have answered: "Mathematics in Göttingen? There is really none any more."

## The lesson

1. **Ask what does not change.** Noether's theorem is the mathematical form of the hunter's question that runs through this vault, *what is invariant here?* A symmetry is a change that makes no difference, and the theorem says that every such change hides a quantity that is constant. Look for the invariant first and the conservation law is a consequence, not a separate thing to memorise.
2. **Structure beats calculation.** Her thesis computed 331 invariants by hand and she called it crap. Her mature work proved that the invariants must exist and stopped there. The lesson is not that calculation is beneath you; it is that a calculation you cannot explain is not yet understanding, and that the explanation is usually a structure.
3. **The room does not decide the work.** Under Hilbert's name, without pay, in a sitting room with a brownshirt on the sofa, at a college that took her when the country would not: the mathematics was the same in every room. That is not a claim that injustice does not matter. It is a claim about where the work lives.

> [!info] Receipt for the brave: the theorem in one line
> Recall that in Lagrangian mechanics the motion makes the action $S = \int L(q, \dot q, t)\,\text{d}t$ stationary, and the equation of motion is $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q}$. Suppose the laws do not care where the origin is: $L$ is unchanged when $q \to q + \varepsilon$ for every constant $\varepsilon$. Then $\partial L / \partial q = 0$, so the equation of motion reads $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} = 0$, and $p = \partial L/\partial \dot q$, the momentum, is constant. Translation symmetry gives momentum conservation in one line. Rotation symmetry gives angular momentum the same way, and time-shift symmetry gives energy, with one more line of work. Noether's paper does this for *any* continuous symmetry of *any* action, and then, in the second theorem, for symmetries with infinitely many parameters, where the conserved quantity degenerates into an identity.
