# Franklin's Coin Flip 富兰克林掷硬币

> *Every circuit diagram on Earth carries a small, deliberate lie: the current arrow points one way, and the electrons march the other. Ask why, and the trail leads back to a retired printer in Philadelphia who, in 1747, faced a fifty-fifty question that could not be checked for another hundred and fifty years — guessed — and guessed wrong. By the time nature revealed the answer, his bookkeeping was carved into every equation, instrument, and textbook in the world. So physics kept the sign, and every student since has been taught the flip. This is the story of the most durable wrong guess in science — and of why, on the science underneath it, Franklin was magnificently right.*

![[franklin-coin-flip-comic.png|697]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Benjamin Franklin** (1706–1790) — runaway printer's apprentice, publisher, retired tradesman turned natural philosopher. Kept the books of the universe the way he kept a print shop's ledger: in credits and debits.
- **Charles du Fay** (1698–1739) — French chemist who found there were *two* electricities, and named them for glass and amber.
- **Peter Collinson** (1694–1768) — London merchant whose gift of a glass tube to a Philadelphia library detonated the whole affair.
- **Georg Wilhelm Richmann** (1711–1753) — the physicist in St Petersburg who paid the full price of the new science.
- **J. J. Thomson** (1856–1940) — the man who, in 1897, finally turned the coin over.

## Act I — The century of the parlour spark (1730s–1746)

In the mid-1700s electricity was **entertainment**. Showmen rubbed glass tubes and made feathers dance; Stephen Gray suspended an orphan boy from silk cords, charged him, and drew sparks from his nose before paying audiences; the Abbé Nollet, for the amusement of King Louis XV, sent a Leyden jar's discharge through 180 royal guardsmen holding hands, and later through a mile-long chain of monks — who leapt, in the perfect synchrony their order had always aspired to, exactly at once.

The **Leyden jar** (1745–46) had raised the stakes: a water-filled glass jar that could *store* the mysterious virtue and release it in one violent snap. Nobody knew what it stored. The reigning theory was du Fay's: there were **two** electricities — *vitreous* (from rubbed glass) and *resinous* (from rubbed amber) — like repelling like, unlike attracting. Two fluids, two names, no arithmetic.

In 1746 Peter Collinson, a Quaker merchant in London, shipped a glass tube and some instructions to the Library Company of Philadelphia — a subscription library founded by a local printer who believed ordinary tradesmen should read. The printer in question had just retired from business at forty-two, rich, restless, and looking for a project.

## Act II — The bookkeeper picks up the tube (1746–1747)

Franklin's whole life had been an apprenticeship in *systems*. The Boston boy who built himself swim paddles at eleven; the fifteen-year-old secretly writing his brother's newspaper under the name of a fictional widow; the seventeen-year-old runaway who walked into Philadelphia with three bread rolls and built a printing empire; the civic engineer of fire brigades, libraries, and a university. He was not a mathematician — he read no calculus — but he had spent thirty years keeping **ledgers**, and a ledger teaches you one deep thing: *nothing appears from nowhere; every credit is somebody's debit.*

![[franklin-apprenticeship-comic.png|640]]

He took Collinson's tube and, with three friends, essentially vanished into experiment. "I never was before engaged in any study that so totally engrossed my attention and my time," he wrote — electricity had eaten the most organised man in America.

And the ledger instinct paid off almost immediately.

## Act III — One fluid, and the guess (1747)

Franklin's proposal, mailed to Collinson in 1747, replaced du Fay's two fluids with **one**. There is a single "electrical fire," present in all matter. Rubbing doesn't *create* it — rubbing **transfers** it: one body gains exactly what the other loses. A body with a surplus he called **positive**, or *plus*; a body with a deficit, **negative**, or *minus*. Bring a plus body to a minus body and the fire flows until the books balance. The Leyden jar stores equal and opposite entries — the inside credited, the outside debited, the glass holding the accounts apart.

Notice what this is: **the conservation of charge** — the first conservation law of the modern kind, discovered not with equations but with double-entry bookkeeping. It explained the jar, unified attraction and repulsion, and turned electricity from taxonomy into *arithmetic*: charges now added and cancelled like numbers. Europe was electrified in both senses; his collected letters became one of the century's scientific bestsellers.

But the theory forced a question with no experiment attached. The fluid is invisible. When you rub glass with silk, *which one ends up with the surplus?* The observable facts — who attracts whom — are perfectly symmetric under swapping every + for −. Franklin had to pick, so he picked: **the glass gains; glass is positive.**

It was a fair coin flip. It stayed in the air for a hundred and fifty years.

## Act IV — Stealing the fire, and burying the martyr (1750–1770s)

The same one-fluid reasoning drove Franklin to his famous conjecture: lightning is not divine artillery but the parlour spark at planetary scale — and it should be *drawable*, like any charge, by a grounded point. He published the design for the experiment: a sentry box, an insulated stand, a tall iron rod, a storm.

Here the honest record complicates the legend. The experiment was first performed **in France** — by Dalibard at Marly-la-Ville in May 1752, following Franklin's published instructions — so lightning was formally domesticated by Frenchmen executing an American's recipe. The **kite** came later that summer, if it came at all as described: the primary account was written fifteen years afterward by Priestley from Franklin's telling, Franklin's own newspaper report was brief and oddly third-person, and some historians doubt the canonical scene outright. What no one doubts is the danger: in 1753 **Georg Richmann**, replicating rod experiments in St Petersburg, was struck dead at his apparatus — electricity's first experimental martyr, his death engraved and circulated across Europe as both warning and proof.

Out of the science came the first electrical *technology*: the **lightning rod**. And with it, the first physics culture-war. Churches resisted — lightning was Providence, and the traditional defence was ringing consecrated bells, a practice that killed ringers by the dozen (the tower is tall, the rope is wet). Gunpowder stored in church vaults made it worse: when lightning found the magazine of San Nazaro in Brescia in 1769, the blast levelled a district and killed thousands. The rods went up. Even comedy joined in: during the American Revolution, George III's court championed *blunt-tipped* rods against Franklin's pointed ones — the tip's shape had become a question of loyalty to the Crown, to the despair of the Royal Society, whose president, Sir John Pringle, reputedly told his king that *the laws of nature are not changeable at royal pleasure* — and soon found himself a former president. Franklin, characteristically, patented none of it: having profited from the inventions of others, he wrote, we should be glad to serve others by our own, "freely and generously."

## Act V — The coin lands, 150 years late (1874–1897)

Through the nineteenth century, Franklin's bookkeeping conquered everything. Ohm, Ampère, Faraday's dynamos, the telegraph, Maxwell's equations, the first power grids — all written with current flowing from + to −, the direction of Franklin's imagined surplus. The sign convention wasn't a footnote anymore; it was the load-bearing notation of an industrial civilisation.

Then the coin came down. Cathode rays — the glow in evacuated tubes — bent in electric and magnetic fields, and in 1897 **J. J. Thomson** measured their charge-to-mass ratio and showed they were a *particle*, two thousand times lighter than hydrogen, present in all matter. The universal carrier of electricity in metals had been found. Its charge was **negative**.

Unfold what that means for the original experiment: when glass is rubbed with silk, nothing flows *into* the glass at all — **electrons leave it.** Franklin's "surplus of fire" is actually a deficit of electrons. The thing that moves in every wire moves *against* his arrow. He had called the coin, and it had landed on the other side.

And it was far too late to care. Flipping the convention in 1897 would have meant rewriting every equation, relabelling every terminal, re-teaching every engineer on Earth — to gain nothing, because physics is perfectly symmetric in the choice. So the arrow stayed. We call it **conventional current**, teach the electron's opposite march in the next breath, and the [[Lorentz Force|left hand]] quietly serves the arrow rather than the particle. One consolation arrived by the same sideways law: the Hall effect showed that in some materials — p-type semiconductors, electrolytes, your own nerves — *positive carriers genuinely do flow*, and Franklin's arrow tells the literal truth. Sometimes the fiction isn't even fiction.

## Act VI — The moral of the ledger

Two things are true at once, and the story is only honest with both.

Franklin **lost the coin flip**: the mobile charge in a wire is the one he called minus, and every student forever after pays a small tax of confusion — the flipped hand, the double arrow, the "conventional vs electron flow" exam question. A convention, once the network locks in, is effectively indestructible; it can be wrong-footed by nature and still outlive every empire that used it. The keyboard you typed on today has the same property.

And Franklin **won the physics**: one fluid, not two; conservation, not creation; charge as a *signed quantity* that adds and cancels — the plus and minus themselves, lifted from a printer's ledger into the permanent language of science. The guess was arbitrary and the insight was not, and nature punished the first while keeping the second entire. The electron, for its part, does not care what we call it.

## Honest edges

- **The kite is shaky as told.** The canonical scene rests on Priestley's account written fifteen years later; Franklin's own contemporary report was terse and third-person; serious historians have argued it may not have happened as described, or at all. The *sentry-box* experiment at Marly (Dalibard, May 1752) is the securely documented first proof — and it was French execution of Franklin's published design.
- **Richmann's death was real** and stands for the many electricians the era injured; the popular engraving of his final moment did as much as any paper to teach Europe that this force was not a toy.
- **Franklin didn't invent charge, or the terms from thin air:** du Fay's two-fluid work was excellent taxonomy, and Watson in England reached conservation-flavoured ideas independently. Franklin's letters won partly on clarity and reach — the network effects that later froze his sign also amplified his science.
- **"Wrong" needs care:** the choice of sign was pure convention — there was no fact of the matter to get wrong *in 1747*. The bad luck is strictly practical: the dominant carriers in metals turned out to sit on the minus side of his ledger, so the notation and the mechanism disagree in exactly the place students live (wires). In electrolytes and p-type semiconductors the arrow and the carriers agree.
- **The slave-owning printer:** Franklin owned enslaved people for decades and printed ads for slave sales; late in life he became president of an abolition society and petitioned Congress against slavery. Both facts stay.

## Cultural ripples

- **"Positive" and "negative"** — along with *battery*, *charge*, *conductor*, *plus*, *minus*, *electrician* — are Franklin's coinages or popularisations: an accounting metaphor that became the universal language of a science he never lived to see completed.
- **The pointed-vs-blunt rod farce** is the ancestor of every politicised science fight since: the physics was indifferent; the tips were tribal badges.
- **QWERTY physics:** the locked-in wrong-footed convention became the textbook example (alongside the keyboard itself, and driving on the left) of how standards outlive their justifications — a network-effects lesson wearing an 18th-century wig.
