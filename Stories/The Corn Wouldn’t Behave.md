# The Corn Wouldn’t Behave 不听话的玉米

> *The spots were not spoiling the experiment. They were telling her what had happened.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Barbara McClintock** (1902–1992) — geneticist, exceptionally good at recognising maize chromosomes, stubborn about protecting her attention.
- **Harriet Creighton** (1909–2004) — her Cornell collaborator in the experiment that connected inherited traits to physical chromosome exchange.
- **Milislav Demerec** (1895–1966) — the geneticist who helped make a lasting research home for her at Cold Spring Harbor.
- **A spotted maize kernel** — several generations of cells, keeping a coloured record of their history.

## 中文锚点

你有没有认真观察过一个小毛病，比如耳机总在某个动作后断音？别人说“偶尔这样很正常”，你却发现，每次出问题都有一点相似之处。麦克林托克看玉米上的斑点，也是这样不肯轻轻放过：这块为什么大，那块为什么小，它们究竟经历过什么？她的坚持不只是“我相信自己”，而是愿意把别人嫌麻烦的细节一遍遍追下去，让解释经得起下一次观察。有时候，真正值得改的不是那个“不听话”的结果，而是我们以为早已弄明白的规则。

## Act I — Stockholm will have to walk

On 10 October 1983, Barbara McClintock learned that she had won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. She was eighty-one. According to Cold Spring Harbor’s account, the news reached her through colleagues and students: she had chosen to do without a telephone. Reporters arrived. She went out to collect black walnuts, and the reporters followed. [1]

This is an excellent way to receive a Nobel Prize.

![[mcclintock-walnuts-comic.png|900]]

*An imagined comic built around the documented walnut walk. The dialogue and the journalists helping are inventions; they are not quotations or additional historical claims.*

The joke has a serious centre. The world had suddenly decided that her work deserved its undivided attention. She had spent decades deciding that it deserved hers.

To understand what she had been looking at, we need to leave the prize ceremony and go back to the corn.

## Act II — A girl who wanted to find out

As a child, McClintock spent time with an aunt and uncle. She later remembered accompanying her fish-dealer uncle on his deliveries and watching him struggle with his motor vehicle. The biographical memoir by geneticist Nina Fedoroff also records a girl content with solitary thinking and keen on skating. These are recollections, not evidence that a future discovery was inevitable. [2]

Still, the adult and the child are recognisably related: something does not work as expected, and she wants to stay with it.

Her family’s feelings about education were less straightforward. Her mother worried about the life a highly educated woman might face; her father supported her going to university. McClintock entered Cornell’s College of Agriculture in **1919**. [3]

That date matters less than the choice. The safe future other people could imagine for her was not the future she wanted to investigate.

At Cornell, she learned to identify maize chromosomes under the microscope. By the time she completed her doctorate in 1927, she was developing a career that moved constantly between two kinds of evidence: what the chromosomes looked like and what appeared in the offspring of controlled crosses. Harriet Creighton became a friend and collaborator. [4]

A chromosome is a long DNA-containing structure carrying many genes. You cannot look down an ordinary light microscope and read its DNA letters. But visible landmarks can let you follow a chromosome or recognise a rearrangement.

Imagine tracking two almost identical suitcases. A dent on one and a red strap on the other suddenly make the journey legible.

## Act III — First, prove that the pieces exchange

In **1931**, Creighton and McClintock published an experiment using distinguishable maize chromosomes: a conspicuous knob at one end and an exchanged segment at the other supplied physical landmarks. They compared those landmarks with the inheritance of traits in the same breeding material. The exchange inferred from the traits matched an exchange visible in the chromosomes. [5]

This was **crossing-over**: corresponding chromosomes can exchange segments during the production of reproductive cells.

It is worth separating this from the discovery that comes later. Trading corresponding stretches between a pair of chromosomes is one operation. A particular genetic element changing its location is another.

The first result helped establish the physical reliability of genetic maps. The second would show why a map sometimes needed updating.

McClintock was therefore not someone who disliked orderly genetics and guessed that anything might happen. She had helped make its order visible.

## Act IV — The colour carries a history

After leaving her position at Missouri, McClintock came to Cold Spring Harbor in 1941. Demerec helped secure a place where she could continue her investigations. The work depended on growing, crossing and keeping track of plants across seasons. [1]

A later account by a scientist who knew her describes roughly **2,000 plants a year**, with records of the kernels planted, ears harvested and tassels used. Her cornfield was an experiment whose labels had to survive the summer. [6]

Now look at one pale kernel with purple patches.

**Do not begin by asking which colour the whole kernel is. Ask why neighbouring patches have different histories.**

Here is a simplified version of one mechanism. In the kernel’s pigment-producing outer endosperm layer, the **aleurone**, a genetic element inserted into a pigment-related gene can interrupt its function. If that element later leaves in a way that restores function in one cell, descendants of that cell can make pigment. [7]

An early restoration leaves time for many descendants: a large coloured sector. A later restoration leaves fewer: a small spot. The patch is a surviving family of cells. In this setting, its size gives evidence about the timing of a change. [7]

That is the little conceptual leap worth taking home. A pattern spread out across space can preserve a sequence of events in time.

Think of a document copied and passed on. Correct a typo before the copying branches, and many later copies contain the correction. Correct it near the end, and only a few do. This is a picture of inheritance through a branching history, not a claim that cells are printing presses or that patch area is an exact clock.

> [!important] Spots are evidence, not a diagnosis by themselves
> Maize colour has several genetic causes. Chromosome loss and breakage can also produce sectors. Seeing a purple spot does not, by itself, prove that an element jumped. McClintock combined the visible patterns with crosses and chromosome observations to distinguish explanations. [7, 8]

## Act V — The address changed

Her 1950 paper gives names to two crucial players: **Ds**, from *Dissociation*, and **Ac**, from *Activator*. Ds was associated with a site of chromosome breakage; its behaviour depended on Ac. Crucially, the location at which Ds activity appeared could change. [8]

The paper describes a case in which Ds activity appeared at the known position of the **C** colour locus, alongside loss of normal colour function. The chromosome could look normal at the microscope’s scale while the genetic behaviour had changed. This was not simply a smashed chromosome explaining every unusual kernel. [8]

In modern molecular terms, **Ac can supply transposase**, an enzyme involved in moving the DNA element. **Ds elements depend on that machinery** rather than supplying a functional version of their own. The Ac/Ds family includes different Ds structures and behaviours; the small teaching diagram “Ac turns Ds on” compresses a much richer system. [9]

The popular name is **jumping genes**. The more precise term is **transposable elements**: DNA sequences that can change position. Not every gene can do this, and a moving element need not be an ordinary protein-coding gene.

The discovery was made without watching a DNA fragment hop in a movie. She inferred movement from what followed it: changes in location, inheritance and expression. Molecular tools later made it possible to examine the elements themselves. [10]

This is a familiar scientific bargain. We often cannot see the event directly. We can ask whether its consequences form a pattern that competing accounts cannot reproduce.

## Act VI — Recognised, and still not understood

The easy version of the story has one lonely genius, a room full of fools and thirty years of laughter.

The record is more interesting.

McClintock had been elected to the **National Academy of Sciences in 1944**, before the work for which the Nobel Prize came. Fedoroff distinguishes recognition of her experimental ability from acceptance of the general significance of transposition. Evidence of mobile DNA in bacteria and bacteriophages later helped make that significance clear. [2]

McClintock published the 1950 paper and presented the work at the 1951 Cold Spring Harbor symposium. Understanding did not spread as quickly as she hoped. [1] The DNA Learning Center’s biography describes work that was known but poorly understood, and her frustration at communicating it. [4]

These distinctions matter. “An expert has found something real” and “this changes how we should think about genomes generally” are different claims. Someone can accept the first while missing the second.

There were also real barriers in her working life. The security she eventually found does not erase the restrictions she had encountered. Nor should those restrictions erase the colleagues, collaborators and institutional support that made sustained research possible.

The useful lesson is not **ignore everybody and trust your conviction**. Many confident people are wrong. It is to keep making the disagreement answerable: preserve the specimens, describe the crosses, trace the pattern, expose the inference to another test.

Her work deserves more than a motivational poster.

## Act VII — What the corn changed

The **1983 Nobel Prize was hers alone**, awarded “for her discovery of mobile genetic elements”. [3]

What had changed was our picture of the genome—the organism’s genetic material. A sequence can be inherited reliably without every part of its arrangement being permanently fixed. The machinery that maintains a living system and the processes that alter it coexist.

McClintock’s Nobel lecture pushed beyond the discovery itself to the genome’s responses to challenges. She distinguished organised responses to familiar kinds of stress from less predictable changes following unusual disruption. This was a research programme, not proof that an organism consciously chooses whichever mutation it needs. [11]

A changing genome is not a wish-granting genome.

The everyday doorway is still a piece of corn. When you look at a patch of colour, you can ask a richer question than “what colour is it?”: **what sequence of events would leave this pattern?** That question also belongs beside a tree’s scar, a worn-out shoe or a software log. The mechanisms differ; the habit of tracing consequences transfers.

There is a direct laboratory use, too. **Transposon tagging** deliberately uses insertion to disrupt a gene; the known inserted sequence can then help researchers locate the affected gene. The interruption becomes a handle for investigation. The phenomenon that made inheritance harder to describe became a tool for finding what a gene does. [12]

And the walnuts?

They were not merely a prop supplied by history for a good ending. Colleagues remembered McClintock gathering them for baking and sharing the results with a few fortunate people. The same biography remembers her humour and pleasure in her work. [4]

A life spent concentrating need not be a life without play.

The corn had been behaving according to its own mechanisms all along. McClintock kept looking until the explanation had room for them.
