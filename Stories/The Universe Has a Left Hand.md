# The Universe Has a Left Hand 宇宙竟然分左右

> A beautiful symmetry. A very cold experiment. An answer nobody could negotiate with.

## Cast of Characters

- **Chien-Shiung Wu 吴健雄 (1912–1997)** — Columbia experimental physicist; made beta decay answer difficult questions.
- **Tsung-Dao Lee 李政道** and **Chen Ning Yang 杨振宁** — the theorists who asked whether a supposedly universal rule had actually been tested where it mattered.
- **Ernest Ambler, Raymond Hayward, Dale Hoppes and Ralph Hudson** — Wu's four collaborators at the US National Bureau of Standards (NBS, now NIST); the experiment needed their low-temperature and radiation-measurement expertise.

## 中文锚点

一屋子人都说“当然是这样”，你还会不会举手问一句：“可是谁试过呢？”难的往往是那一刻：也许真是自己没听懂，问出来还挺丢脸。吴健雄却把一个被当成定论的答案，变成了仪器可以回答的问题。她和同事把钴原子核冷却到极低温，让它们的自旋尽量朝同一个方向排列，再去数它们放出的电子，结果并没有出现大家期待的左右对称。她的勇气不在于认定自己一定比别人聪明，而在于肯下功夫，让答案不再取决于谁说得更有把握。

## The Story

### 1. Before the laboratory, a door that might not open

The young Wu had a place at university, and a worry.

Her school had trained her to become a teacher. It had offered little advanced mathematics or science. Perhaps the university would admit her only to education. A place was an opportunity; it might also be a track she could not leave.

Her father thought otherwise. He brought home algebra and chemistry books and encouraged her to catch up. She entered mathematics, then asked to transfer into physics. Decades later, in an autobiographical speech, she remembered how close she might have come to missing that turn altogether. This is Wu's own account, not a childhood anecdote invented to explain her later greatness. [1]

There is a familiar kind of encouragement that says *you can be anything*. This one came with books and work to do.

By 1956, Wu was a specialist in beta decay at Columbia. She had earned her doctorate at Berkeley and worked on radiation detectors during the Manhattan Project. The person who would test a great theoretical assumption had spent years learning how to distinguish a particle's behaviour from an instrument's bad habits. [2]

### 2. An obvious law with an unasked question

Imagine filming a physical experiment and reversing its handedness. Would the reflected experiment obey the same laws?

The claim is stronger than “some objects look symmetrical.” Your left glove and right glove are different objects. **Parity conservation** says that the laws do not prefer one handed version of a complete physical process over the other.

For precision, physicists use *space inversion*: replace every position $(x,y,z)$ with $(-x,-y,-z)$. A reflection in one plane differs from this by a rotation; when the laws also respect rotations, the mirror language captures the same symmetry question.

Lee and Yang investigated a puzzle in particle decays. In their 1956 paper, they separated a reassuring belief into the experiments that actually supported it. Evidence for parity conservation in strong and electromagnetic interactions did not settle the question for the **weak interaction**, responsible for beta decay. They proposed tests that could. [3]

This was not a declaration that evidence did not matter. It was a demand to stop lending one set of evidence to another question.

### 3. To ask a nucleus about direction, first give it one

In beta-minus decay, a nucleus emits an electron and an antineutrino as a neutron changes into a proton. But an ordinary radioactive sample contains nuclei with no common spin orientation. Any preference measured relative to an individual nucleus's spin is washed out when all those directions are mixed together.

Think of asking whether people tend to leave a theatre by the door on their right, while standing outside a hundred theatres facing a hundred different ways. Counting eastbound pedestrians will not answer the question.

**The nuclei had to share an orientation.** Spin is intrinsic quantum angular momentum, not a miniature ball visibly turning. Nevertheless, a nucleus's spin can have an orientation relative to an applied magnetic field.

Wu's collaboration used cobalt-60 in a crystal, brought to roughly **0.003 kelvin**. At such temperatures, thermal disorder was weak enough for a useful nuclear alignment. The cooling used *adiabatic demagnetisation*: a magnetic material was thermally isolated and its field reduced so that the magnetic degrees of freedom could absorb energy from the rest of the material. A separate arrangement provided the field for orienting the nuclei. Making this work was a substantial low-temperature experiment before anyone counted a decisive electron. [4]

Wu had planned an overseas trip with her husband. According to the recollections assembled by NIST, she abandoned it to pursue the test. She worked on beta detection with Hayward and Hoppes; Ambler and Hudson brought the cryogenic expertise. Their leaking glass joints were sealed with glycerin and household detergent. A failed seal could cost hours of warming, repair and cooling. [5]

A law of nature was being questioned by people who also had to make the plumbing behave.

### 4. Why an electron choosing a direction breaks the mirror

There is a subtlety here. **A spin direction does not transform like an electron's direction of travel.**

For ordinary orbital angular momentum, the reason is visible in the formula from [[Torque]]:

$$
\mathbf J=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p.
$$

Under space inversion, both position and momentum reverse, so

$$
(-\mathbf r)\times(-\mathbf p)=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p.
$$

Angular momentum stays the same; the two minus signs cancel. Intrinsic spin has the same transformation behaviour, although it does not come from a little orbit inside the particle.

So invert an event in which an electron travels **opposite** the nuclear spin. The spin stays put and the electron's momentum reverses: the inverted event has the electron travelling **along** the spin.

If parity were conserved, the decay would give those two corresponding directions equal rates. A preference for one over the other would make the decay distinguish its inverted counterpart.

That is the question. Not whether more electrons happen to hit the upper counter, not whether the laboratory has an “up,” and not whether the apparatus itself is lopsided. **Does emission prefer one direction relative to the nuclear spin, after the apparatus has been checked?**

### 5. The result had to survive being turned around

The original paper records a preference: electrons were emitted **opposite the nuclear spin**. Reversing the polarising field reversed the observed beta-count asymmetry. As the sample warmed and its nuclear alignment faded, the difference disappeared; gamma-ray measurements separately tracked the loss of alignment. [6]

That last part matters. Cooling an instrument can change its behaviour. A magnetic field can interfere with a detector. Merely obtaining two different numbers would have left room for several boring explanations.

The team tested counter stability and used additional source arrangements to check possible magnetic deflection of electrons. Their paper also discussed scattering and the limits on extracting a precise asymmetry parameter. The conclusion did not require pretending that every instrumental detail had vanished. It required showing that the directional preference belonged to the decay. [6]

The NBS notebook page for **27 December 1956** records an early successful run. Hudson later added the wonderfully blunt annotation:

> **“PARITY NOT CONSERVED!”** [4]

The later addition matters: this is not a claim that someone shouted those words at the instant of the first count.

By the early hours of 9 January, according to Hudson's later recollection, the group was sufficiently convinced to celebrate. He brought wine; they drank from paper cups. [5]

The paper, by all five experimentalists, reached *Physical Review* on **15 January 1957**. The following paper by Richard Garwin, Leon Lederman and Marcel Weinrich reported another parity test; it explicitly acknowledged hearing about Wu's preliminary result before their own experiment began. [6]

One cherished rule had become a question with an experimental answer.

### 6. Five experimenters, two laureates

The 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics went to **Lee and Yang**. Their contribution was real: they had exposed the missing test and proposed ways to perform it. The Nobel presentation speech also explicitly credited Wu and her collaborators' experiment. None of the five experimentalists shared the award. [7]

Those facts should be allowed to sit beside each other. Erasing the theorists to repair the omission would repeat the same bad habit of reducing a collaboration to whichever name serves the story. Nor do the award citation and ceremony speech, by themselves, establish the committee's private reasons for leaving the experimental work unawarded.

Wu's career did not stop at that absence. She became a full professor of physics at Columbia, wrote an influential book on beta decay, and continued research that included the physical behaviour of haemoglobin. [2]

In the autobiographical speech, she returned to women's access to science. More women entering universities was not the same as women reaching senior academic positions. Her optimism did not make her stop noticing the difference. [1]

The child had needed an open door. The adult could see that getting through one did not mean all the others had opened.

## Cultural ripples

The title is a provocation, not a claim that every process in the universe favours the left. Ordinary mirrors still work. Electromagnetism did not suddenly cease to respect parity. **The weak interaction defeated a claim of universality.**

The lasting consequence is a changed standard for a symmetry: attractive is a reason to investigate it; experiment decides whether nature obeys it. Lee and Yang made the gap in knowledge explicit. Wu, Ambler, Hayward, Hoppes and Hudson built a way to force an answer.

There is also a practical connection to anyone who has debugged a program, repaired a bicycle, or tried to work out why a measurement keeps drifting. The illuminating moment is often a reversal: change one thing, keep the rest under control, and see whether the effect follows. Here the alignment reversed, the count difference reversed, and warming erased both alignment and asymmetry. Those interventions made the result interpretable.

The extraordinary result came from a recognisable kind of work: making an explanation earn your trust.
