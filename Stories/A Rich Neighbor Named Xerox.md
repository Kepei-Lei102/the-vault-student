# A Rich Neighbor Named Xerox 一位叫施乐的有钱邻居

> *Every window you have ever dragged, every menu you have ever pulled down, every file you have ever double-clicked, descends from one laboratory that built the future and could not bring itself to sell it — and from two young men who each, in their own way, walked out with the television.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Xerox PARC** — the Palo Alto Research Center, founded 1970: the West Coast's answer to Bell Labs, funded by photocopier money, staffed by people who thought computers should be personal.
- **Alan Kay** (b. 1940) — PARC's visionary; Smalltalk, the Dynabook, and the line about inventing the future.
- **Larry Tesler** (1945–2020) and **Adele Goldberg** (b. 1945) — the researchers who gave the demo; one of them did not want to.
- **Steve Jobs** (1955–2011) — Apple's co-founder, twenty-four years old when he bought a ticket to the future.
- **Bill Atkinson** (1951–2025) — Apple's graphics genius; the man who turned what he glimpsed into something that actually shipped.
- **Bill Gates** (b. 1955) — Microsoft's co-founder; the second thief, and the author of the comeback.
- **Andy Hertzfeld** (b. 1953) — Macintosh software engineer, in the room, and the story's narrator.
- **Xerox management** — in Connecticut, three thousand miles from the lab, selling copiers.

## 中文锚点

你今天在屏幕上拖动的每一个窗口、点开的每一个菜单、双击的每一个图标，都来自一个实验室——**施乐 PARC**（帕洛阿尔托研究中心）。1973 年，它就造出了 **Alto**：位图屏幕、鼠标、重叠的窗口、所见即所得的编辑器、以太网、面向对象的 Smalltalk 语言——**整个 21 世纪的桌面，在 1973 年就已经放在一间屋子里了**。悲剧在于，拥有它的公司是一家卖复印机的公司，看不见自己手里是什么。1979 年 12 月，24 岁的乔布斯用"让施乐以每股 10 美元买 10 万股上市前的苹果股票"换来了一场演示：他看到图形界面后"像揭开了眼前的一层纱"，在屋里蹦来蹦去，冲着施乐的人喊：**你们坐在金矿上！**——然后回去做出了 Lisa 和 Macintosh，并且**真的加了自己的发明**：下拉菜单、直接拖拽、能重叠的窗口。1983 年 11 月，微软宣布 Windows；第二天下午，乔布斯把盖茨叫来，十个苹果人围着他一个，乔布斯吼："你在抢我们的东西！"盖茨的回答成了科技史上最好的一句回嘴：**"史蒂夫，我觉得这事可以换个角度看。更像是，我们俩有个叫施乐的有钱邻居，我闯进他家想偷电视机，结果发现你已经把它偷走了。"** 后来的官司（苹果告微软，施乐告苹果）全都输了：法院裁定"图形界面"这个**想法**不受版权保护——于是它成了全人类的公共领域。两个普罗米修斯，还是两个小偷？取决于你怎么看。但有一条教训是确定的：**发明和交付是两种不同的才能，而历史付钱给真正把东西交到你手上的人。**

## The Story

### Act I — The lab that built tomorrow and left it in the building

Xerox founded PARC in 1970 with a mandate and a budget most companies would kill for: photocopier profits, no product deadlines, and a brief to invent "the office of the future." The lab took the brief literally. By **1 March 1973** its researchers had a working **Alto**: a personal computer — one person, one machine, a decade before the phrase was ordinary — with a **bitmapped display of 606 × 808 pixels turned portrait, like a sheet of paper**, a **three-button mouse**, an **Ethernet** connection to every other Alto in the building, **Bravo**, the first *what-you-see-is-what-you-get* text editor, and **Smalltalk**, Alan Kay's language in which everything on the screen was an object that could be opened, moved and talked to. Overlapping windows, pop-up menus, icons, a pointer you steered with your hand: the whole desktop, in a room, in 1973. Roughly **two thousand** Altos were built and used inside Xerox and a few universities — and never sold.

Kay had said the lab's creed out loud two years earlier. By his own account it came "in a fit of passion" at an early PARC meeting in 1971: **"The best way to predict the future is to invent it."** (The honest footnote: the physicist Dennis Gabor had written in 1963 that "the future cannot be predicted, but futures can be invented" — Kay sharpened it into the sentence everyone quotes.) PARC lived by it. What it could not do was the other half: get the invention out of the building.

The company that owned the Alto sold copiers, and its headquarters was in Connecticut. When Xerox finally did ship a descendant, the **Star** of 1981, a complete office system cost up to **$100,000** — a machine for corporations, sold by a copier sales force, into a market that did not yet know it wanted a mouse. It failed. The future had been invented; it was sitting in a lab with nobody to carry it to the door. That is the victim in this story, and note that it is also the giant: no one stole anything from Xerox that Xerox was using.

### Act II — The first thief buys a ticket

In **December 1979** Apple was a year from going public and Xerox's venture arm wanted in. Steve Jobs, twenty-four, named his price: Xerox could buy **100,000 pre-IPO Apple shares at $10 — a million dollars** — if it let him and his engineers inside PARC to see what the lab was doing. Xerox agreed. It was, in the plainest sense, a paid admission ticket.

Not everyone at PARC wanted to sell it. **Adele Goldberg**, who had helped build Smalltalk, refused to give the demonstration — she saw exactly what Apple would do with it — and was overruled by her own management, which had a stock deal to honour. So **Larry Tesler** and colleagues ran the show, in two sessions, with the Apple team crowded round an Alto. Tesler remembered the audience more than the screen: Jobs, he told Walter Isaacson, was "hopping around so much I don't know how he actually saw most of the demo." Jobs kept shouting the same thing at his hosts — **"You're sitting on a gold mine!"** — and later described the afternoon as "like a veil being lifted from my eyes. I could see what the future of computing was destined to be."

Here is the confession that makes the story honest. Sixteen years later, in the 1995 interview that became *Triumph of the Nerds*, Jobs said PARC had actually shown him **three** things that day — the graphical interface, object-oriented programming, and a hundred-plus Altos networked with email — and that he "was so blinded by the first thing they showed me, which was the graphical user interface," that he barely registered the other two. The visionary saw one third of the future, and it took him a decade to notice the rest. ([[Object-Oriented Programming]] is the third he walked past; the Smalltalk objects on that screen are that card's ancestors.)

The consequences arrived fast. Apple went public on **12 December 1980** at $22 a share and closed the day at $29: Xerox's million-dollar ticket was worth nearly three million by dinner. Tesler left PARC for Apple in 1980. And **Bill Atkinson** — who by Tesler's account had barely been able to stand still either — went home and built what he *thought* he had seen. This is the part the theft narrative gets wrong, and the historian Alex Soojung-Kim Pang is blunt that the popular version is "wrong in almost every way a story can be wrong": the Alto's windows did not overlap the way Atkinson assumed they did, so he invented **regions** and the compositing that lets a dragged window slide over another; PARC's menus popped up, so Apple invented the **pull-down menu bar**; and *direct manipulation* — grabbing a file with the pointer and dragging it — was Apple's synthesis, not PARC's demo. The Lisa (1983) and the Macintosh (1984) copied the *idea* and then out-invented the original. Which is, of course, the same thing as stealing, depending on where you stand.

### Act III — The second thief, and the best line in the trade

Meanwhile Microsoft had been Apple's partner since 1982, writing applications for the unreleased Macintosh — which meant Microsoft's programmers had spent a year with a Mac prototype on their desks. In **November 1983**, at Comdex, Microsoft announced **Windows**.

Andy Hertzfeld tells what happened next, and it happened the very next afternoon. Jobs summoned Gates to Cupertino. Gates came alone; in the conference room he faced Jobs and about ten Apple people — Hertzfeld among them, and Mike Boich, the Mac's evangelist. Jobs went straight at him: **"You're ripping us off! I trusted you, and now you're stealing from us!"**

Gates, Hertzfeld says, just looked back at him and answered:

> **"Well, Steve, I think there's more than one way of looking at it. I think it's more like we both had this rich neighbor named Xerox and I broke into his house to steal the TV set and found out that you had already stolen it."**

![[rich-neighbor-two-thieves.png|640]]

It is the best comeback in the history of the trade because it is not a defence. It is a confession — *I broke in* — that turns, in the same sentence, into an indictment of the man shouting. Two thieves, one house, one television, and the second burglar arriving to find the dust outline where it stood. (Hertzfeld, an honest narrator, adds that he "might have confused two different meetings," and Boich remembers the details slightly differently; the line itself, everyone agrees on.)

### Act IV — The victim sues, the thieves sue each other, and the court gives the TV to everyone

The legal coda is where the story stops being a joke and becomes the floor the whole industry stands on.

In **1985**, with Jobs freshly ousted and Apple needing Microsoft's Word and Excel on the Macintosh, John Sculley signed a licence giving Microsoft the right to use "certain parts" of Apple's interface in **Windows 1.0**. Microsoft then shipped Windows 2.0 and 3.0, which looked a great deal more like a Mac. In **1988 Apple sued** Microsoft and Hewlett-Packard. The district court went through the interface element by element — **189** of them — and found that **179 had already been licensed** in the 1985 deal, and that most of the rest were not copyrightable at all. On **19 September 1994** the Ninth Circuit agreed: Apple, it ruled, "cannot get patent-like protection for the idea of a graphical user interface, or the idea of a desktop metaphor" under copyright law. The Supreme Court declined the case in 1995.

And the neighbor? Xerox woke up — on **15 December 1989**, nearly ten years after the demo — and sued Apple over the Macintosh. The court threw the case out in 1990: Xerox had waited too long to bring a copyright claim and was left with a weaker unfair-competition charge that failed. The victim, having finally noticed the empty television stand, was told the statute of limitations had run.

Read the two rulings together and the joke inverts. The court's answer to "who owns the desktop?" was *nobody* — the idea of windows, icons, menus and a pointer belongs to no company, and every system since has been free to use it. The thieves did not get to keep the TV. Neither did the neighbor. It was carried out into the street, and everyone has been watching it since.

## Cultural ripples

**Two Prometheus, or two thieves — the reader decides.** The honest version of the story refuses the easy villain. Nothing here was theft in law: Apple paid for its demonstration in stock, and Microsoft copied *ideas*, which the courts then ruled copyable. What both did to Xerox was worse than theft: they gave it an **accurate appraisal**. Each looked at the Alto and understood, within an afternoon, what its owner had failed to understand in a decade. Jobs said the quiet part aloud in 1996 — "Picasso had a saying, 'good artists copy, great artists steal,' and we have always been shameless about stealing great ideas" — and meant it as a boast.

**Invention and deployment are different talents, and history pays the deployer.** This is the story's twin to [[Space Travel]], told from the other coast. Bell Labs built Unix *by not asking*, on a cast-off machine, and shipped it into the world through three typists. PARC built the future with every resource a lab could want — and nobody asked *it*. The room that invents is rarely the room that ships; the second room gets the credit, the money, and the lawsuit.

**The GUI became public ground — on purpose.** The 1994 ruling is why your phone's maker, your laptop's maker and a Finnish student's free operating system could all give you windows without paying anyone: look-and-feel is not property. It is one of the few places where the law decided that an idea this large should belong to everyone, and the desktop you are reading this on is the result.

**Jobs kept score.** The man who bought a ticket to PARC and then out-invented it spent the rest of his career refusing to be the sleeping neighbor — the time-boxed promises and scheduled funerals of [[Courage]] are the discipline of someone who had watched a giant lose the future by being comfortable.
