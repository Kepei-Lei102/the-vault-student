# Aristotle to Apollo — Two Thousand Years of Wrong-Ness 从亚里士多德到阿波罗

> *"In my left hand I have a feather; in my right hand, a hammer. And I guess one of the reasons we got here today was because of a gentleman named Galileo a long time ago, who made a rather significant discovery about falling objects in gravity fields. And we thought: where would be a better place to confirm his findings than on the Moon?"*
> — **Commander David Scott**, Apollo 15, the lunar surface, **2 August 1971**.

> *"How about that! Mr Galileo was correct in his findings."*
> — Scott, three seconds later, after watching the hammer and the feather hit the regolith together.

![[aristotle-to-apollo-fact-check-comic.png|620]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Aristotle** (384–322 BC) — Tutor to Alexander the Great, founder of the Lyceum in Athens. Wrote *Physics*, *On the Heavens*, *Meteorology*. His system of natural motion — heavier objects fall faster; objects in motion need a continuous force — was the consensus view of educated Europe for *two thousand years*. His mistake was elegant: he treated air resistance and friction as if they were nothing, and built a coherent (and almost-right-on-Earth) physics on top of that omission.
- **John Philoponus** (~490–570 AD) — Alexandrian Christian philosopher and commentator on Aristotle. The first major figure to challenge Aristotle on falling bodies — wrote that *two objects of different weight do not, in fact, hit the ground at very different times*, on the basis of having watched it happen. The first recorded "look harder at the data" moment in the history of falling-body physics. He was a thousand years too early; the dominant philosophical climate buried him.
- **Jean Buridan** (~1300–1361) — Rector of the University of Paris, scholastic philosopher. Developed the theory of *impetus*: a moving body carries some quality (impetus) acquired from the agent that pushed it, which keeps it going until impetus is exhausted. *Almost momentum* — it was the conceptual ancestor that medieval Europe finally produced. Buridan was teaching this in Paris three hundred years before Galileo refined it.
- **Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642) — Pisa, Padua, Florence, Arcetri. Astronomer, mathematician, inventor of the geometric and military compass, the telescope as a scientific instrument, the experimental method as a *methodology* rather than an accident. His 1638 *Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche, intorno à due nuove scienze* — *Discourses Concerning Two New Sciences* — destroyed Aristotle on motion through experiment, geometry, and pure logic. Spent the last nine years of his life under house arrest at Arcetri, partially blind, smuggling the manuscript to Leiden because he wasn't permitted to publish in Italy.
- **Isaac Newton** (1643–1727) — *Principia Mathematica*, 1687, published 45 years after Galileo's death, formalised the inertial picture as Law I. The synthesis that swept European education and turned "Aristotle was wrong" from an open question into a closed one. See [[Newton's Laws of Motion]] for the load-bearing physics; this Story records what came before Newton was possible.
- **David Randolph Scott** (1932–) — US Air Force pilot, Mercury 7 era astronaut training class of 1963, Gemini 8 (with Neil Armstrong, 1966), Apollo 9 (Earth orbit, 1969), and **Commander of Apollo 15** (July–August 1971), the fourth Moon landing and the first "J-mission" — extended scientific exploration with the lunar rover. Performed the hammer-and-feather demonstration on the lunar surface at the end of the third moonwalk, 2 August 1971. Still alive at time of writing.

## 中文锚点

**从亚里士多德到阿波罗**: 这是一个跨越 2300 年的故事。从公元前 350 年亚里士多德写下「重的物体下落得更快」这句话开始，到 1971 年 8 月 2 日美国宇航员 David Scott 在月球表面同时丢下一把锤子和一根羽毛 (它们以完全相同的速度落到月壤上)，整整两千三百年。

故事的核心其实非常简单：亚里士多德认为「物体运动需要持续的推力」和「重的下落快」。他错了，但他错得非常自然 — 在地球上、有空气、有摩擦的世界里，他的两个错误结论看起来都完美符合日常经验：

1. 推一辆车，松手就停 — 这是「需要持续推力」
2. 一片羽毛和一块石头从同一高度释放 — 石头先到地面 — 这是「重的下落快」

**两个错误来自同一个盲点**: 亚里士多德把空气阻力 (air resistance) 和摩擦 (friction) 当成可以忽略的背景噪音。**伽利略 1638 年的飞跃就是想象「如果完全没有摩擦呢？」** — 那个理想化的思维实验。

历史上的关键节点：
- 公元前 350 年: 亚里士多德的物理学诞生
- 公元 6 世纪: 亚历山大里亚的 Philoponus 第一次正式怀疑
- 公元 14 世纪: 巴黎大学的 Buridan 发展「冲力 (impetus)」理论 — 几乎就是动量了
- 1638 年: 伽利略《关于两门新科学的对话》在荷兰出版 — 用斜面实验和纯逻辑彻底击垮亚里士多德
- 1687 年: 牛顿《自然哲学的数学原理》把这一切公式化为三大定律
- **1971 年 8 月 2 日**: David Scott 在月球上 (一个真正没有空气的地方) 做了那个完美的演示，**给两千多年的争论盖上了视觉化的印章**

这故事的迷人之处在于: 亚里士多德的错误直觉 *至今依然存在于每个新生的物理学生脑海里*。问任何一个还没学过物理的人「真空中羽毛和锤子谁先落地」，相当多的人会说锤子。两千多年的错误直觉因为太符合日常经验而牢牢扎根 — 唯一能让它*视觉化地*彻底失效的地方，是月球。

## Act I — Aristotle's System Was Beautiful, Coherent, and Wrong (~350 BC)

The thing to understand about Aristotle's physics is that *it was not stupid*. It was an internally consistent, predictively useful, philosophically deep system that explained an enormous range of everyday phenomena. That is why it lasted twenty centuries.

Aristotle's *Physics* (the book) argued that there are two kinds of motion: **natural** and **violent**.

- **Natural motion** is each element returning to its "proper place." Earth (the heavy element) falls to the centre. Water sits above earth. Air rises through water. Fire rises through air. Each of the four sublunary elements has a *natural* place; left alone, it moves toward that place.
- **Violent motion** is anything else. A thrown rock, a pushed cart, an arrow in flight. Violent motion *requires a mover*. Stop pushing, and violent motion ceases.

From this picture two empirical predictions follow:

1. **Heavier bodies fall faster** — because they contain more of the element earth, their natural motion downward is more vigorous. Aristotle wrote, in *On the Heavens*: *"The downward movement of a mass of gold or lead, or of any other body endowed with weight, is quicker in proportion to its size."*
2. **A body in violent motion needs a continuous force** — because the moment the mover stops moving it, violent motion ceases.

Both predictions *look correct on Earth*. A leaf falls more slowly than a stone. A cart stops when you stop pushing. Aristotle wrote what he saw. The data, naïvely interpreted, supported him.

What Aristotle missed — what nobody could see clearly without the right thought experiment — is that **the friction and air resistance doing the slowing-down are themselves forces**. They are doing the work that *looks like* "the natural state of motion is rest." In a perfectly frictionless, evacuated environment, a cart pushed once would coast forever and a leaf would fall at the same rate as a stone. But that environment did not exist on Aristotle's Earth, and Aristotle had no way to imagine it.

## Act II — A Thousand Years of Doubt (6th–14th centuries)

Cracks appeared early. **John Philoponus** (~490–570 AD), an Alexandrian Christian commentator on Aristotle, wrote that *he had watched two objects of different weight dropped from the same height, and they did not arrive at dramatically different times*. He reported actual observations — the first recorded empirical challenge in the falling-bodies record. Philoponus also argued against the "continuous-force" claim, proposing instead that a projectile acquired an *internal impressed force* from the thrower that decayed as the projectile moved. His ideas circulated in Byzantine and later Arab philosophy but were largely lost to the Latin West.

**The Arab philosophers** kept Philoponus-style critiques alive. Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, ~980–1037) and Abū'l-Barakāt al-Baghdādī (~1080–1165) both wrote that a moving body carried some quality from the agent that pushed it — Philoponus's "impressed force" by another name. Buried in commentaries; preserved.

**Jean Buridan** at the University of Paris in the 14th century picked the thread back up. Buridan's theory of **impetus** — that a moving body acquires a quality proportional to its mass and velocity, which persists until exhausted by resistance — is *one elaboration short of being momentum*. ($\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$ has the same dependencies; the only thing Buridan didn't have was the calculus to make impetus the time-rate-of-change relationship with force.) Buridan's student **Nicole Oresme** went further, sketching a graphical version of constant-acceleration motion using diagrams that look astonishingly like modern $v\text{-}t$ graphs.

For three hundred years before Galileo, Paris was teaching a version of mechanics that already disagreed with Aristotle on the load-bearing points. The scholastics had the conceptual machinery; what they lacked was the *experimental* turn that would let them measure what they could now describe. The fall of Constantinople in 1453, the rise of Italian humanism, and the printing press (~1450 onward) combined to push the medieval impetus tradition into the hands of someone who would test it.

> [!info] Beyond syllabus — Why didn't Buridan's impetus theory sweep Europe?
> Several reasons. (a) The scholastic university system embedded Aristotle in its core curriculum; replacing him meant replacing the whole apparatus. (b) The medieval natural philosophers were still doing *commentary* on ancient texts rather than systematic experiment — Galileo's later innovation was treating experiment as the primary source of truth rather than as illustration of doctrine. (c) Impetus was a verbal theory without a mathematical formulation; you could not *predict* anything quantitative from it. Buridan and Oresme had the *concept* but not the *equations*. The combination — experimental method + mathematical formalism — needed both Galileo and Newton, and arrived together.

## Act III — Galileo, the *Discorsi*, and the Inclined Plane (1564–1642)

Galileo Galilei was born in Pisa, taught mathematics at Pisa and Padua, became court mathematician and philosopher to the Grand Duke of Tuscany, and discovered Jupiter's four largest moons with his improved telescope in 1610. The astronomy got him both his fame and his trouble — his *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632), which argued for the Copernican (heliocentric) model, brought him before the Roman Inquisition in 1633 and ended with his confession, recantation, and house arrest at his villa in Arcetri for the rest of his life. The legendary "*Eppur si muove*" — "*and yet it moves*" — that he is said to have muttered under his breath after recanting, is **apocryphal** (no contemporary source); but the spirit of it has stuck because the story is true: he did not believe his recantation, and physics was on his side.

Under house arrest, partially blind, Galileo wrote his *most important* book. The *Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche, intorno à due nuove scienze* — *Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Concerning Two New Sciences* — was completed in 1637 and smuggled out of Italy for publication in Leiden, Netherlands, in 1638. Galileo could not publish in Italy because of the conditions of his sentence. The *Discorsi* is the book that destroyed Aristotelian motion.

The book contains three of physics's most important moves:

**(1) The inclined-plane experiments.** Galileo could not measure the time for an object to fall freely — clocks of his era weren't accurate enough. So he slowed the fall down by rolling polished bronze balls down inclined planes of varying angles, measuring time with a water clock (counting the weight of water flowing during the descent). Found: the distance covered is proportional to the square of the time, $s \propto t^2$ — the modern SUVAT relation for uniform acceleration from rest. Found: the angle of the plane changes only the constant of proportionality, not the relationship. Extrapolated to a vertical plane: gravity gives the same $s \propto t^2$ relation, with acceleration $g$. This is the genuine experimental basis. (The Newton's Laws of Motion card carries the modern version.)

**(2) The two-stones thought experiment** — pure logic, no apparatus required.

> Suppose Aristotle were right: a heavier body falls faster than a lighter body. Take two stones, one heavy, one light. Tie them together with a string. Drop them.
>
> If the heavy stone falls faster, it should drag the light one along faster than the light stone would fall alone — but the light stone, being lighter and slower-falling, should *hold the heavy stone back* and slow it down. So the combined object should fall *slower* than the heavy stone alone.
>
> But the combined object is *heavier* than the heavy stone alone. So, by Aristotle's rule, it should fall *faster* than the heavy stone alone.
>
> The combined object cannot fall both faster *and* slower than the heavy stone. Aristotle's rule contradicts itself.

This is one of the cleanest *reductio ad absurdum* arguments in the history of science. Galileo destroyed two thousand years of orthodoxy without performing any experiment at all — only by pushing the rule to its consequences.

![[aristotle-to-apollo-two-stones-comic.png|560]]

**(3) The idealisation.** The deep methodological move. Galileo asked: *what would happen with no air resistance, no friction, no obstacles?* The question itself was new. Aristotle and the medievals had treated the world as *given* — friction and resistance were part of the data. Galileo treated friction and resistance as *something to be removed in thought*, so that the underlying law could become visible. **Idealisation as a method** is what mathematical physics is built on; Galileo invented it for falling bodies. Newton then applied it to the rest of mechanics.

> [!info] The Tower of Pisa apocrypha
> The story that Galileo dropped two cannonballs of different mass from the Leaning Tower of Pisa, and that they hit the ground at the same time before a crowd of skeptical professors, is **almost certainly apocryphal**. Its origin is **Vincenzo Viviani**'s biography *Racconto istorico della vita di Galileo Galilei*, written in 1657 — fifteen years after Galileo's death. Viviani was Galileo's last student and devoted disciple. There is no contemporary account of any such demonstration in Pisa during Galileo's time there. The 1638 *Discorsi* itself does not mention it.
>
> Why has the story stuck? Because it is **legible** in a way the inclined-plane experiments are not. "Drop two things from a tall building" is something every listener can immediately picture. The inclined plane requires understanding ramps, friction, the geometric relationship between slope and acceleration. The Pisa story is the *cartoon* of Galileo's real argument; the *Discorsi* is the argument itself. The cartoon won the popular mind. The Apollo 15 demonstration in 1971 was, in a sense, a direct reenactment of the apocryphal Pisa story — performed where the experimental conditions were perfect.

Galileo died on **8 January 1642**, blind, under arrest, having seen the *Discorsi* published in Leiden by hands he had to trust because he could not himself see the proofs. Newton was born on Christmas Day of that same year (Julian calendar; 4 January 1643 by modern Gregorian reckoning) — the symbolic handover that historians since Westfall have not been able to resist mentioning. *The torch passed by less than a year.*

## Act IV — Newton Closes the Book (1687)

Newton's role in this Story is precisely formalisation. The pedagogical version of his three-laws synthesis lives in [[Newton's Laws of Motion]]; the priority question (Galileo had N1, Buridan had something like impetus, Hooke had things to say) lives in [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]].

For *this* Story, the load-bearing fact is the *Principia*'s social effect. Between 1687 and roughly 1730, the *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* swept through European universities, the Royal Society, the Académie des Sciences, and the secondary curricula that fed all three. Aristotle did not disappear instantly — Aristotelian physics was still taught in some Italian and Spanish universities into the 19th century — but the centre of gravity had moved. By 1750 a European student in a major university was being taught *Newton's laws of motion* as the framework; Aristotle was history.

The two-thousand-year argument was not lost in a final debate or a dramatic moment. It was lost the way most paradigm shifts are lost: by *attrition*. The Aristotelian professors died of old age, and the Newtonian generation that replaced them never accepted the old framework in the first place. As Max Planck observed (much later, in 1948): *"A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die, and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it."* The wrong physics held on for two thousand years because air resistance and friction *made* it look right to anyone who hadn't been taught to imagine otherwise. Once the right framework was in the textbooks, the wrong framework took two generations to flush out.

## Act V — The Moon, 2 August 1971

The most-loved demonstration of Galileo's discovery happened **329 years** after the *Discorsi* was published. Apollo 15 was the fourth crewed Moon landing, the first "J-mission" — extended scientific stay, the lunar rover, three moonwalks instead of two, geological training that had taken the crew through the Icelandic highlands and Hawaiian volcanoes for two years. Commander **David Scott**, Command Module Pilot Al Worden, and Lunar Module Pilot Jim Irwin reached the Moon on 30 July 1971, landing in the Hadley-Apennine region at the foot of the Apennine Mountains.

On the third and final moonwalk, just before re-entering the lunar module *Falcon* for the last time, Scott set up an experiment that NASA's flight planners had approved as a *public-education* demonstration. He held up a 1.32 kg aluminium geological hammer in his right gloved hand and a 0.03 kg falcon feather (a gift from the US Air Force Academy, whose mascot was the falcon — *Falcon* was also the lunar module's name) in his left.

> *"In my left hand, I have a feather; in my right hand, a hammer. And I guess one of the reasons we got here today was because of a gentleman named Galileo a long time ago, who made a rather significant discovery about falling objects in gravity fields. And we thought: where would be a better place to confirm his findings than on the Moon? And so we thought we'd try it here for you. The feather happens to be appropriately a falcon feather, for our Falcon. And I'll drop the two of them here, and hopefully they'll hit the ground at the same time."*

He let them go. The video — black-and-white, low-frame-rate, fish-eyed from the LM's surface camera — shows the two objects fall together in lunar gravity ($g_{\text{moon}} \approx 1.62~\text{m·s}^{-2}$, about $1/6$ of Earth's) and reach the surface simultaneously. There is no air. There is no resistance. Galileo's idealisation is reality.

> *"How about that! Mr Galileo was correct in his findings."*

Roughly **one billion people** watched it live on Earth, broadcast over the worldwide TV pool. NASA later donated the hammer and the feather to the Smithsonian; both are on display in the National Air and Space Museum in Washington. The original 16-millimetre film footage of the demonstration sits on the NASA YouTube channel, eleven minutes of dry technical context wrapping eight seconds of physics history.

There is something perfect about the gesture. The Moon — a place that two thousand years of Aristotelian cosmology had insisted was utterly different in nature from Earth, made of *aether* not the four sublunary elements — turned out to be the place where Aristotelian Earth-bound intuition could be most cleanly disproved. The cosmological model was wrong AND the falling-body model was wrong, and the same eight seconds of footage demolishes both. Aristotle's twin walls came down together.

## Cultural ripples — why is Aristotle still in every classroom?

Ask a class of fresh physics students: *"if I drop a bowling ball and a feather inside a vacuum chamber, which hits the floor first?"* A consistent minority — usually 20-40% across many studies — will say "bowling ball." Even after a teacher has explained the *Discorsi* argument. Even after they have watched the Apollo 15 footage on a phone. The intuition is durable.

Why? **Because every student arrives at school with daily evidence in Aristotle's favour.** They have watched feathers fall slowly all their lives. They have pushed shopping carts and watched them stop. The Aristotelian model is *trained into the perceptual system* by 5 to 18 years of lived experience in an atmosphere with friction. The Galilean idealisation — *imagine no air resistance* — requires the student to override an instinct that has been reinforced thousands of times. It is genuinely hard. The PER (Physics Education Research) community spends considerable effort just *getting the Aristotelian default out of student heads* before any other physics can be built on top.

There is a deeper reason the intuition stays so durable, and it is *not* about physics — it is about what a student can hold in their head. **Vacuum is an abstraction.** It is the absence of a thing the student has never consciously noticed in the first place. Most students do not actively perceive air; they have never *felt* it as something that exists in their classroom, that fills the space between objects, that resists motion. Air is the medium their entire sensory world has been embedded in for their whole lives — so completely that they only register it when there is a draft. When a teacher says "*imagine the air is gone*," the student tries — but the absence of an unfelt thing is not a vivid mental object. The mental image collapses back to "feather and hammer dropped in a room I have stood in," which is a room with air, which is Aristotle's room. **Galileo's idealisation is a two-step mental act: first see the air; then imagine its absence.** Most fresh students stall at step one — and so the Aristotelian default never gets challenged at all.

This compounds the "lived evidence in Aristotle's favour" problem above. The lived evidence is everywhere; the *thing the lived evidence is caused by* is invisible. Without recognising the cause, you cannot strip it away. The Apollo 15 video is a gift to teachers because it *short-circuits the whole chain*: the student sees a place with no air (the absence is made visible by the lack of dust kicked up, the absolute silence of the surface mics, the blackness of the sky in daytime), and *in that very same frame* watches two objects with very different weights land together. The two-step abstraction collapses into one image. There is no longer anything to imagine.

This is the deepest lesson of the 2000-year Story: **intuition built from data is not the same as physics built from theory**. Aristotle's mistake was being maximally faithful to what he could see. Galileo's correction required imagining what he *couldn't* see (a friction-free world). Every generation of physics students reenacts the transition. The lecture-hall version of the argument lasts six weeks; the Apollo 15 video does in eight seconds what a textbook chapter often cannot.

- Galileo Galilei, *Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche, intorno à due nuove scienze* (Leiden: Elzevir, 1638). **The book that destroyed Aristotle.** The inclined-plane experiments and the two-stones thought experiment both appear here.
- Galileo Galilei, *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632) — the book that triggered the 1633 trial; concerned with Copernican astronomy, not directly with falling bodies, but inseparable from Galileo's biography.

- **Apollo 15 Mission Audio Transcript**, 2 August 1971, NASA technical document. Scott's hammer-feather demonstration begins at approximately 167:22:17 mission elapsed time.
