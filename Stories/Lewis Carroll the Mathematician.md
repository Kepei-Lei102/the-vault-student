# Lewis Carroll the Mathematician 数学家刘易斯·卡罗尔

> *Charles Lutwidge Dodgson spent 26 years as a Christ Church Oxford lecturer in mathematics and 30 years writing toward childhood. He invented a literary architecture — the magical-world-with-its-own-internal-logic that the child navigates rather than suffers — that turned out to be exactly what every later generation of grief needed. He didn't know that. He just didn't want to leave the children's table.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Charles Lutwidge Dodgson / Lewis Carroll** (1832–1898) — Christ Church, Oxford, mathematics lecturer 1855–1881. Author of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* (1865), *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871), *The Hunting of the Snark* (1876), *Sylvie and Bruno* (1889–1893), and a dozen mathematical works under his real name. Lived in college rooms his entire adult life. Never married.
- **Alice Pleasance Liddell** (1852–1934) — middle daughter of Henry Liddell, Dean of Christ Church. Aged 10 when Carroll told her the story that became *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, on a boating trip up the Thames on 4 July 1862. She asked him afterwards to write it down.
- **Henry Liddell** (1811–1898) — Dean of Christ Church, classical philologist (co-author of the Liddell-Scott Greek-English Lexicon still in print today). Alice's father.
- **Bertrand Russell** (1872–1970) — read Alice as a child, later pointed out that the Mad Hatter's tea party is structurally a frozen-time scenario where "the table moves around them, not them around the table" — Carroll's quiet joke against the strict temporal ordering of Victorian logic.
- **Hayao Miyazaki** (b. 1941) — Japanese animator and filmmaker, *Spirited Away* (2001) and *The Boy and the Heron* (2023). The clearest modern inheritor of Carroll's magical-world architecture, and arguably the artist who pushed the architecture toward its sharpest possible application: not "wish to be a child again" but "wish to undo the loss that ended childhood."
- **Queen Victoria** (1819–1901) — supposedly so charmed by *Alice* that she requested Carroll's next book, and was sent *An Elementary Treatise on Determinants*. (The story is probably apocryphal — Carroll denied it in print — but the joke has outlived the denial.)

## 中文锚点

**刘易斯·卡罗尔 (Liú yì sī Kǎluó'ěr)** 是笔名；本名 **道奇森 (Dodgson)**，牛津 Christ Church 学院数学讲师，终身住在学院宿舍，没结过婚。他白天教数学（写了《Symbolic Logic》逻辑教科书、《Pillow Problems》睡前心算题、《Euclid and His Modern Rivals》一部为欧几里得辩护的四幕剧），晚上给学院院长 Liddell 家的三个女儿讲故事，其中一个故事在 1865 年成为了《Alice's Adventures in Wonderland》(《爱丽丝梦游仙境》)。

这张卡讲的不是「爱丽丝」的故事——而是讲卡罗尔本人。**他的杰作来自一个想留住童年的愿望**。结巴在小孩面前消失。Christ Church 的房间一直没换。72 道睡前数学题在黑暗里口算解出来。《Sylvie and Bruno》是一部成年人拜访仙境的自传体作品。爱丽丝在《Looking-Glass》里从小卒升到皇后——代价是离开棋盘。

后面的故事是文化继承：宫崎骏《千与千寻》(2001) 和《君たちはどう生きるか / 你想活出怎样的人生》(2023)、Connolly《失物之书》(2006)、Tomb Raider《古墓丽影：暗影》(2018)——每一部都从卡罗尔继承了同一套**架构**：奇幻世界有它自己的内在逻辑，孩子在其中**穿行**而不是**承受**它，旅程改变了孩子本身。卡罗尔的愿望是温和的（他童年完整、幸福，只是不想长大）；他建造的架构却恰好是后人——特别是经历了亲人离去的后人——需要的东西。

## Act I — Christ Church, the lecturer who lived in college

Charles Lutwidge Dodgson took his BA at Christ Church in 1854 and was immediately appointed to a *Studentship* — Christ Church's name for what other colleges call a Fellowship. He held it for the next 44 years. The terms were strict: lifelong tenure, with room and board in college, on three conditions. **Take holy orders, remain unmarried, and continue resident in the college.** He met all three. He was ordained deacon in 1861 (though he never took the further step to priest, and rarely preached). He never married. And he kept the same set of rooms in Tom Quad for the rest of his life, dying there in 1898 at age 65.

His official job was lecturing in mathematics. By all student accounts he was a mediocre teacher — shy, with a pronounced stutter that returned the moment he stood at a lectern, more comfortable with the printed page than the lecture hall. The mathematical work he produced was *real* — three textbooks on Euclid, a pamphlet on the theory of determinants (the one Queen Victoria was supposedly sent), a four-volume *Symbolic Logic* attempting a diagrammatic version of propositional reasoning, plus pamphlets, satires, and recreational puzzles. None of it was *great* mathematics. Most of it was good Victorian mathematics — careful, conservative, suspicious of innovation.

He was suspicious of innovation specifically: in *Euclid and His Modern Rivals* (1879), a satirical play in four acts staged entirely in a fictional college common room, Dodgson defended Euclid's *Elements* against the new geometry textbooks of the 1860s and 70s that were trying to displace it. He suspected the new texts of softening rigour. He suspected non-Euclidean geometry of being a fashionable mistake. He **rejected imaginary numbers** as logically incoherent — see [[Stories/The Argument for i]] for the four-century drama of mathematical acceptance Carroll was on the wrong side of. He was, in mathematical taste, a holdout for the geometry of 300 BC, two thousand years after Euclid wrote.

The stutter vanished around children. This is the single best-attested fact about Charles Dodgson's social life. His diaries from the 1860s, 70s, and 80s are full of long, easy conversations with the children of Christ Church faculty — the Liddell sisters first, then other faculty children as the Liddells grew up. With adults he was reserved, formal, often awkward. With seven-year-olds he was a different person — fluent, inventive, mischievous, in his element. The same shy ordained-but-unpreaching deacon who could not get through a sentence at High Table could keep a child laughing for an hour.

## Act II — The 1862 boat trip

On 4 July 1862, Dodgson, his friend Robinson Duckworth, and the three Liddell sisters — Lorina (13), Alice (10), and Edith (8) — rowed three miles up the Thames from Folly Bridge to Godstow. The day was overcast (the *romantic* "golden afternoon" Dodgson later claimed was a literary embellishment; the actual Oxford weather records show a cool, cloudy day). To entertain the children, Dodgson made up a story about a girl named Alice who fell down a rabbit hole into a world of talking animals and impossible logic.

![[lewis-carroll-golden-afternoon-comic.png|560]]

Alice Liddell, ten years old, asked him to write it down.

He wrote the manuscript longhand over the next eighteen months, illustrated it himself, and gave it to her in November 1864 as a Christmas present — *Alice's Adventures Under Ground*, a 90-page hand-lettered booklet now in the British Library. He showed the manuscript to friends; they urged publication; he expanded it for the printed version (adding the Mad Hatter and the Cheshire Cat, both absent from the original), commissioned John Tenniel to illustrate it, and published *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* under the pen name Lewis Carroll on 4 July 1865 — three years to the day after the boat trip. The book never went out of print.

What's pedagogically remarkable is how much actual mathematics is hidden inside what looks like a children's book:

- **The Mad Hatter's tea party** is a frozen-time loop. The Hatter, the March Hare, and the Dormouse are stuck at "always six o'clock" because the Hatter quarrelled with Time. The table moves around *them* (they shift seats every cup), not them around it. Bertrand Russell pointed out that this is Carroll's joke against the strict temporal ordering of Victorian logic — and at the cyclic-time idealist philosophies his Oxford colleagues had begun importing from German philosophy.
- **The Cheshire Cat** is a topological joke. The cat vanishes leaving only its smile behind — a property (smile) detached from its bearer (cat). Carroll the logician would have known this was technically incoherent (properties don't exist independently of substances in classical metaphysics) and that's exactly why it's funny.
- **Alice's size changes** ("Drink Me," "Eat Me," the caterpillar's mushroom) are scaling experiments — Carroll the mathematician thinking about geometric similarity in narrative form. The "I'll be late" anxiety when she's two inches tall and the long-table corridor is a reminder that scaling distance also scales the relative size of time.
- **The Mock Turtle's lesson plan** — *Reeling, Writhing, Ambition, Distraction, Uglification, Derision* — is a parody of contemporary English mathematics education. Carroll is mocking the way the standard curriculum had become a fixed set of rote operations rather than an exploration. (His *Tangled Tale* of 1885 was an attempt to fix this by serialising ten mathematical puzzles with student "solutions" published the following month, graded for elegance.)

## Act III — The mathematician at night

![[lewis-carroll-by-day-by-night-comic.png|620]]

The Carroll most readers don't know is the one who lay awake at night solving geometric problems in his head to ward off insomnia. He published 72 of them as *Pillow Problems* in 1893, with a preface explaining the method:

> *Almost any unwelcome subject may be effectually excluded from the mind by giving full occupation to some entirely different train of thought... The chief incentive was the desire to find some real occupation, of a unmusical kind, for the unwelcome wakeful hours.*

The problems include geometric constructions, algebraic identities, and elementary number theory. Solving them required holding diagrams in the mind without paper and grinding through coordinate calculations entirely mentally. Several of them are difficult by any standard. Pillow Problem #72 — the famous "three points chosen randomly on a sphere; what is the probability that they all lie on the same hemisphere?" — is a beautiful piece of geometric probability that anticipates the field by half a century. (Answer: $1/2$. The proof is a one-line symmetry argument once you see it.)

His other mathematical works form a curious portfolio:

- **Symbolic Logic** (1896) — a textbook for propositional logic with a graphical method (a square grid where regions represent propositions) that anticipates Venn diagrams in some respects. The vault's [[Logic]] card uses propositional connectives that Carroll was teaching in this textbook a century and a quarter ago.
- **The Game of Logic** (1887) — a board game with counters for teaching syllogistic reasoning. Marketed to schools. Did not sell well.
- **A Tangled Tale** (1885) — ten mathematical puzzles serialised in *The Monthly Packet*, with student "solutions" published in following issues and Carroll's commentary on each. The puzzles range from elementary arithmetic to non-trivial probability. The student commentary is gentle but sharp; Carroll graded by elegance, not just correctness.
- **Curiosa Mathematica** (1888, 1893) — two volumes of mathematical recreations including the famous "Doublets" word-puzzle (later renamed "word ladder" — *cold → cord → ford → form → foam → loam → warm*) that he invented as a Christmas-holiday entertainment for the Liddell children in 1879. (The puzzle is now a standard graph-theory exercise: vertices are 4-letter words, edges connect words differing by one letter, the puzzle is shortest path.)
- **Voting methods** — Carroll worked on theories of voting and election reform in three pamphlets (1873, 1874, 1876), anticipating Arrow's impossibility theorem by nearly eighty years and analysing the cyclic-preference paradox that Condorcet had discovered a century earlier. This is recreational social-choice theory, on its own merits actually serious work.

His mathematical reputation today is what it was in his own time: *minor*. He was a competent Victorian mathematician with a sharp eye for recreational problems, and he is remembered chiefly because the *other* thing he did turned out to be Alice.

## Act IV — Sylvie and Bruno, the autobiographical adult-in-fairy-world

*Sylvie and Bruno* (1889) and its sequel *Sylvie and Bruno Concluded* (1893) are the works Carroll considered most important. Almost no one reads them today. They are alternating chapters of two stories — one in a Victorian English drawing room, one in a fairyland called Outland — narrated by an unnamed adult who keeps slipping involuntarily between the two worlds. The drawing-room story is realist Victorian melodrama (the heroine, Lady Muriel, has a romantic plot). The fairyland story is whimsical-philosophical fable, occasionally interrupted by the same characters reappearing in displaced form.

The structure is autobiographical. **Carroll IS the unnamed adult narrator who keeps drifting from the adult world into a child's fantasy.** The drift is involuntary, and gentle, and slightly sad. The preface to *Sylvie and Bruno* contains one of Carroll's clearest statements of his own creative motive — that his stories are an attempt to "keep alive in the heart the joys of childhood." Sales were disappointing. Critics found the alternation tedious. The books were Carroll-the-mathematician trying to write Carroll-the-storyteller's autobiography, and the two halves didn't merge.

But the structural innovation *did* land. The "adult who slips between worlds" device became a standard architecture in 20th-century portal fantasy — the entire C. S. Lewis Narnia framing (Pevensie children stepping from wartime England through a wardrobe), Michael Ende's *Neverending Story* (Bastian reading himself into a different world), Hayao Miyazaki's *Spirited Away* (Chihiro and her parents driving through a tunnel into the spirit world) — all descend, structurally, from the alternating-chapters device that *Sylvie and Bruno* failed to make commercially viable. Carroll's last finished work was the architecture later generations would build their grief into.

## Act V — The chess game in Through the Looking-Glass

*Through the Looking-Glass* (1871), Carroll's second Alice book, is structured as a chess game. The front matter of the book reproduces a literal chess problem: White to play and win in eleven moves, with Alice as the White Pawn at d2 moving to d8 where she is promoted to Queen.

![[lewis-carroll-alice-chess.svg|640]]

The diagram in the book lists every move — Alice's, the Red Queen's, the White Knight's, the rest — and matches them to chapter events. Each chapter is one move forward by Alice, and meanwhile the other pieces are also moving according to the chess rules Carroll specifies. The game ends with Alice on d8 as queen, taking the Red Queen, and checkmating the Red King.

Read structurally, *Looking-Glass* is the story of a pawn making the eight-square promotion journey to the back rank — *at the cost of leaving the board behind*. Becoming Queen is winning the game, and winning the game is ending the game. Alice wakes up at the end, back in her Victorian drawing-room with the cats. She is a pawn no more, and the chess world is gone.

Carroll the mathematician wrote a children's book whose structural skeleton is the chess move "pawn promoted to queen on the back rank." Read with that in mind, the whole book becomes a sustained metaphor for **growing up** — every step forward through the chapter-squares brings Alice closer to queenhood, and queenhood ends Alice's relationship with the looking-glass world. To complete the journey is to leave the place.

This is the bleakest moment in Carroll's published work, and it is hidden inside a chess puzzle. **The book ends with the children's table folded up forever.** It is the same observation his *Sylvie and Bruno* was trying to make twenty years later, and it is why his late work feels so wistful: he was working out, in mathematical form, that you cannot live on a chess board forever.

## The hidden engine — wishing to be a child

The thesis at the centre of this card: **Carroll's masterpieces came out of the wish to stay a child.** The evidence is consistent.

- The stutter vanished only around children. He was fluent with seven-year-olds, halting with adults.
- He lived in college rooms his entire adult life — the same set, never upgraded to a larger residence, never set up an independent household.
- He wrote two books explicitly aimed at children, then *Sylvie and Bruno* about an adult who keeps drifting back into a child's fantasy world, then *Pillow Problems* which he solved in the dark because he could not sleep.
- He never married, and his closest documented friendships were with the daughters of Christ Church colleagues.
- His diary entries from the 1860s and 70s are *happiest* on the days he spent rowing or photographing or telling stories to the Liddell children and others.
- The chess game in *Looking-Glass* explicitly equates growing up with leaving the magical world.

The wish was *not acute*. Carroll's own childhood at Croft Rectory in Yorkshire was happy and intact — his parents lived until he was an adult, he had ten siblings he was close to throughout his life, his memories of childhood were warm rather than yearning. He did not need to *recover* a lost childhood; he just didn't want to leave the one he had. His was the gentler version of the wish.

The architecture he built turned out to be exactly what later generations needed for the harder version.

## Cultural inheritance — the architecture lives on

Carroll did not invent the magical-world story. Persephone in the underworld is older than literature. What Carroll did invent — *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* in 1865 and *Through the Looking-Glass* in 1871 — was the **architecture**:

1. **The magical world has its own internal logic.** It is not a chaotic dream-space but a coherent place with rules, and the rules can be learned. Wonderland's logic is wrong from the outside (animals talk, time stops, sizes change on command) but it is *consistent* inside the book. Alice can reason within it.
2. **The child navigates rather than suffers it.** Alice is not a victim of Wonderland; she explores it, asks questions, argues with the Caterpillar, takes tea with the Hatter, judges the Knave's trial. She is the protagonist, not the passenger.
3. **The journey changes the child.** Alice is a different person at the end of *Looking-Glass* than at the start of *Wonderland* — older, more confident, no longer crying when things go wrong. The structure is *Bildungsroman* in fairy-tale form.

This architecture is the spine of an entire genre that descends from Carroll directly:

- **Peter Pan** (J. M. Barrie, 1904) — the Neverland is Carroll's magical-world architecture with the additional theme that *some children never want to come back*. (Barrie was a personal friend of the Llewelyn Davies family the same way Carroll was a friend of the Liddells, and the parallels are unmistakable.)
- **The Chronicles of Narnia** (C. S. Lewis, 1950–1956) — wardrobe portal, internal logic of Narnia, child navigates and is changed by the journey. Lewis was an Oxford professor (the third of three referenced in this section, after Carroll and Tolkien) and explicitly grew up on Carroll.
- **The Phantom Tollbooth** (Norton Juster, 1961) — Milo passes through the tollbooth into the Lands Beyond, encounters the Mathemagician and the demons of ignorance, returns to the real world transformed. Carroll's architecture made American.
- **The Neverending Story** (Michael Ende, 1979) — Bastian reading himself into Fantastica; explicit invocation of the *Sylvie and Bruno* alternating-chapters device.
- **Bridge to Terabithia** (Katherine Paterson, 1977) — children invent a magical kingdom in the woods; the loss at the centre of the book is the first articulation of the *grief* application of Carroll's architecture.
- **Coraline** (Neil Gaiman, 2002) — Carroll's architecture as horror; the Other Mother as the predatory inversion of the welcoming magical world.
- **Pan's Labyrinth** (Guillermo del Toro, 2006) — same architecture, set in fascist Spain, where the real world is worse than the fantasy.
- **Spirited Away** (Hayao Miyazaki, 2001) — Chihiro's parents turned to pigs; she has to work in the spirit-world bathhouse to save them. Miyazaki's first explicit Carroll inheritance.
- **The Boy and the Heron** (Hayao Miyazaki, 2023) — an 82-year-old's late-period confession that he never solved the same wish Carroll never solved; Mahito grieves his mother and his great-uncle offers him a frozen, perfect world to inherit. Miyazaki is the most rigorous prosecutor of Carroll's architecture in the modern world, and *Heron* is the final statement.
- **Tomb Raider — Shadow of the Tomb Raider** (Crystal Dynamics, 2018) — Lara Croft enters Paititi, a hidden Mayan city with its own internal cosmology; *Looking-Glass*-style mirror imagery in the Xibalba sequences; Lara grows up at the cost of her childhood (the Croft Manor flashbacks bookend the journey).
- **Inception** (Christopher Nolan, 2010) — the adult version: Cobb's subconscious as a childhood-architecture he cannot quite return to.
- **His Dark Materials** (Philip Pullman, 1995–2000) — daemons + parallel-Oxford + the Subtle Knife cutting between worlds; Pullman was explicit that his trilogy is in conversation with Carroll, C. S. Lewis, and Milton.

The list is long because the architecture is genuinely foundational. Open a children's portal-fantasy novel published any time after 1900 and the structural skeleton — coherent magical world + navigating child + transformative journey — is almost certainly inherited from Carroll, sometimes through three or four generations of literary descent.

## The sharper inheritance — the wish to undo loss

The thread that runs through *The Book of Lost Things* (John Connolly, 2006), *Spirited Away* (2001), and *The Boy and the Heron* (2023) is sharper than Carroll's own engine.

In all three, the protagonist child has lost a parent — David's mother in *Lost Things*, Chihiro's parents (transformed, not dead) in *Spirited Away*, Mahito's mother in *Heron*. The fantasy world they enter is not a refuge from a happy childhood they don't want to leave. It is a place where the loss *might be undone*. The architecture has shifted from **wish to stay a child** to **wish to undo the thing that ended childhood**.

Miyazaki has been quietly prosecuting this thread across his entire career. *Castle in the Sky* (1986), *Kiki's Delivery Service* (1989), *Princess Mononoke* (1997), *Spirited Away* (2001), *Howl's Moving Castle* (2004), *The Wind Rises* (2013), and finally *The Boy and the Heron* (2023) all feature the same loss-and-magical-world structure with progressively more autobiographical urgency. *Heron* is, by Miyazaki's own statements, his late-period confession — the 82-year-old admitting he never solved the puzzle the entire body of his work was prosecuting.

Carroll did not write this version of the story. His wish was the gentler one — *I had a happy childhood and I do not wish to leave it.* But the architecture he built was load-bearing enough to carry the harder versions that came after. **He invented the rooms; later generations turned them into the place to put their grief.**

This is the deepest claim this Story makes: that a Victorian Oxford mathematics lecturer's gentle wish to stay at the children's table produced the literary architecture of every modern grief novel about lost parents. He did not know that. He could not have known. He just kept rowing the children up the Thames.

## Honest edges

The modern reader cannot encounter Charles Dodgson without encountering the modern uneasiness about him. Two things sit in the historical record and have to be looked at honestly.

**The child photography.** Carroll was an accomplished amateur photographer during the medium's first decades. Between roughly 1856 and 1880 he photographed over 3000 subjects, of which a large fraction were children — often in tableaux costume (medieval, Roman, fairy-tale), occasionally undressed, with the formal parental permission that was the photographic convention of the period. The most famous image is his 1858 photograph of Alice Liddell aged 6 dressed as a beggar girl, looking directly at the camera, slightly impudent. Victorian photography of children in costume was a widely practised artistic genre, not specific to Carroll; the Pre-Raphaelite circle, Julia Margaret Cameron, and dozens of other Victorian photographers worked in the same idiom. None of this was secret at the time, none of it scandalised contemporaries who knew the work, and Carroll burned his own photographic negatives in 1880, citing the time the hobby consumed. *The honest modern view is that the genre itself reads uncomfortably to twenty-first-century eyes for reasons that have to do with how childhood is photographed now, and that there is no contemporaneous evidence of misconduct, and that the discomfort is real and need not be argued away.*

**The Liddell rupture (June 1863).** Carroll's diary pages covering the period 27 May – 26 June 1863 are missing. They were physically removed — pages torn from the bound journal — by a member of his family after his death. The Liddell family ended their close friendship with Carroll in the same month. The conventional speculation has long been that Carroll proposed to Alice (then 11), or that her mother Lorina Liddell suspected him of inappropriate interest, but **the missing pages do not say what they said before they were removed**, and the speculation is downstream of Victorian editorial reflexes about unmarried male relatives of college girls. Karoline Leach's 1999 book *In the Shadow of the Dreamchild* argued from circumstantial evidence that Carroll was probably interested in the children's *governess* or in Lorina Liddell herself, and that the family-removed pages concealed Victorian-adult romantic embarrassment rather than anything involving Alice. *The honest modern view is that we do not know what was on those pages, that we have to be careful not to back-project modern categories onto Victorian friendships between adult men and the daughters of their colleagues, and that the rupture happened.*

The vault's general practice on biographical-edges is to state the facts, name the modern uneasiness, decline to whitewash, and decline to over-pathologize. The same pattern applies in [[Stories/Newton vs Hooke]] (Newton's vindictive pettiness toward a smaller man), [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]] (Newton's Royal Society stitch-up of Leibniz), [[Stories/The Bernoulli Family]] (Johann Bernoulli backdating *Hydraulica* to claim priority over his own son). Difficult biographies stay difficult; the Stories card surfaces the difficulty rather than smoothing it.
