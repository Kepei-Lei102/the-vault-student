# Gauss the Prodigy 高斯神童

> *His mother never learned to read. She could not write down the date of his birth — she only remembered it was a Wednesday, eight days before Ascension. So the greatest mathematician of his age sat down, invented a formula for the date of Easter, and calculated his own birthday.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777–1855) — bricklayer's son from Braunschweig; the *Princeps Mathematicorum*, "prince of mathematicians."
- **Gebhard Dietrich Gauss** (1744–1808) — his father: bricklayer, winter butcher, summer gardener, Saturday accounts-keeper. Honest, hard-handed, and unconvinced that schooling paid.
- **Dorothea Benze Gauss** (1743–1839) — his mother. Illiterate, sharp, and fiercely on his side; the reason the boy went to school at all.
- **J. G. Büttner** — schoolmaster of the Katharinen-Volksschule, one classroom, a hundred boys, a willow switch — and, to his lasting credit, an honest eye.
- **Martin Bartels** (1769–1836) — Büttner's seventeen-year-old assistant, hired to cut quill pens; became the boy's first real study partner, and much later, professor to **Lobachevsky**. Remember that name for Act III.
- **Duke Carl Wilhelm Ferdinand of Brunswick** (1735–1806) — the patron who paid for everything and asked for nothing.
- **Farkas (Wolfgang) Bolyai** (1775–1856) — Gauss's one true friend from university; his son **János** will be the story's saddest casualty.

## 中文锚点

高斯（1777–1855）出生于德国不伦瑞克的一个贫苦家庭，母亲不识字，父亲是砌砖工。三岁纠正父亲的工资账；九岁那年，老师让全班把一长串数相加，他几乎立刻把石板拍在讲台上："Ligget se"（放在这儿了）。公爵出资送他上学；十九岁生日前一个月，他证明了正十七边形可以尺规作图（[[Heptadecagon]]），从此立志做数学家，并从那天起开始写一本秘密日记——146 条极简的拉丁文记录，藏了一辈子，直到他去世四十二年后才被发现。二十四岁那年他出版《算术研究》，同年"找回"了失踪的谷神星（[[Stories/Inventing Variance]]）。他的座右铭：*Pauca sed matura* ——少而精。这份"少"成就了他，也伤害了别人：波约•亚诺什的非欧几何、阿贝尔的椭圆函数，都撞上了他抽屉里那句"我早就想到了"。

## The Story

### Act I — The slate 石板

Braunschweig, the 1780s. Gebhard Gauss laid brick, butchered in winter, kept gardens in summer, and did the accounts on Saturdays. The family story — Gauss himself loved telling it in old age, so season it accordingly — is that one Saturday the three-year-old, playing on the floor, looked up and said the payroll total was wrong. Gebhard re-added the column. The child was right.

It was Dorothea who insisted the boy be schooled. In Büttner's one-room Volksschule — a hundred boys, one master, discipline by switch — the arithmetic class was one day set a long summation to keep it busy. Every version of the legend agrees on what happened next; almost nothing else about it is stable. The boy thought for a moment, wrote a single number on his slate, walked up, and dropped it on the master's desk in his Braunschweig Low German: **"Ligget se."** — *there it lies.* Then he sat down and waited an hour while everyone else added, erased, and added again. When Büttner finally turned the slates over, the youngest slate in the pile carried one number, and it was the right one.

![[gauss-prodigy-slate-comic.png|660]]

What did he see? The tidy version of the problem — add $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 100$ — has a tidy answer: don't march through the sum, *look at it whole*. Fold the list in half. $1 + 100 = 101$. $2 + 99 = 101$. $3 + 98 = 101$. Every pair says the same thing — the pair-sum is the **invariant** — and there are fifty pairs:

$$1 + 2 + \cdots + 100 = 50 \times 101 = 5050.$$

The hunter's move, at age nine: while the room counted forwards, he read the *structure* — what stays the same when everything changes ([[Arithmetic and Geometric Progressions]] turns the trick into the general formula $S_n = \tfrac{n}{2}(a + l)$).

> [!warning] The legend, audited
> The earliest account — Sartorius's memorial essay, published the year after Gauss died — gives the scene, the slate, and *"ligget se"*, and **names no numbers at all**. The 1-to-100 series, the sums-from-1-to-100-as-busywork framing, Büttner's villainy, the whipping that didn't happen — all accreted over a century of retelling, and a famous survey traced over a hundred mutually contradicting versions. Even the boy's age wobbles between seven and ten depending on the telling. The vault tells it at nine, in Büttner's arithmetic class of 1786, and holds the honest line: the slate and the sentence are sourced; the beautiful arithmetic is the *plausible reconstruction* everyone loves too much to drop. Legends, too, deserve error bars.

And Büttner? The retellings made him the dull tyrant, but the sourced record is kinder: he looked at what the boy could do, admitted it was beyond him, and **spent his own money** ordering a better arithmetic textbook from Hamburg. When it arrived he shook his head — nothing left to teach — and handed the boy to his assistant. Seventeen-year-old **Bartels**, employed to trim quills, sat with the nine-year-old in the evenings, and the two of them worked through the binomial theorem and infinite series together. The one-room school had, by accident, produced a two-person research seminar.

### Act II — The Duke and the seventeen corners 公爵与十七个角

Word of the bricklayer's son climbed the town. E. A. W. von Zimmermann — the Collegium Carolinum's professor of natural history — arranged an audience, and in 1791 the fourteen-year-old stood in front of **Duke Carl Wilhelm Ferdinand of Brunswick**, who did a simple, history-bending thing: he paid. School fees, then the Collegium Carolinum, then the university at Göttingen — a standing stipend with no conditions attached. Gebhard thought study a luxury; the Duke made the question irrelevant. (Dorothea, for her part, once cornered Gauss's university friend Farkas Bolyai and asked, in terror, whether anything would really come of her son. Bolyai answered: *the greatest mathematician in Europe.* She burst into tears.)

Here is the part the syllabus never has time for: **the teenage Gauss almost didn't choose mathematics.** At Göttingen he was torn between mathematics and philology — classical languages were the prestigious track, and he was brilliant at them. The tie was broken on the morning of **30 March 1796**, a month before his nineteenth birthday, "by intense thought… in bed, before rising": the regular seventeen-sided polygon — a problem untouched since Euclid, a shape two thousand years of geometers had silently assumed impossible — **can be drawn with ruler and compasses alone.** [[Heptadecagon]] carries the mathematics (and the correction that he proved it constructible but never bothered to draw it); this card carries the consequence. A nineteen-year-old had out-geometried antiquity before breakfast, and the philologists lost him by lunchtime.

### Act III — The secret diary 秘密日记

Something else happened on 30 March 1796: Gauss started a diary. Nineteen pages. He kept it for eighteen years, **146 entries**, most of them a single cryptic line of Latin. Entry number one is the heptadecagon. Entry from 10 July 1796, in full:

> **ΕΥΡΗΚΑ! num = Δ + Δ + Δ**

— *Eureka: every whole number is the sum of three triangular numbers*, a theorem Fermat had conjectured and no one had proved, disposed of in one line of a private notebook. Some entries are stranger: 11 October 1796 says **"Vicimus GEGAN"** — *we have conquered GEGAN* — and to this day nobody knows what GEGAN was. The greatest mathematician alive kept a victory log in a private code, and part of it has never been cracked.

The diary was not published. Neither was most of what it recorded. Gauss's seal carried his motto — ***Pauca sed matura***, *few but ripe* — and he meant it: results left the drawer only when polished into inevitability, and the drawer held things the world would have called revolutions. The diary itself vanished into family papers and was only rediscovered in **1897, forty-two years after his death**; when it and his notebooks were finally read, the priority lists of nineteenth-century mathematics needed rewriting. Elliptic function theory — in the drawer decades before Abel and Jacobi published it ([[Stories/Abel the Other Boy Who Died Young]] walked that road on foot). The germ of non-Euclidean geometry — in his notes and letters years before Lobachevsky and Bolyai printed theirs.

And this is where the story stops being charming. In 1832 his old friend Farkas Bolyai sent him a slim appendix written by his son **János**: a complete, blazing construction of geometry without the parallel postulate, the work of a young cavalry officer who had bet his career on it. Gauss replied:

> *"To praise it would amount to praising myself. For the entire content of the work… coincides almost exactly with my own meditations, which have occupied my mind for from thirty to thirty-five years."*

He meant it as high praise — *the boy is as far along as I am* — and he wrote warmly of the younger Bolyai elsewhere. It does not matter. János Bolyai, holding the letter, understood only that the summit he had bled for had a flag on it, planted silently, decades ago, by a man who had told no one. He never published mathematics again. Being right and being kind are different achievements, and hoarding ripeness has a body count: the theorems were safe in the drawer, but the *people* who rediscovered them were not. (The wickedest twist: Lobachevsky, the other independent inventor of non-Euclidean geometry, had been taught at Kazan by — **Bartels**. The quill-cutting assistant from Büttner's classroom stands at both ends of the story, evenings with the boy who kept the secret, lectures for the man who told the world.)

### Act IV — 1801, the year of the double 二十四岁那一年

Two things happened in 1801, and either alone would have made the year famous.

In September, the ***Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*** was published — finished when Gauss was 21, printed when he was 24, dedicated to the Duke who had paid for everything. It rebuilt number theory as a *discipline*: congruences and the $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$ notation, quadratic reciprocity proved (he called it the *theorema aureum*, the golden theorem, and would give seven more proofs over his life), and the cyclotomic theory whose corollary is the seventeen corners of Act II. Mathematicians called the book sealed with seven seals; those who broke in found the next century inside.

And in December, a lost world was found. Piazzi had glimpsed **Ceres** on the first night of the century and lost it in the Sun's glare after 41 observations spanning three degrees of sky; the astronomers of Europe could not refind it. The 24-year-old who had just rebuilt arithmetic spent the autumn inventing orbit determination, told the astronomers where to point, and von Zach found Ceres within a fingernail of the prediction — *"Without the ingenious labours and calculations of Doctor Gauss,"* he wrote, *"we might perhaps never have found Ceres again."* The full drama — the monk, the three degrees, the birth of least squares and of the [[Normal Distribution]]'s job description — lives in [[Stories/Inventing Variance]]; what matters *here* is the shape of the fame. The *Disquisitiones* was read, in his lifetime, by perhaps a dozen people who understood it. The planet made him a celebrity overnight. The public pays for the visible; the deepest work waited a century for its audience. He took the directorship of the Göttingen observatory in 1807 — the Duke, his patron, had died of his wounds at Auerstedt in 1806, fighting Napoleon — and remained an astronomer, officially, for the rest of his life.

### Coda — the seventeen-pointed star 十七角星

He worked until the end — surveying Hanover (inventing the heliotrope, and the differential geometry of surfaces while he was at it), magnetism with Weber ([[Maxwell's Equations]] keeps his name on the law about flux), the method of least squares in the observatory's daily bread. Dorothea lived with him in Göttingen until she died at 97, blind; he nursed her himself. His sons got no such warmth for their mathematics — he steered them away from the subject, by their account fearing a lesser Gauss would lower the name; two emigrated to America. Genius, in this house, was not a shared inheritance but a guarded title.

He asked for the heptadecagon on his tombstone. The stonemason refused — seventeen sides in stone would look like a circle, and a circle would look like nothing. His memorial in Braunschweig stands on a **seventeen-pointed star** instead: the corners insisted on, one way or another.

One last indignity, too good to omit. His brain was preserved at Göttingen for science. In **2013**, an MRI study discovered that at some point in the preceding 150 years the jar labels had been swapped: the brain the world had been measuring and photographing as *Gauss's* belonged to the physician Conrad Heinrich Fuchs, and Gauss's own sat under Fuchs's name. Even the prince of mathematicians could not escape a labelling error — [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]] would like a word.

## Cultural ripples

- ***Pauca sed matura* became the cautionary tale, not the ideal.** Modern science runs on the opposite covenant — publish, claim, share, let priority be public — partly because the nineteenth century watched what the drawer did to Bolyai and Abel. The question "would you rather be Gauss (right, silent) or Euler (prolific, open)?" is still a live argument about how science should work.
- **The schoolboy sum is the world's most-taught legend about *seeing structure*** — told in every classroom that meets arithmetic series, usually with invented details, which is itself the lesson [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]] teaches about how stories calcify.
- **"Prince of mathematicians"** — the *Princeps* coin struck by Hanover on his death fixed the ranking rhetoric we still use (the "greatest since Newton" genre starts here).
- **The Duke's stipend is the standing argument for patronage without deliverables**: no conditions, no applications, no reports — and the return on investment was the *Disquisitiones*, Ceres, and least squares. Funding agencies have been trying to rediscover this mechanism ever since.
