# The Nobel Prizes in the Beaker 烧杯里的诺贝尔奖章

> *A hiding place usually changes where you look. This one changed what you were looking for.*

## Cast of Characters

- **George de Hevesy** (1885–1966) — Hungarian chemist; very good at following matter after it stopped looking familiar.
- **Niels Bohr** — his friend in Copenhagen, with two other people's medals to protect.
- **Max von Laue and James Franck** — the German physicists whose names were engraved on those medals.
- **Ernest Rutherford and Friedrich Paneth** — the demanding mentor and the collaborator in an earlier experiment that made failure useful.

## 中文锚点

想象一下，朋友托你保管一件刻着他名字的贵重物品，可一旦被人搜到，连他也会有危险。你会先想到藏进抽屉，或找个地方埋起来。赫维西却把两枚金奖章溶进了药液：金子还在，能被认出来的奖章却不见了。他敢这么做，是因为他知道怎样把金子取回来。这个选择最让人心里一紧的地方，是为了替朋友保住它，眼下反而得亲手让它消失。

## Act I — A very inconvenient object

In April 1940, German forces occupied Denmark. At Bohr's institute in Copenhagen were the Nobel medals of von Laue and Franck, sent there for safekeeping. Gold exported from Nazi Germany was already a dangerous matter; the engraved names made these particular pieces of gold evidence against identifiable people. Hevesy later recalled suggesting burial. Bohr worried that someone could dig them up. [1]

Every ordinary hiding place left the same problem intact: eventually, someone might open the drawer or lift the floorboard and recognise a medal.

Hevesy's answer was five words:

> “I decided to dissolve it.” [1]

That sounds like the moment a rescue plan turns into an insurance claim. To understand why it was a rescue plan, it helps to meet the younger man who had spent months failing to separate two things.

## Act II — The useful failure

Hevesy grew up in a prosperous Budapest family and earned his doctorate in 1908. He arrived in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory in 1911 to learn radioactive measurement. Rutherford set him a separation problem: extract a radioactive substance called **radium D** from a large quantity of lead. Hevesy tried the available chemistry. It kept failing. [2]

We now call radium D **lead-210**. It is an isotope of lead: the same proton number and essentially the same chemistry, with a different neutron count. Ordinary chemical separation was asking the atoms to behave differently in precisely the respect in which they behaved alike. [2]

Hevesy changed the purpose of the experiment. If the radioactive lead accompanied ordinary lead through chemical processes, its radiation could reveal where the lead went. The inseparability was useful. With **Friedrich Paneth**, he developed radioactive-tracer experiments in 1913. [2]

Think of putting a distinctive suitcase on a luggage conveyor to find out where the conveyor goes. The marker helps only if it travels with the luggage. A suitcase that leaps off the belt follows a different story.

That is the causal idea behind a tracer. The label must be detectable, and it must follow the process you are trying to measure. [[Nuclear Physics]] explains the isotope distinction; [[PET Scanning]] shows what this way of asking questions eventually became.

Years before the medals, Hevesy had already learned to ask what a material would **continue doing** when appearances stopped being helpful.

## Act III — Gold, temporarily without a face

*Interpretive comic, not a reconstruction of the room or a record of dialogue. The sequence compresses the chemical change; the captions are ours.*

Gold is awkwardly well suited to being a medal. It does not readily tarnish or dissolve. The chemical resistance that helps a prize outlast its owner also made Hevesy's task slow. His retrospective account places the work during the occupation; it does not give us a stopwatch or a soldier reaching for the laboratory door. [1]

The reagent was **aqua regia**, a strongly corrosive acid mixture capable of dissolving gold. Its action combines oxidation of gold with chloride binding of the resulting gold ions. The metal enters solution as dissolved chemical species, including the **tetrachloroaurate ion**, $\mathrm{[AuCl_4]^-}$. [3]

The gold had not melted. A melted medal would still leave a lump of metal. Nor had the gold nuclei been changed into another element: this was chemistry, involving electrons and chemical bonding, not nuclear transmutation.

The embossed face and engraved name disappeared because the solid object disappeared. The gold remained chemically recoverable.

That is the joke chemistry plays on a search based on recognisable objects: a medal can cease to exist as a medal without its gold ceasing to exist as gold. The distinction was suddenly carrying much more than a vocabulary lesson.

## Act IV — People cannot hide like gold

Hevesy was from a Jewish family. He had returned from Freiburg to Copenhagen after the Nazi takeover in Germany; in autumn 1943 he had to flee again, this time to Sweden. The successful treatment of two objects did not make their custodian safe. He continued his research in Stockholm. [4]

In 1944 he received the **1943 Nobel Prize in Chemistry**, awarded for his use of isotopes as tracers. It was recognition of the scientific work, not a prize for hiding prizes. The apparent date mismatch is real: that year's chemistry award had been reserved and was awarded the following year. [5]

The man who had made two Nobel medals disappear now had a Nobel medal of his own.

## Act V — The gold comes back; the paperwork follows

After the war, the gold was recovered and replacement medals were made. The Nobel account cites a letter from Bohr dated **24 January 1950** concerning delivery of the gold to Stockholm, and records Franck receiving his recoined medal at the University of Chicago on **31 January 1952**. The ending took longer than a last-page reunion scene. [1]

Recovery does not undo everything. The earlier struck objects, with their particular scratches and surfaces, were gone. The material could return in a newly made object carrying the same public recognition.

That is why the story is more interesting than a clever hiding trick. Hevesy accepted a real loss of form to preserve something that could be restored. He needed to know which part of the destruction was reversible before he began.

## The hospital connection

The tracer work has an everyday destination quite separate from the medals: nuclear medicine. A clinician can use a suitably labelled substance to investigate where it travels or accumulates. In PET, a radioactive label carried by a tracer supplies emissions from which the scanner reconstructs its distribution. The tracer's chemistry determines what biological process the measurement can speak about. Hevesy's work helped establish this experimental tradition; the later scanners required many other people's inventions. [4, 6]

The connection between the two stories is a question, rather than a piece of equipment: **what remains identifiable when I can no longer recognise it by sight?**

With tracers, Hevesy made hidden matter detectable. With the medals, he made conspicuous matter hard to recognise.

## Honest edges

- **Two medals, belonging to Laue and Franck.** Bohr's own medal was auctioned with August Krogh's in March 1940 for Finnish relief. It was not a third medal in the solution. The detailed Nobel account distinguishes these events even though its introductory summary confusingly says three were dissolved. [1]
- **The chemistry is secure; the cinematic staging is not.** No invented exchange with a searching soldier, last-second escape or exact glassware arrangement is needed. The comic is explicitly an interpretation.
- **Hevesy was not a lone inventor of modern medical imaging.** Paneth belongs in the early tracer story; later biological applications and imaging instruments were collaborative developments. [2, 4, 6]
- **Aqua regia is not a kitchen-table activity.** The historical explanation is not an experiment to reproduce; the reagent is highly corrosive and can release toxic gases. [3]
