# Abel — the Other Boy Who Died Young  阿贝尔——另一个早逝的少年

> *"Abel has left mathematicians enough to keep them busy for five hundred years."* — Charles Hermite
>
> The letter offering him a professorship in Berlin arrived at Froland **two days after he was already in the ground.**

Everyone knows the boy who died in the duel. Fewer know that three years *earlier*, and a thousand kilometres north, a gentler boy had already lived the same tragedy — proved the same theorem, was lost by the same referee, and died just as young of something slower than a bullet. [[Stories/Galois at Twenty|Galois]] you can almost explain: he was abrasive, a revolutionary, a maker of enemies. **Niels Henrik Abel had none of that.** He was poor, modest, warm, and beloved by the handful of people who understood him. It made no difference at all. That is the harder lesson of the two: reception is not a reward for being likeable. The machine that ignores you does not care whether you are kind.

He died at **twenty-six**, of tuberculosis, in debt, in a borrowed room. And the cruelty at the centre of his story is not a slammed door or a rejected paper. It is a piece of *good* news — the best news of his life — that travelled a few days too slowly to reach a living man.

## Cast of Characters

- **Niels Henrik Abel** (1802–1829) — Norwegian mathematician. Proved the general quintic unsolvable by radicals (1824) and, more lastingly, opened the vast theory of **elliptic and "abelian" functions**. Dead at twenty-six, penniless, of tuberculosis.
- **Bernt Michael Holmboe** (1795–1850) — the schoolteacher who arrived at exactly the right moment, saw what he was holding, and fed a fifteen-year-old the masters. Every genius needs one of these. Abel had exactly one.
- **August Leopold Crelle** (1780–1855) — Prussian engineer and mathematics enthusiast who founded the journal that carried Abel to the world, became his devoted friend, and — too late — his rescuer.
- **Augustin-Louis Cauchy** (1789–1857) — the great French analyst who took Abel's masterpiece home to referee and *mislaid it*. Three years later he would do almost exactly the same to Galois.
- **Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi** (1804–1851) — the German prodigy racing Abel, step for step, to the theory of elliptic functions. A rival who became, in effect, a co-discoverer.
- **Adrien-Marie Legendre** (1752–1833) — the old master who had spent forty years on elliptic integrals and lived to watch two boys leap clean over him — and was *delighted.*
- **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777–1855) — the greatest mathematician alive, who received Abel's pamphlet and never cut the pages open.
- **Évariste Galois** (1811–1832) — the twin. Dead three years later in a duel; the quintic's impossibility carries both their names.

## 中文锚点

**核心论题**：尼尔斯·亨里克·阿贝尔（1802–1829）是 [[Stories/Galois at Twenty|伽罗瓦]] 的"孪生悲剧"。他生在拿破仑战争后极度贫困的挪威，牧师父亲早逝、家道中落。少年时靠一位老师 Holmboe 发现天赋，直接去读欧拉、拉格朗日、高斯的原著——"要读大师，不要读大师的学生"。

**1824 年**，他证明了**一般五次方程没有根式解**（比伽罗瓦"为什么"的回答早了几年；见 [[Cubic Graphs]]）。为省印刷费，他把证明压缩成**仅六页**的小册子，寄给高斯——而高斯从未裁开那几页。他真正更伟大的遗产，其实是**椭圆函数**与后来以他命名的"**阿贝尔积分/阿贝尔函数**"。1826 年他在巴黎写下毕生杰作，投给法国科学院——却被 **Cauchy 遗失**（三年后同一个人又对伽罗瓦做了几乎一样的事）。

他在巴黎染上肺结核，回到挪威负债、无教职，仍与 **Jacobi** 并驾齐驱地开创椭圆函数论。1829 年 4 月 6 日病逝于 Froland，年仅 26。**Crelle 为他争取到的柏林教授职位的聘书，在他下葬两天后才寄到。**

身后，"**abelian（阿贝尔的）**"成了数学里最常见的形容词之一——常见到连首字母都不再大写。这是**最诚实的一次以人命名**（与 [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy|斯蒂格勒定律]] 相反，正如普朗克把常数 $k$ 命名给玻尔兹曼）。2003 年设立的**阿贝尔奖**，是挪威迟到近两百年的道歉。

## Act I — A pastor's son in a starving country

Niels Henrik Abel was born on **5 August 1802** in Nedstrand, on Norway's rocky southwest coast, the second of what would be seven children of a Lutheran pastor. It is hard to overstate how bad a moment this was to be born poor in Norway. Within a few years the Napoleonic wars and a British naval blockade cut the country off; there was famine. In 1814 Norway was handed from Denmark to Sweden like a piece of furniture. Into this the Abel family sank, slowly and then all at once.

His father, **Søren Georg Abel**, was a pastor and, for a while, a member of the new Norwegian parliament — a cultured, ambitious, unstable man whose career and reputation collapsed in scandal and drink before he died in **1820**, when Niels was seventeen. His mother, by most accounts, was overwhelmed and unwell. The family was left destitute, and the second son — quiet, gifted, watchful — became one of the people it leaned on. Money, or its absence, is not a footnote in this story. It is a character. It set the ceiling on his schooling, shortened his one trip abroad, put him in the cold rooms where the tuberculosis found him, and, at the very end, decided the pace of a letter.

He was sent at thirteen to the **Cathedral School** in Christiania (today's Oslo), a school then in decline. Its mathematics was taught by a brute — a man who, in 1817, beat a pupil so savagely the boy died, and was dismissed for it. The replacement, arriving in 1818, was a young teacher named **Bernt Michael Holmboe**, and he is the reason we are talking about Abel at all.

Holmboe set the class an open problem, saw how *this* one boy answered it, and quietly stopped teaching him the syllabus. He gave him real mathematics instead — and then did the thing that defines a great teacher: he got out of the way and pointed at the summit. Abel did not work through textbooks. He went straight to the **masters** — Euler, then Lagrange, then Gauss, in the original. He is supposed to have said that he learned mathematics *by studying the masters, and not their pupils.* (It is the exact instinct of the other boy, four hundred kilometres south, reading Lagrange "like a novel." The two never met; they were the same animal.) When his father died and the money ran out entirely, Holmboe and two university professors, **Christopher Hansteen** and **Søren Rasmussen**, paid out of their own pockets to keep him in study. He entered the University of Christiania in **1821**, already, at nineteen, past the frontier of what anyone in Norway could teach him.

## Act II — The quintic, twice

Here is a thing the legend usually leaves out, and it is the most human thing in the whole story. Before Abel proved the quintic *unsolvable*, he thought he had **solved** it.

At about nineteen he believed he had found the formula — the fifth-degree analogue of the quadratic formula that mathematicians had hunted for two hundred and fifty years. It was sent off toward publication, routed to the Danish mathematician **Ferdinand Degen**. Degen, wisely, asked the young man to do one small thing: work a numerical example. And in trying to, Abel found *his own mistake.* No one had to correct him. He corrected himself — the mark of a real mathematician, who would rather know the truth than be right. (Degen did one more thing worth remembering: he told Abel to turn his gaze toward **elliptic integrals**, which he called a "Pacific Ocean" of mathematics. That steer decided the rest of Abel's life, and it is where his true greatness lies — but that comes later.)

Then, in **1824**, he proved the opposite, and proved it correctly: **there is no general formula in radicals for the quintic.** No arrangement of $+,-,\times,\div$ and $n$-th roots will ever produce the roots of the general fifth-degree equation. (For *why* — the symmetry argument, the unbreakable atom $A_5$ — see [[Cubic Graphs]] and its human sequel in [[Stories/Galois at Twenty]]. This story is about the man, not the machinery.) He was not quite the first: an Italian, **Paolo Ruffini**, had published a five-hundred-page proof in 1799 with one real gap, which almost no one had read. History compromises by calling it the **Abel–Ruffini theorem** — and then, in daily use, drops Ruffini and calls it "Abel's." (That small injustice has its own home in [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]].)

Now watch poverty write the next line. To announce this result to the world, Abel printed it himself, at his own expense — and to afford the printer, he **crushed the proof down to six pages.** Six. A result that ended a 250-year search, compressed until it was nearly cryptic, because paper cost money he did not have. He sent copies to the great men of Europe, including **Gauss** at Göttingen, from whom a single word of interest would have changed everything.

Gauss never answered. When his papers were sorted after his death, Abel's pamphlet was found among them **with its pages still uncut** — never even opened. (The story that Gauss flung it aside calling it a "monstrosity" is probably a later embroidery; the uncut pages are the documented, and somehow sadder, fact. He didn't reject it. He just never looked.) The most important short paper of the decade sat sealed on the desk of the one man who could have read it in an afternoon.

## Act III — Crelle, and the ocean

If the story were only rejection it would be unbearable, and it would also be untrue. Because in **1825** Abel finally got out of Norway — a small government travelling grant — and in Berlin he walked into the single luckiest encounter of his life.

**August Leopold Crelle** was an engineer, a builder of roads and railways, and a mathematics enthusiast of boundless warmth who was, at that very moment, about to launch a new journal: the *Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik.* He met this shy, penniless Norwegian, read a few pages, and understood instantly what he was looking at. Abel became the **flagship author** of the new journal — its first volumes (1826 onward) carry a fuller, clearer version of the quintic proof and a stream of foundational papers. Crelle's Journal is still one of the most prestigious in mathematics, and it was built, in part, as a stage for Abel. More than a publisher, Crelle became his friend, his advocate, and — in a way that will break your heart by Act V — the man trying hardest to save his life.

And it was here that Abel followed Degen's old steer out into the Pacific. His deepest work is **not** the quintic — that was the headline of a boy. His ocean was **elliptic functions**. The story in one line: for a century people had studied *elliptic integrals* — awkward, resistant objects — the way one studies the arc-length of an ellipse. Abel (and, simultaneously, a brilliant young German named **Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi**) had the inversion idea: don't study the integral, study its **inverse function** — and that inverse turns out to be *doubly periodic*, a function that repeats in two independent directions across the complex plane. It was like turning a lock that had been jammed for a hundred years. An entire continent of mathematics opened at once.

The two young men raced. Abel in Norway, Jacobi in Königsberg, paper answering paper across 1827–1829, each pushing the other higher — one of those rare rivalries that is really a collaboration between people who never sit in the same room. Watching them both was old **Legendre**, then in his seventies, who had given *forty years* of his life to elliptic integrals and might have been forgiven for resenting the boys who leapt over him in eighteen months. Instead he was overjoyed. He wrote to Abel with the pure delight of a man who has waited his whole life to see his subject finished by better hands. *These,* he as good as said, *are the young men.*

## Act IV — Paris, and the memoir Cauchy lost

In the summer of **1826**, Abel went to Paris, the capital of mathematics, carrying his masterpiece.

It is called the *Mémoire sur une propriété générale d'une classe très-étendue de fonctions transcendantes* — and inside it is the result now known simply as **Abel's theorem**, the seed of the whole theory of what would later be named, after him, **abelian integrals.** He believed it was the best thing he had ever done. He was right. He submitted it to the **Académie des Sciences** on 30 October 1826, and it was handed to two referees: Legendre, and **Augustin-Louis Cauchy.**

Cauchy took it home. And then — busy, self-absorbed, deep in his own work — he simply set it aside and forgot it. Legendre, for his part, complained the manuscript was near-unreadable: faint ink, a cramped hand (Abel had, once again, been saving paper). Between the two of them, the founding document of a major branch of mathematics slid quietly under a stack and stopped existing. Abel waited in Paris for a verdict that never came. He was shy, foreign, and broke; the great French mathematicians did not seek out the quiet Norwegian, and he was too proud and too poor to force himself on them. *"The French,"* he wrote home, with the flat hurt of a lonely young man, are reserved with strangers.

Hold this next to the twin story and feel the pattern close. Three years later, another boy — [[Stories/Galois at Twenty|Galois]] — would submit *his* masterpiece to the same Academy, and it too would be lost, one of its referees again **Cauchy.** The same indifferent machine, the same man at the switch, swallowing the two greatest algebraic ideas of the century within a few years of each other. It was not malice. Malice would almost be easier to forgive. It was that genius arriving without a powerful sponsor is simply *not seen*, and the not-seeing is nobody's crime and everybody's.

And in the cold of that Paris winter, in cheap lodgings, Abel began to cough.

## Act V — Froland, and the letter two days late

He came home to Norway in late 1827 with tuberculosis in his lungs, debts on his back, and **no job.** Sit with that. The man rewriting the foundations of analysis, whom Legendre and Crelle and Jacobi already knew to be one of the finest mathematicians alive, could not get a permanent academic post in his own small country. He tutored schoolboys and substituted for his old professor Hansteen when Hansteen travelled. He kept publishing — the elliptic-function papers of these last two years are among the greatest he ever wrote, produced by a feverish, dying man who would not stop.

He was engaged to **Christine "Crelly" Kemp**, a governess. At Christmas 1828, desperately ill, he made the journey by open sledge across the frozen country to be with her at **Froland**, an ironworks in the south where she was employed. He never really got up again. He hemorrhaged; he burned with fever; for a few weeks he faded. **Niels Henrik Abel died at Froland on 6 April 1829, aged twenty-six**, with Christine at his side.

Now the knife. All that winter, in Berlin, **Crelle** had been fighting for him — pulling every string he had to secure Abel a proper appointment, a chair, a salary, a life. And he **won.** He got it. He sat down and wrote his friend the good news: it was done, a position in Berlin was his, he could stop being poor, he could come and be well and *work.* The letter reached Froland on **8 April 1829.**

Two days too late. It was handed to a household in mourning, addressed to a man already buried. Everything Abel had needed for years — recognition, security, an end to the cold rooms — arrived intact, complete, and forty-eight hours after it could do anything but hurt.

## Act VI — The lowercase honor

The vindication that follows is real, and it is enormous, and it is unbearable precisely *because* it is so complete. Everything the living man was denied, the dead man received in full.

In **1830**, the Paris Academy awarded its highest honor, the **Grand Prix** in mathematics, jointly to Abel and Jacobi for the theory of elliptic functions. Abel had been dead a year. Legendre and the others pushed for it; the lost Paris memoir was hunted down — Cauchy, prompted, finally *found* it — and it was published at last in **1841**, twelve years after its author died waiting for a word about it. Jacobi, learning what had happened to the manuscript, wrote to Legendre in something close to fury: *how could the Academy have let such a thing lie forgotten?*

But the real monument is quieter, and it is everywhere. In 1870 Camille Jordan, systematising the algebra Abel and Galois had opened, needed a name for the well-behaved, *commutative* symmetry groups — the ones whose elements don't care what order you combine them in, exactly the property at the heart of Abel's quintic proof. He called them **abelian groups.** And the word *took.* It spread into every corner of mathematics — abelian group, abelian integral, abelian function, abelian variety, abelian extension, abelian category — until it became something almost no eponym ever becomes: **a word so ordinary that it lost its capital letter.** Working mathematicians write "abelian" the way they write "prime" or "continuous," without a thought for the boy behind it. It is the deepest kind of remembering — a name dissolved all the way into the language.

That matters here for a specific reason. This vault keeps a running ledger of *misplaced* credit — [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]], where the thing is almost never named for the one who found it. "Abelian" is the **counter-case, and an honest one.** Abel really did open this world; the name really is his; and it is carved not into one theorem but into the working vocabulary of the whole subject. It belongs beside the one other clean case this vault treasures — **Planck** choosing to carve *Boltzmann's* constant $k$ onto the tombstone of the man who earned it ([[Stories/Boltzmann's Tombstone]]). When mathematics gets credit right, it gets it right forever.

And then, at the very end, a country's apology. Norway — which had had no chair to offer its greatest son — established the **Abel Prize** in 2002 and first awarded it in 2003: several million kroner, presented by the king, deliberately built to be the "Nobel of mathematics" that Alfred Nobel never created. The idea had first been floated in 1902, on the centenary of Abel's birth, and had fallen through. It took another hundred years. Norway is, in effect, still writing the letter — and this time making very sure it arrives.

## Cultural ripples

**The elliptic-function ocean still runs the internet.** Abel's deepest work was not the headline theorem but the doubly-periodic functions he opened — and the *elliptic curves* that grew from the same soil are now the backbone of modern **elliptic-curve cryptography**, guarding a large share of the world's secure traffic. It is the same quiet miracle as the other boy's: Galois's finite fields under AES, Abel's elliptic world under your bank login. Two teenagers, dead in poverty within three years of each other, doing pure mathematics with no possible use — and two centuries later the whole planet's privacy leans on both. *Nothing is more practical than something beautiful that no one yet needs.*

**The tragedy that isn't about personality.** The vault's Galois card fights the myth of the tortured genius scribbling through one night. Abel fights the *complementary* myth — the idea that if only he'd been more assertive, more political, more Galois, he'd have been seen. He was the opposite of Galois: gentle, modest, universally liked by those who knew him. And it changed nothing, because the failure was never in the man. **Genius does not carry itself. It has to be *received*** — and reception is a human, institutional, fallible thing, deaf to how deserving or how likeable you are. That is the lesson the two boys teach together that neither teaches alone.

**"I have not the time" has a northern echo.** Galois's three margin words are the memento mori of mathematics. Abel's is quieter and, if anything, worse: not a race against a dawn duel but a slow fever, a mounting pile of the greatest papers of the age, and a letter with everything he needed inside it, moving across Europe at the speed of a horse, two days behind a life.
