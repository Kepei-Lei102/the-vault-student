# One Take, Many Tracks 一遍过与多轨录音

> *Recording began as a way to freeze a performance and ended as a way to compose with time itself. In between: two war souvenirs mailed home in pieces, a crooner too impatient to sing everything twice, and a guitarist who told the surgeons to set his shattered arm — permanently — at picking angle.*

![[one-take-many-tracks-sneeze-comic.png|560]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Fred Gaisberg** (1873–1951) — the Gramophone Company's roving recordist; carried the horn to a Milan hotel room.
- **Enrico Caruso** (1873–1921) — the tenor whose voice happened to be built for the horn; the gramophone's first global star.
- **Jack Mullin** (1913–1999) — US Army Signal Corps engineer; mailed two German tape machines home and changed American broadcasting.
- **Bing Crosby** (1903–1977) — the biggest voice on American radio; hated performing every show twice; bankrolled the tape revolution.
- **Les Paul** (1915–2009) — guitarist and incorrigible tinkerer; built his own instrument, then his own medium.
- **Mary Ford** (1924–1977) — singer; the stacked, self-harmonising voice of the "New Sound."
- **George Martin** (1926–2016) — Abbey Road producer; made four tracks sound like forty.
- **Brian Wilson** (1942–2025) — the Beach Boy who stopped touring and made the studio itself the instrument.
- **King Tubby** (Osbourne Ruddock, 1941–1989) — Kingston electronics repairman who rebuilt a mixing desk into a musical instrument and played it in real time.
- **Lee "Scratch" Perry** (1936–2021) — producer at the Black Ark, who treated the studio as a place where records were *grown* rather than captured.
- **Brian Eno** (b. 1948) — the man who gave the practice its theory, in a 1979 lecture called *The Studio as a Compositional Tool*.

## 中文锚点

**一遍过与多轨录音**：录音技术的百年史，其实是一部**逐步获得"对时间的随机访问"**的历史。1902 年，卡鲁索对着铜喇叭唱进蜡盘——**一遍过**（yī biàn guò）：错一个音，整张蜡盘作废，重头再来；录音*就是*演出本身。二战后，美国军官 Mullin 把两台德国磁带机当战利品寄回国，Crosby 为了不用每晚给东西海岸各直播一遍而投资 Ampex——**剪辑**诞生了：剃刀加胶带，是人类第一个"撤销"键。吉他手 Les Paul 在 1948 年车祸中粉碎了右臂，他让医生把胳膊**永久固定在持琴拨弦的角度**；随后他委托 Ampex 造出第一台八轨机（"章鱼"）——**多轨录音**让每条音轨可以单独重录，互不干扰。最后，数字音频（[[Sound Encoding]]）把声音变成一串数字：复制零损耗、剪辑只是改指针、撤销免费——**没有任何一条 take 是最终版**。蜡盘=只写一次；磁带=顺序访问+剪刀；多轨=并行轨道；数字=随机访问。最后一步最彻底：牙买加的 **dub**（配音版）里，King Tubby 把调音台当乐器现场演奏——**混音本身就是作曲**；Eno 1979 年给了它理论。于是「先写歌、再录音、再混音」这条流水线不再成立：作曲与处理之间的界线，也被随机访问消解了。

## Act I — One room, one horn, one chance (1877–1925)

Edison invented the phonograph in 1877 and guessed its market completely wrong: he imagined dictation machines and the preserved last words of the dying. Music was item four on his own list of uses. (The same Edison, a decade later, would fight the [[Stories/The War of the Currents|War of the Currents]] with the same gift for engineering and the same blind spots about what people actually wanted.)

For its first fifty years, recording used no electricity at all. Sound itself did the work: performers played into a great brass **horn**, the horn focused the pressure onto a diaphragm, the diaphragm drove a stylus, and the stylus carved the wave into rotating wax — physics with no amplifier anywhere. The medium dictated the music. Musicians crammed around the horn's mouth in order of loudness; string basses were replaced by tubas because the horn simply could not hear them; a singer stepped *back* for the high notes so the stylus would not jump its groove.

On 11 April 1902, in a suite at the Grand Hotel in Milan, Fred Gaisberg sat a rising thirty-year-old tenor named **Enrico Caruso** in front of the horn. Caruso sang ten arias in about two hours, took his fee of £100, and went to lunch. Gaisberg's London office, cabled for approval beforehand, had answered: **"FEE EXORBITANT. FORBID YOU TO RECORD."** Gaisberg recorded anyway — and the discs sold so spectacularly that they made Caruso an international star *before* his New York debut, invented the recording star as a species, and repaid that exorbitant fee some thousands of times over.

Caruso is also the first case of a pattern that will repeat all century: **the medium chooses its stars.** His enormous, focused tenor sat exactly in the band of frequencies the horn respected, and it rode *over* the shellac surface noise that drowned subtler voices and strings. He wasn't just a great singer; he was a great signal.

And here is the constraint the whole story fights against: **wax is write-once.** There was no editing of any kind — not a note, not a cough. A mistake in the final bar meant shaving the wax and performing the entire piece again. A recording *was* a performance: one room, one balance, one chance, about four minutes long, because that is what fit on a side. In 1925 the microphone arrived (electrical recording, from the Bell Labs of [[Stories/The Boolean-to-Silicon Bridge]] fame) and transformed *what* could be captured — but not the tyranny of *when*. A disc still could not be cut apart and rejoined. Time, once recorded, was solid.

## Act II — The machines in the mail sacks (1935–1946)

During the war, Allied radio monitors kept hitting a puzzle. German stations broadcast full symphony orchestras at three in the morning — with no shellac hiss, no four-minute side breaks, and no plausible reason for the Berlin Philharmonic to be awake. If it was live, German orchestras never slept. If it was recorded, the Germans could record in a way nobody else on Earth could.

They could. The **Magnetophon** — AEG's tape machine, running BASF's plastic tape coated in iron oxide — had been shown publicly in Berlin as early as 1935, and in 1940 an engineer named Walter Weber, chasing a distortion, stumbled into **AC bias**: feed a high-frequency signal into the record head along with the audio, and the machine's muddy, telephone-grade sound snapped into high fidelity. The Reich classified the good machines and used them to schedule culture at any hour it liked. Recorded time had quietly become *movable* time — and it was a military secret.

**Jack Mullin**, a US Army Signal Corps engineer chasing German electronics at the war's end, found the hi-fi Magnetophons at a radio facility near Frankfurt. Captured German technology was legal spoils — the patents had been voided as war reparations — so Mullin disassembled two machines, mailed them home to San Francisco piece by piece alongside fifty reels of tape, and spent months rebuilding and improving them. On 16 May 1946 he demonstrated the result to the Institute of Radio Engineers in San Francisco. The room could not reliably tell the tape from the live feed. American audio engineering, in one afternoon, discovered it was years behind a defeated country.

## Act III — Editing is born from a crooner's laziness (1946–1948)

American network radio had a rule with the force of religion: **broadcasts are live.** Recordings (clumsy transcription discs) sounded cheap and were largely banned from the networks. The rule had a price, and the person paying most of it was the most popular entertainer in the country. Because of time zones, **Bing Crosby** performed every show *twice* — once for the East Coast, once again for the West. The biggest star in America spent his evenings repeating himself.

Crosby's rebellion was gloriously unheroic: he did not want better art, he wanted to *go play golf*. In 1946 he jumped to the brand-new ABC network, which was hungry enough to let him pre-record his show on transcription discs. The sound was poor, the editing (dubbing disc to disc) was clumsy, and listeners drifted. Then in 1947 Jack Mullin auditioned his rebuilt German machines for Crosby's people. Crosby hired him on the spot, and from **1 October 1947** *Philco Radio Time* went out mastered on Mullin's two Magnetophons — the first taped network broadcast season in American history, running on a pair of war souvenirs and captured BASF tape.

What happened next is the conceptual birth of everything modern. With tape, a flubbed joke could be **cut out with a razor blade** and the tape rejoined; the best verse from Tuesday's take could be spliced into Thursday's show. Mullin once rescued a dying joke by splicing in a big laugh saved from an earlier take — and thereby invented the **laugh track**, an honest edge if ever there was one. The razor blade was the first *undo*. Time had become a physical ribbon: still sequential, but at last **cuttable, movable, rearrangeable**.

Crosby then did the most consequential thing any entertainer has done for engineering: he acted as a venture capitalist. **Ampex** — six engineers in San Carlos, California, building on what Mullin showed them — got a **$50,000** investment from Crosby, and in April 1948 shipped the Model 200, America's first professional tape recorder. Crosby handed one of the earliest machines to a guitarist friend of his.

## Act IV — The man who asked for his arm back at picking angle (1915–1957)

The friend was **Les Paul**, born Lester Polsfuss in Waukesha, Wisconsin, and there was never a time he wasn't rebuilding his tools. As a boy he punched extra holes in his mother's player-piano rolls to add notes to other people's performances — editing music on punched paper before he owned a single electronic component. He bent a coat hanger into a neck-worn harmonica holder so he could play guitar and harmonica at once; the design is essentially unchanged today. And when a note passed up at a roadside barbecue gig read *"your voice and harmonica are fine, but your guitar's not loud enough,"* he jammed a phonograph needle into his acoustic guitar's top and wired it to a radio. The complaint set the course of his life: make the guitar louder, then make it *more than one guitar*.

In 1941 he built **the Log** — a solid four-by-four pine post strung with steel strings and homemade pickups, with the sawn halves of a guitar body bolted on so audiences would accept it. Gibson's executives laughed at "the kid with the broomstick" — until solid-body electrics started selling, at which point they came back, and in 1952 put his name on the **Gibson Les Paul**. (Most people alive today know his name as an object. He found that funny.)

He was already stacking himself. On *Lover* (released 1948), recorded in his garage with disc-cutting lathes, he layered **eight guitar parts**, some recorded at half speed so they played back an octave up with impossible sparkle. But disc-to-disc overdubbing is a tower of copies: each new layer re-records all the previous ones, noise compounding, and one slip on layer eight destroys layers one through seven. Still one take — just a taller, crueller one.

Then, on 26 January 1948, on an icy bridge in Oklahoma, his car went off the road. His singing partner — Colleen Summers, soon to be **Mary Ford** — survived. Les Paul's right arm and elbow were shattered. The doctors told him the elbow would never move again; they could amputate, or they could fuse the arm rigid in one position, forever, and he should choose the position. **He had them set it bent, angled in toward his body — so that the hand would fall at the strings.** Eighteen months later he was playing. Whatever else can be said of the man, read that choice: offered one posture for the rest of his life, he chose *playing the guitar*.

With the Ampex from Crosby — modified, naturally, with an extra head — he and Mary recorded anywhere they parked: hotel rooms, apartments, the car. **"How High the Moon"** (1951), with its dozen stacked guitar and vocal layers, sat at #1 for nine weeks. Radio hosts asked who the other girls in the group were. There were no other girls; there was Mary, in unison and harmony with herself — a sound with no possible live source, the first #1 hit that **could not be performed**, because it wasn't a performance. It was an *arrangement of performances*. The take had died; the session was born.

![[one-take-many-tracks-other-girls-comic.png|560]]

One problem remained: sound-on-sound was still destructive — the tower-of-copies problem wearing a new medium. The cure came from inside Ampex: **Sel-Sync** (Ross Snyder, 1955), the quietly brilliant idea of letting the record head double as a playback head for the tracks already laid down, so that new material lands on parallel tracks of the same tape in perfect sync — **and nothing already recorded is touched.** Les Paul immediately commissioned the first eight-track machine, paid about $10,000, took delivery in 1957, and nicknamed it **the Octopus**. This is the moment multitrack recording exists: time on tape now had *addresses*. Ruin track five and you re-record track five; tracks one through four and six through eight do not care. The vocalist can show up on Tuesday, the strings on Friday.

## Act V — The studio becomes the instrument (1966–1967)

Ten years later the Octopus's children were standard studio furniture, and two groups on opposite sides of the Atlantic pushed the idea to its artistic limit.

At Abbey Road, **George Martin** and engineer Geoff Emerick had only four-track machines for *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* (1967) — so they **bounced**: fill four tracks, mix them down onto one track of a second machine, and keep going, paying one generation of tape hiss for every three tracks of freedom. For *Strawberry Fields Forever*, John Lennon liked the dreamy opening of take 7 and the heavy ending of take 26 — recorded in **different keys and different tempos**. Martin's rescue exploited a physical accident: on tape, speed drags pitch and tempo together, and the two takes' differences happened to nearly cancel. Speed one up, slow the other, and cut — the join lands just under a minute in, and millions have sung across it without noticing. For *A Day in the Life*, a forty-piece orchestra was overdubbed onto itself until it sounded like one hundred and sixty players. None of this music ever existed in a room. It existed on tape, and *only* on tape.

In California, **Brian Wilson** — who had quit touring after a mid-flight breakdown in December 1964 and stayed home with the machines — assembled **"Good Vibrations"** over seven months, in four different studios, from about ninety hours of tape: interchangeable modular sections, recorded separately and *composed afterward*, at the editing block. Three minutes thirty-five seconds of music; the most expensive single ever made to that date; a #1 hit its own band had to learn to imitate on stage. Deaf in one ear, Wilson mixed in mono and heard arrangements whole, like architecture.

And here the story owes its honest edge: **random access to time has a shadow, and the shadow is that nothing ever has to be finished.** The same freedom that assembled "Good Vibrations" dismantled its successor — *SMiLE* collapsed in 1967 under infinite revisability and Wilson's failing health, and he only completed it thirty-seven years later, in 2004. When every take can be redone, "done" becomes a decision, and some minds cannot make it. Perfectionism is the occupational disease of the editable.

## Act VI — Random access, literally (1976–now)

Tape had freed recording from the single take, but tape is a *ribbon*, and a ribbon has three prison walls. It is **linear**: reaching minute forty means winding through minutes one to thirty-nine. It is **destructive**: a splice is surgery on the master, and a punch-in overwrites forever. And it is **generational**: every bounce and every copy adds hiss, because an analogue copy is a *measurement* of a measurement, and measurements err. Digital audio demolishes all three walls at once, and the reason is the central fact of [[Sound Encoding]]: **a sample is a number.** Numbers copy exactly — the hundredth digital copy is bit-for-bit the first ([[Information Theory]]'s discrete symbols shrugging off analogue decay) — and numbers on a disk can be read in any order at all.

The pieces arrived in sequence. In the late 1970s, Thomas Stockham's **Soundstream** made the first commercial American digital recordings — and, more importantly, edited them *at a computer terminal*, no razor anywhere. In 1989 two engineers named Peter Gotcher and Evan Brooks — who had started out burning replacement sound-chip EPROMs for drum machines — shipped Digidesign's Sound Tools, and in 1991 its descendant **Pro Tools**. Editing became **non-destructive** in the fullest sense: the recorded audio on disk is never modified at all. An "edit" is an entry in a list — *play file A from 1:02.31 for 4.5 seconds, then cut to file B* — and the razor blade cuts **references, not sound**. Undo, which wax made impossible and tape made surgical, became free. In 1992 the **Alesis ADAT** put eight digital tracks on a $3,995 machine recording onto supermarket S-VHS cassettes, and multitrack recording left the temple: within a decade there were hundreds of thousands of home studios, and today a free phone app offers more tracks than Abbey Road had for *Pepper*. The #1 album of 2019 was recorded by a teenager and her brother in his childhood bedroom — Les Paul and Mary Ford's living room, seventy years on.

![[one-take-many-tracks-four-eras.svg|900]]

Look at the whole arc at once and it is a computer-science story wearing headphones. **Wax was write-once memory** — record once, read many, no revision. **Tape was sequential access with destructive writes** — plus scissors. **Multitrack gave the tape parallel, separately-writable tracks** — addresses, in one dimension. **The digital session is random access** — every instant addressable, every operation reversible, a take just a pointer in a database. The history of recording is the history of **gaining random access to time**.

And the ending has a twist worth savouring: once anyone could edit anything, *not editing* became the luxury. Albums "recorded live in one room, straight to tape" are now advertised as such, the way bread is advertised as handmade — jazz and classical never stopped, and pop returns to it whenever it wants to signal blood and nerve. Caruso's prison — one room, one chance — has been renovated into a boutique hotel. Scarcity became an aesthetic, which is what always happens to defeated constraints.

## Act VII — And then the mix became the composition (1972 → now)

There is one more wall, and it is not made of wax or tape or silicon. It is the assumption underneath the whole pipeline: **write the song, then record it, then mix it.** That order takes for granted that the song exists *before* the sound does — that mixing is a treatment applied afterwards to something already finished. Act V cracked that. What followed demolished it.

The clearest demolition happened in Kingston, and it happened first. In the early 1970s Jamaican studios started putting an instrumental of the A-side on the B-side, initially as pure economy — you had already paid for the rhythm track, so why not sell it twice. **King Tubby**, an electronics repairman who serviced sound-system amplifiers and had rebuilt his own mixing desk (custom high-pass filter, spring reverb wired for abuse), began treating that B-side as raw material. He would drop the vocal out entirely and let it back in for one word; fire the snare into a cavern of reverb and cut the reverb dead; ride the faders and switches live, in one pass, hands moving like a player's. What came out — the **version**, the **dub** — was a new piece of music made *entirely out of mix decisions* on somebody else's multitrack. Nothing was written. Nothing was performed on an instrument. The composition was performed on the desk.

That is worth sitting with, because it inverts the pipeline completely: here mixing is not the stage after composition, it **is** the composition, and the multitrack is not a finished work but a supply of parts. Every remix ever made descends from that B-side economy, and so does the idea — now ordinary — that a record is something you can compose *from*.

**Brian Eno** supplied the theory at the end of the decade. His 1979 lecture, later published as *The Studio as a Compositional Tool*, made the argument plainly: recording had converted music from an event that happens in time into a **material you can work**, which means a composer can begin with no plan at all and let the piece emerge from what the machines suggest. Painters had always worked that way — apply, step back, respond, revise. Eno's point was that recording had finally given music the same relationship to its own material, and that treating the studio as a documentation device was leaving the best of it unused. He was describing what Martin, Wilson and Tubby had already been doing without a name for it.

And now it is simply the default. When a track's identity *is* a filter sweep, a saturated 808, a chopped and pitched vocal, the sound-design choices are not decorations applied to a composition — there is no prior composition to apply them to. Hip-hop was built this way from the first breakbeat; electronic music has never worked any other way. The distinction that made mixing a separate profession quietly stopped describing most of the music being made.

The practical consequence closes the arc. The roles collapsed back into one person — the writer, player, engineer, mixer and mastering engineer are increasingly the same human at the same desk — not because specialists became unaffordable, but because in this kind of work those stages are not separable in the first place.

> [!warning] The stage gates were also stopping points
> Act V's honest edge was that random access means nothing ever has to be finished. The collapse makes that harder, in a way worth naming. The old pipeline's handoffs were **deadlines in disguise**: when the mix engineer took the multitrack, you had stopped writing, whether or not you felt done; when the mastering engineer took the mix, you had stopped mixing. Each handoff removed the work from your hands and made a decision you did not have to make.
>
> Do every stage yourself and nobody ever takes it away. The freedom is total and so is the absence of any moment that declares the thing finished — which is why the discipline solo producers actually need is not a better chain but an artificial gate: a deadline, a fresh pair of ears, or simply a day away from it before deciding. *SMiLE* is the warning, and it was written before the tools got this good.

Read the whole card at once and the shape is clear. Each act bought a new freedom over recorded time — repeat it, cut it, layer it, address any instant of it — and this last act spends all of them at once, on the only thing left to make free: the boundary between making a piece of music and processing one. The pipeline was never a law of music. It was a description of a factory, and the factory has closed.

## Honest edges

**Nobody in this story invented multitrack alone — least of all its hero.** Sound-on-sound predates Les Paul (Sidney Bechet recorded a one-man-band record by disc overdubbing in 1941), Sel-Sync was Ross Snyder's invention at Ampex, and tape itself was German engineering (with AC bias *patented* in the US as early as the 1920s and forgotten — a fine [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy|Stigler]] specimen). Les Paul's genius was to be the *demand* that made the supply exist: the one musician who knew exactly what to ask the engineers for, and who played the results onto the charts so the world could hear why it mattered.

**Crosby didn't invent tape; he financed an import.** The "Bing Crosby created modern recording" retelling flatters the star and erases both the German engineers who built the Magnetophon and the fact that the enabling act was a war — the technology arrived as spoils, not as invention. Mullin's mailing was legal; it was still the transplantation of a defeated country's crown jewel.

**The home studio ate its first family.** *Les Paul & Mary Ford at Home* put five shows a week on television from their own living room — the first bedroom studio, decades early. The machines never went off; the marriage did, in 1964. Mary Ford had spent years unable to leave the place where she worked, because it was also where she lived. The bedroom-producer dream the ADAT later sold to everyone carries that fine print still.

**And the laugh track:** the same razor blade that saved Crosby's flat joke grew into an industry of canned laughter — audiences cued to find things funny by the spliced-in mirth of people long dead. Every technology in this story was an honesty trade: more perfect art, less certain testimony.

## Cultural ripples

- **The three-minute pop song is fossilised wax.** A ten-inch 78 rpm side held about three minutes, so that became the length of a "song" — and radio, jukeboxes, and Spotify playlists still live inside a constraint that physically expired a century ago.
- **The language is a museum of dead media.** We still *rewind* streams, *tape* shows, *cut* records, lay down *tracks*, and release *albums* — the last one a literal photo-album-style book of 78 rpm discs. Every word is a fossil of a storage medium, the way "footage" measures film that no longer exists.
- **Authenticity anxiety is an invention of editing.** Once takes could be assembled, "can they actually sing?" became askable — through Milli Vanilli's revoked Grammy (1990), lip-sync scandals, *MTV Unplugged* as a purity ritual, and the Auto-Tune wars. Caruso was never asked whether his records were "real"; the question did not exist yet.
- **"Producer" changed jobs without changing its name.** In 1960 a producer supervised: booked the room, hired the players, decided which take. Today, in most popular genres, the producer *makes the track* — the beat, the sounds, the arrangement — and the word now names the composer. The title survived a total change of occupation, which is a [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy|Stigler]]-shaped joke about how professions are labelled.
- **The concert inverted.** For Caruso, the record was a souvenir of the performance. For a modern pop act touring an assembled studio production, the performance is a re-enactment of the record — click tracks and backing stems holding the live show to the session's grid.

## Where this surfaces in the vault

- **[[Sound Encoding]]** — dual residency. That card carries the machinery: sampling, quantisation, Nyquist, and the 44,100-videotape hack that launched digital audio; the analogue-copies-decay vs digital-copies-are-exact asymmetry that powers Act VI is its samples-are-numbers thesis, acted out by history.
- **[[Information Theory]]** — *why* the hundredth digital copy is identical to the first: discrete symbols reject small errors, which analogue ribbons cannot. Generation loss is what a channel does to you without Shannon's armour.
- **[[Parallel and External Sorting]]** — in the same decades, computing learned to work *around* sequential media (the tape-based external sorts) while studios learned to make art around them. Same constraint, two cultures, and both escaped to random access together.
- **[[Stories/The Loudness War]]** — the direct sequel, and the boundary dissolving from the other end. Its Act V defines mixing against mastering because the story needs the distinction to assign blame; Act VII here watches that same distinction stop describing how music is actually made.
- **[[Stories/The War of the Currents]]** — the sibling arc: same Edison, same lesson that infrastructure fights are decided by physics and paid for in showmanship.
