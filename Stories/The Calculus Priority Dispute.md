# The Calculus Priority Dispute 微积分优先权之争

> *"Second inventor counts for nothing."*
> — Isaac Newton, *Commercium Epistolicum* (1712), the report he secretly wrote about himself.

> *"I would rather be Leibniz, dying poor and forgotten, than Newton, who went to extreme lengths in his attacks."*
> — apocryphally, every continental mathematician for the next century.

![[calculus-priority-timeline.png|697]]

## Cast of Characters

- **Isaac Newton** (1642–1727) — Cambridge mathematician, alchemist, theologian, eventually Master of the Royal Mint and President of the Royal Society. Discovered calculus, gravity, the spectrum, and the binomial series. Also: a profoundly difficult colleague — this is his *second* major priority dispute. The first was a years-long fight with **Robert Hooke** over optics (1670s) and the inverse-square law of gravity (1680s); when Hooke died in 1703 and Newton took the Royal Society presidency, Newton allegedly had Hooke's portrait removed. (See [[Stories/Newton vs Hooke]] for the full story.) The Leibniz fight was the same playbook, larger stakes.
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646–1716) — German polymath, philosopher, diplomat, librarian to the Duke of Hanover. Discovered calculus independently, invented the binary number system, designed a mechanical calculator, drafted plans for an academy of sciences. Also: out-talked, out-charmed, out-lived almost everyone — except in this fight.
- **John Collins** (1625–1683) — English accountant and mathematics correspondent. Held copies of Newton's unpublished manuscripts; passed them around. Sleeves Leibniz inspected during his 1676 London visit.
- **Henry Oldenburg** (~1619–1677) — Secretary of the Royal Society. Intermediary in the early Newton-Leibniz correspondence; died before the dispute went hot.
- **Nicolas Fatio de Duillier** (1664–1753) — Swiss mathematician, briefly Newton's intimate friend, who made the first formal accusation that Leibniz had stolen.
- **John Keill** (1671–1721) — Scottish mathematician, Newton's enforcer; renewed the plagiarism accusation in 1708 and lit the fuse.
- **Johann Bernoulli** (1667–1748) — Swiss mathematician, Leibniz's most prominent partisan. Threw down the brachistochrone challenge to all comers in 1696 (see [[Stories/The Bernoulli Family]]).
- **Charles Babbage**, **John Herschel**, **George Peacock** — Cambridge undergraduates who, ninety-six years after Newton's death, finally fixed the damage.

## 中文锚点

**微积分优先权之争**：科学史上最长、最丑陋的发明权纠纷之一。

牛顿在 1665-66 年瘟疫期间独立发明了微积分（他叫「流数法」），但没有发表。莱布尼茨在 1675-76 年独立发明了微积分，并使用了我们今天还在用的记号（$dy$、$dx$、$\int$），并于 1684 年率先发表。

英国（牛顿派）和欧洲大陆（莱布尼茨派）各执一词。1712 年，皇家学会任命「中立委员会」调查，结论：牛顿先，莱布尼茨剽窃。**这份报告其实是牛顿亲自匿名写的**——他当时是皇家学会的会长。

莱布尼茨 1716 年死在汉诺威，孤独贫困；牛顿 1727 年葬于威斯敏斯特大教堂，国葬规格。

历史的最终判决：两人独立发明。牛顿想法更早，莱布尼茨发表更早，**莱布尼茨的记号最终胜出**。但代价是英国数学落后欧洲大陆将近 100 年——直到 1812 年剑桥本科生 Babbage、Herschel、Peacock 三人成立「分析学会」(Analytical Society)，强行把 Leibniz 记号带入英国，英国数学才追上来。

教训（按重要程度排）：

1. **独立发现本身就是巨大的胜利。** 莱布尼茨没有输——牛顿只是「技术上没赢」而已。两个人各自从零推出同一套微积分，本身就是这套数学「成熟到该出现」的最好证据。承认对方独立发现，自己一点也不会变小。
2. **真正的祸根是牛顿的性格 + 拖延发表。** 把成果搁抽屉里几十年，被别人独立发现后又指控对方剽窃 —— 这是自找的。如果牛顿 1669 年写完 *De Analysi* 当年就发表，1684 年莱布尼茨独立发表时大家会觉得「漂亮，两个人都对」，整件事根本不会发生。
3. **承认没赢比硬要赢体面得多。** 同时期的反例：达尔文与华莱士 1858 年关于自然选择的「联合发表」，两个独立发现者一起在林奈学会公布，两人事业都因此加分而非受损。牛顿选了相反的路。
4. **记号本身没有国籍**。爱国主义只是表象，真正的病根是把「忠于人」误当作「忠于科学」。

## Act I — Two independent inventions (1665–1684)

### Newton at Woolsthorpe, 1665–66

In 1665, Cambridge closed for the plague. Newton, twenty-three, went home to his mother's farm at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire and there, *in less than two years, alone*, invented:

- the method of fluxions (differential calculus)
- the inverse method of fluxions (integral calculus)
- the binomial series
- the law of universal gravitation
- the theory of colours

He called this period his *annus mirabilis* — his miraculous year. He did not publish any of it. Newton was thirty before he showed even a fraction of the calculus work outside Cambridge, and even then only in private letters and circulated manuscripts. *Why* is part of his character — Newton was preternaturally cautious about priority, perhaps because his early disputes with Robert Hooke over optics had been bruising. He kept his discoveries close.

The unpublished 1669 manuscript **De Analysi per Aequationes Numero Terminorum Infinitas** ("On Analysis by Equations Unlimited in Their Number of Terms") laid out the method of fluxions on paper. Newton's friend John Collins made a copy. The copy circulated, quietly, among English mathematicians who could be trusted.

### Leibniz in Paris, 1672–1676

Leibniz arrived in Paris in 1672 on a diplomatic mission, twenty-six years old and dazzlingly out of his depth in mathematics. He met Christiaan Huygens, who took pity and gave him reading lists. Within four years Leibniz had taught himself enough mathematics to overtake almost everyone alive.

In **October 1675**, Leibniz wrote down — for the first time — the symbol $\int$ for "summa" (sum). On the same page, $dx$ for the differential. Within months he had the chain rule, the product rule, and a coherent algebraic system that handled tangents, areas, volumes, and arc lengths in a unified language.

He visited London in **1673** and again in **October 1676**. On the second visit he saw, briefly, in John Collins's possession, a copy of Newton's *De Analysi*. He took notes — but the notes that survive show interest in Newton's series methods, not the fluxional calculus per se. (Modern scholars have settled this. Leibniz's calculus was already invented before the 1676 visit; what he saw of Newton's work didn't materially help him.)

In **June and October 1676**, Newton wrote the **Epistola Prior** and **Epistola Posterior** — two letters to Leibniz via Oldenburg. The Epistola Posterior contains an *anagram*:

> *"6accdæ13eff7i3l9n4o4qrr4s8t12vx"*

Decoded, the anagram says (in Latin): *"Given any equation involving any number of fluent quantities, to find the fluxions, and conversely."* Newton was claiming priority over the FTC (Fundamental **Theorem** of Calculus — "theorem" not "theory"; a stubbornly common slip) by writing it in cipher — staking the flag without revealing the method. It is the most Newton thing he ever did.

### Leibniz publishes — 1684

Leibniz, by contrast, published. In October 1684 in the *Acta Eruditorum* — the leading German scientific journal — he printed **"Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis"** ("New Method for Maxima and Minima"). Six pages. The world's first calculus paper.

It contained the differential calculus essentially as we still teach it: $d(uv) = u\,dv + v\,du$, $d(u/v) = (v\,du - u\,dv)/v^2$, the chain rule, and a sample optimisation problem. In **1686** Leibniz published the integral calculus in a follow-up paper: $\int$ as the inverse of $d$, the substitution rule, the connection between area and antiderivative.

These two papers, by the standards of the day, *were* the calculus to anyone outside Newton's intimate circle in England. The **Bernoullis** (Jakob and Johann) read them in Basel and immediately ran with the system. Within a decade, "the Leibnizian calculus" was a teachable, taught subject. Newton's fluxions were still a private affair in Cambridge.

## Act II — The slow burn (1684–1710)

For fifteen years, Newton and Leibniz coexisted uneasily. Newton kept hinting in correspondence that he had invented it all first; Leibniz mostly let it slide. *Principia Mathematica* (1687) used calculus everywhere but disguised its structure in geometric proofs in the style of Euclid — a strategic retreat that meant most readers couldn't see the calculus even though it was working under the hood.

Then in **1699**, Nicolas **Fatio de Duillier**, a Swiss mathematician briefly close to Newton, published a pamphlet that explicitly accused Leibniz of stealing. Leibniz protested. Newton publicly distanced himself from Fatio (their personal relationship had cooled). The accusation died down — but the seed was planted.

In **1704** Newton finally published his calculus. The vehicle was an appendix to his *Opticks*, called *De Quadratura Curvarum* — "On the Quadrature of Curves." Twenty years after Leibniz's "Nova Methodus." The fluxional notation $\dot{x}$, $\ddot{x}$ made its first formal printed appearance there.

In **1708**, John Keill — a Scottish Newton loyalist — published a paper in the *Philosophical Transactions* that revived the plagiarism charge in unmistakable terms. Leibniz, by then sixty-two and slowing down, formally complained to the Royal Society and asked them to disavow Keill or to investigate.

He was about to learn what investigation by the Royal Society meant when Newton was its president.

## Act III — The Royal Society's verdict, 1712

![[calculus-priority-rs-verdict.png|697]]

In response to Leibniz's complaint, the Royal Society in 1711 appointed an investigating committee. The committee was billed as "neutral" and "international." It was neither. The members were drawn from Newton's London circle. The committee did not invite Leibniz to testify. It did not publish a transparent process.

In **1712** the committee published its report under the title **Commercium Epistolicum D. Johannis Collins, et Aliorum de Analysi Promota** — "The Correspondence of John Collins and Others on the Promotion of Analysis." The report concluded:

1. Newton invented his method of fluxions in 1666.
2. Leibniz had access to Newton's manuscripts via the 1676 visit and via correspondence.
3. Therefore Leibniz's calculus was substantially derivative of Newton's.

In **January 1715**, an anonymous *review* of the *Commercium Epistolicum* appeared in the *Philosophical Transactions* — a long essay that summarised the report's verdict, declared Leibniz a plagiarist with finality, and added several rhetorical flourishes ("the second inventor has no right").

**Newton wrote both the report and the anonymous review.** His authorship of the review was confirmed in the early twentieth century when the manuscript was found in his hand. *He served as president of the Royal Society, presided over the committee, drafted the verdict, and then anonymously reviewed his own verdict in the Society's journal — and signed nothing.*

It is one of the most thorough cases of academic self-dealing on record. Modern historians use it as a case study in how institutions can be captured.

## Act IV — Leibniz alone (1716) and Newton in glory (1727)

Leibniz spent his last years in Hanover, increasingly isolated. His patron, the Duke of Hanover, became King George I of England in 1714 — and pointedly *did not* bring Leibniz to London. (The Hanoverian court had decided Leibniz's continued presence was politically inconvenient given the Royal Society verdict; Newton was now George's most prominent English subject.)

Leibniz died in **November 1716**, aged seventy. His funeral was attended by his secretary alone. The Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris, of which he was a foreign member, did publish a eulogy. The Royal Society in London did not.

He had drafted, but not published, a defense — the *Charta Volans* ("Flying Sheet") — in which the Bernoullis would later vindicate him. But the public verdict at the moment of his death was the Royal Society's, and that verdict was that he was a fraud.

Newton lived another eleven years, secure in his presidency, his peerage (Sir Isaac since 1705), and his international reputation. He died in **March 1727**. He was given a state funeral, his pall borne by the Lord Chancellor and three earls. He was interred in **Westminster Abbey**, in the nave, where his monument still stands. His epitaph (by Pope, written for a separate monument):

> *Nature and Nature's laws lay hid in night;*
> *God said, Let Newton be! and all was light.*

A few months later, the *Encyclopædia Britannica* and the major French scientific journals printed obituaries identifying Newton as the inventor of calculus.

## Act V — Britain's century in the notation wilderness

The verdict had unintended consequences.

The continent kept Leibniz's notation: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, $\int$, $d$. British mathematicians, out of patriotic loyalty to Newton, kept Newton's: $\dot{y}$ for the first derivative, $\ddot{y}$ for the second, fluxions and fluents.

These look superficially equivalent. They are not. Leibniz's notation treats $dy$ and $dx$ as separable algebraic objects, which makes:

- the chain rule look like fraction-cancellation: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}$;
- separation of variables in ODEs look like algebra: $g(y)\,dy = f(x)\,dx$;
- $u$-substitution look like substitution: $du = g'(x)\,dx$;
- the implicit-differentiation differential form (see [[Implicit Differentiation]]) work at all.

Newton's $\dot{y}$ does none of these. It is opaque, atomic, and forces every operation to be a separate theorem rather than a manipulation of symbols.

![[calculus-priority-notation-comparison.svg|697]]

> [!info] A note on the *third* notation — Lagrange's prime
> While the dot-vs-d war raged, **Joseph-Louis Lagrange** (1736–1813) introduced a third option in *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* (Paris, 1797): the **prime notation** $f'(x)$. Lagrange's project was philosophical — he wanted to ground calculus in algebra, without infinitesimals or limits — and his notation followed: $f'$, $f''$, $f'''$, with cleaner typography than either Leibniz's $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or Newton's $\dot{y}$.
>
> Lagrange lost his philosophical project (limits won; algebra without infinitesimals turned out to be impossible to make rigorous), but his notation *survived*, because the cleaner typography is genuinely useful when stating theorems and writing functional proofs. Modern mathematics uses **all three** notations, each in its niche:
>
> - **Leibniz $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$** — for differentiation in context. The chain rule, separation of variables, $u$-substitution, implicit differentiation, anything algebraic on the differentials. Dominant for working calculations.
> - **Lagrange $f'(x)$** — for the derivative as a stand-alone object. Statement of theorems (FTC1 reads cleaner as "$F'(x) = f(x)$" than "$\frac{dF}{dx} = f$"), proof writing, and the inverse-function rule $g'(x) = 1/f'(g(x))$.
> - **Newton $\dot{y}$** — survives in *physics*, exclusively for time derivatives in classical mechanics. The dot's compactness keeps equations of motion legible. (In Cambridge mechanics M1, you'll see $v = \dot{s}$, $a = \dot{v} = \ddot{s}$ — that's Newton's notation, alive and well, just confined to its proper niche.)
>
> So the true outcome of the notation war is *all three* notations sharing modern mathematics, each doing what it does best. The British were right that Newton's notation has uses — they were just wrong that it had to do *every* job. Lagrange's notation is the quiet third winner.

For the next **century** while the dot-vs-d war was unresolved, mathematicians on the continent — the Bernoullis, Euler, d'Alembert, Lagrange, Laplace, Legendre, Cauchy, Fourier, Gauss, Poisson, Riemann — built modern analysis on the back of Leibniz's notation. British mathematicians, encumbered by the dot, fell behind. The eighteenth-century roll call of British analysts is short: Maclaurin, Taylor, a few others. By 1800, *the Continent led Britain in essentially every branch of mathematics*. Cambridge undergraduates studied a national pride that hadn't actually produced new mathematics in eighty years.

## Act VI — Babbage, Herschel, Peacock, and the d-ist revolution (1812–1830s)

In **1812**, three Cambridge undergraduates founded the **Analytical Society**:

- **Charles Babbage** (later: designer of the Difference Engine and Analytical Engine, the conceptual ancestor of the computer)
- **John Herschel** (later: astronomer, photography pioneer, son of the discoverer of Uranus)
- **George Peacock** (later: Lowndean Professor of Astronomy, foundational algebraist)

They had a slogan, witty enough to survive intact for two centuries:

> ***"The principles of pure d-ism in opposition to the dot-age of the university."***

Pun fully intentional: *"d-ism"* is the Leibniz $d$-notation; *"dot-age"* puns Newton's dot notation against the senile decline of British mathematics. ("Dotage" — old age, declining faculties.) The slogan was a manifesto.

In **1816**, the Analytical Society translated **Sylvestre Lacroix's** *Traité Élémentaire du Calcul Différentiel et du Calcul Intégral* (1802) into English, in Leibniz notation, as a Cambridge textbook. Babbage, Herschel, and Peacock co-authored the translation. It became the calculus text at Cambridge.

By **1820** Cambridge examinations had switched to Leibniz notation. By **1830** Britain was producing calculus papers indistinguishable in notation from continental ones. Within a generation — really within twenty years — British mathematics caught up. **George Boole** (algebraic logic), **George Green** (Green's theorem), **Arthur Cayley** (matrix theory), **James Clerk Maxwell** (electromagnetism, in vector calculus that *only* works in Leibniz notation), **William Rowan Hamilton** (quaternions): all built on the new foundation.

Babbage himself moved on from notational reform to designing programmable mechanical computers. The Analytical Engine — a general-purpose programmable machine, never built in his lifetime — ran on punch-card programs that **Ada Lovelace** wrote in 1843. The conceptual descent of the Analytical Engine through Turing to the modern computer is direct.

So: a notation reform led by three Cambridge twenty-somethings in 1812 also produced — by indirection — the first conceptual computer thirty years later. The story has a payoff that even the participants didn't see.

## Cultural ripples

**The longest priority dispute in mathematics ever settled.** It took roughly two hundred years from Newton's *De Analysi* (1669) to the modern consensus (mid-19th century) that Newton and Leibniz invented calculus *independently*. The modern consensus also notes:

- Newton's discovery is *earlier* (1665–66 vs 1675–76).
- Leibniz's *publication* is earlier (1684 vs 1704).
- Leibniz's *notation* is the one we use, and the one that won the fight.

**The template for "did X steal from Y" disputes.** The Newton-Leibniz pattern recurs throughout science: Watson and Crick's use of Rosalind Franklin's Photo 51 (DNA, 1953); Newton himself vs Hooke (gravity, 1670s); Tesla vs Edison ([[Stories/The War of the Currents]] — see also S9 in queue); Mendel ignored 1865, vindicated 1900; the Anning/de la Beche question (paleontology, 1820s); a hundred others. Every time, the *first to publish* wins the textbook line; the *first to discover* wins the historical footnote. Sometimes neither wins anything during their lifetime.

**Notation as a thinking tool.** This is the lesson that most cleanly survives. Notation isn't a way of recording results — it's a way of *enabling* them. The right notation makes hard things easy and shows you connections you'd otherwise miss; the wrong notation makes you work for results that ought to be obvious. Babbage's "d-ism" line is more than a pun: it captures the conviction that *how you write determines what you can think*.

That conviction has ripples in modern mathematics. **Vector and tensor notation** in the 19th-20th century (Maxwell, Heaviside, Einstein) made electromagnetism and general relativity tractable. **Bra-ket notation** (Dirac, 1939) made quantum mechanics computable. **Big-O notation** (Bachmann 1894, Landau 1909, popularised by Knuth) made asymptotic analysis a casual tool. **Lambda calculus** (Church 1936) made computation a mathematical object. Every one of these is a Leibniz move at a different scale.

**The cost of refusing to share a discovery.** Newton's secrecy is the self-inflicted wound that *created* the dispute. He had the calculus on paper in 1666; the manuscript *De Analysi* circulated privately in 1669; he didn't publish formally until 1704 — *thirty-eight years* of sitting on it. If he had published in 1669, Leibniz's later independent invention would have been recognised as exactly that — a beautiful demonstration that the right ideas were in the air — and the Royal Society would have had nothing to fight about. The whole dispute is downstream of one man's refusal to publish.

**The dignity of admitting an independent discovery.** Two people independently inventing the same thing is *not a tie* in any morally interesting sense — it's a *triumph*, evidence that the idea was ripe. Both inventors are vindicated, not diminished. The contrast is **Wallace and Darwin** in 1858: when Wallace's letter on natural selection arrived from the Malay Archipelago, Darwin (with the help of Lyell and Hooker) arranged a *joint* presentation at the Linnean Society. Both men recognised the independent discovery and both careers were strengthened, not weakened, by the joint announcement. Newton chose the opposite path. The Wallace-Darwin precedent existed; Newton was institutionally capable of doing what Darwin did. He chose not to.

**Patriotism and mathematics.** The notation war that followed *was* about national pride — but national pride was the symptom, not the disease. The disease was Newton's character spreading downstream into institutional behaviour: a Royal Society that confused defending Newton with defending British science, and a generation of British mathematicians that confused loyalty to the man with loyalty to mathematics. Whenever a community's identity gets attached to a *person* or *formalism* rather than to *results*, the community pays an opportunity cost. The lesson generalises beyond mathematics — any field where the founder cult outlives the founder is at risk of the same trap.

## The modern verdict

The settled position of historians of mathematics, reached around 1850 and unchanged since:

- **Newton and Leibniz invented calculus independently.**
- Newton's discovery preceded Leibniz's by about ten years.
- Leibniz's publication preceded Newton's by about twenty years.
- The two systems are mathematically equivalent.
- Leibniz's notation is superior — and has won universally, including in Britain.
- The Royal Society verdict of 1712 is a stain on the Society's institutional history. Modern members of the Royal Society are quietly embarrassed by it.

**Both** are credited as co-discoverers in every modern textbook. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (see [[Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]]) is sometimes called the **Newton-Leibniz formula** — 牛顿–莱布尼茨公式 in Chinese — exactly to acknowledge the dual origin.

## Receipts

Primary and secondary sources, informally cited:

- **Newton's notebooks and the *De Analysi* manuscript** are at Cambridge University Library; selections are published in D. T. Whiteside, *The Mathematical Papers of Isaac Newton* (Cambridge, 1967–81, 8 volumes).
- **Leibniz's mathematical papers** are at Hanover, in the *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe* (Akademie-Verlag, ongoing). The 1684 *Acta Eruditorum* paper is online via several open archives.
- **Commercium Epistolicum** (Royal Society, 1712 — second edition 1722). Newton's authorship of the anonymous 1715 review was established by L. T. More, *Isaac Newton: A Biography* (1934).
- The standard modern history is **A. Rupert Hall**, *Philosophers at War: The Quarrel between Newton and Leibniz* (Cambridge, 1980). Even-handed, fully documented.
- For the British stagnation and the Analytical Society: **Niccolò Guicciardini**, *The Development of Newtonian Calculus in Britain, 1700–1800* (Cambridge, 1989).
- Babbage's own account of the Analytical Society is in his autobiography, *Passages from the Life of a Philosopher* (1864). The "d-ism vs dot-age" slogan is from there, chapter 4.
- For a popular treatment: **Jason Socrates Bardi**, *The Calculus Wars: Newton, Leibniz, and the Greatest Mathematical Clash of All Time* (Thunder's Mouth Press, 2006). Less rigorous than Hall but more readable.
- Wikipedia's "Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy" is a competent starting point and links to the primary sources.
