# Erdős the Wandering Mathematician 流浪的数学家埃尔德什

> *He never owned a house, a car, a chequebook or, for most of his life, a job. He arrived on doorsteps with one half-empty suitcase and the words "my brain is open", stayed until the paper was written, and left. He did this for sixty years, wrote fifteen hundred papers with five hundred people, and died at a conference with a problem on the board.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Paul Erdős** (1913–1996) — Budapest-born, the most prolific mathematician since Euler; number theory, combinatorics, probability, graph theory. No fixed address after 1934. *Erdős* is pronounced roughly "AIR-dish".
- **Anna Erdős** (1880–1971) — his mother, a mathematics teacher who lost two daughters on the day he was born and guarded the son with a ferocity he never escaped and never wanted to; travelled with him from her seventies until her death in Calgary.
- **Ronald Graham** (1935–2020) — Bell Labs, later UC San Diego; juggler, trampolinist, Erdős's friend, banker, secretary and executor; kept the "Erdős room" in his house and answered his mail.
- **Atle Selberg** (1917–2007) — the Norwegian who, with Erdős and then bitterly without him, found the elementary proof of the prime number theorem in 1948–49; Fields Medal 1950.
- **George Szekeres** (1911–2005) and **Esther Klein** (1910–2005) — the Budapest friends whose 1933 problem about points in the plane became "the Happy Ending problem", because they married.
- **The Supreme Fascist (SF)** — Erdős's name for God, who keeps the Book of perfect proofs and shows it to no one.

## 中文锚点

**埃尔德什**（Paul Erdős，1913–1996）是二十世纪发表论文最多的数学家：一千五百多篇，合作者五百一十一人——数学界至今用"**埃尔德什数**"来量自己离他多远：与他合著过的人是 1，与 1 合著过的是 2，全世界数学家里，埃尔德什数的中位数是 5。他一辈子**没有家、没有工作、没有银行账户**，所有家当装在一只半空的手提箱里，从 1934 年离开匈牙利起，六十年间在同事家的门口出现，说一句"**我家大脑常开门**"（化用《北京欢迎你》的"我家大门常打开"，my brain is open，欢迎一切问题进来），住到论文写完就走，一年飞十几个国家。他有自己的一套语言：小孩叫"ε"（epsilon，无穷小），上帝叫"**至高法西斯**"（他藏着一本收录所有最完美证明的"**天书**"），"死了"指不再做数学，真正去世叫"**走了**"。他把奖金全部送人——沃尔夫奖五万美元，自己留了七百二十块——转而给自己解不出的问题标价，从二十五美元到一万美元，至今仍有人在领。这张卡讲三件事：**他为什么这样活**（出生那天两个姐姐死于猩红热，母亲把他护得太紧，他到二十一岁还不会自己给面包抹黄油；犹太人，1934 年逃离匈牙利，家族大半死于大屠杀），**他留下了什么数学**（**拉姆齐理论**：六个人的聚会里一定有三个人互相认识或三个人互不相识——这张卡真的把三万多种情形全部算了一遍；**概率方法**：不用构造，只要证明随机对象"平均下来"就满足条件，就一定存在一个满足的——这是现代组合学的支柱），以及**两条并不好看的真相**：1979 年格雷厄姆拿五百美元跟他打赌，赌他做不到一个月不碰安非他命，他硬是做到了，赢了赌局，然后说"你让数学倒退了一个月"；1949 年他和塞尔伯格为素数定理初等证明的归属决裂，塞尔伯格拿了菲尔兹奖，两人再没和好。1996 年 9 月 20 日，他在华沙的一场组合学会议上心脏病发作，"走了"，黑板上还有题。

## The Story

### Act I — The day he was born

On 26 March 1913, while Anna Erdős was in the hospital giving birth to her son, her two daughters, aged three and five, died at home of scarlet fever. She came back to an empty house with a baby. What she did with the rest of her life follows from that day: the boy was kept out of school, taught at home, kept from other children's germs, dressed and fed and protected until, by his own cheerful account, he could not butter a piece of bread until he was twenty-one and tried it for the first time in an English boarding house. "It wasn't so hard," he reported.

Both parents were mathematics teachers; the father, Lajos, spent the boy's early childhood in a Siberian prison camp after capture in the First World War. Paul, alone with his mother and a governess, did arithmetic. At three he could multiply three-digit numbers in his head; at four he discovered negative numbers on his own by working out that 100 minus 250 was "150 below zero", and told his mother, who was not impressed. At seventeen, in a Budapest that was producing Szilárd, Wigner, Teller and von Neumann in the same decade ([[von Neumann the Martian]]), he found a new proof of Chebyshev's theorem that there is always a prime between $n$ and $2n$ — a proof short enough to be the one students learn now — and at twenty-one had his doctorate.

Then 1934. A Jewish mathematician in Hungary in 1934 did not need a theorem to see what was coming. He took a fellowship in Manchester, then Princeton in 1938. Of his family in Hungary, four of his five aunts and uncles and most of his cousins were murdered in 1944; his father had died of a heart attack in 1942; his mother survived. He did not go home for over a decade, and when at last he did, he never again stayed anywhere for long.

### Act II — My brain is open

What began as exile became a method. From the 1940s until his death he had no home. He owned what fitted in one suitcase and one plastic bag from a Budapest department store: two changes of clothes, a raincoat, a few papers. He would land in a city, telephone a mathematician, and announce himself at the door: *"My brain is open."* The host would take him in — one did not refuse — and for a week or a fortnight Erdős would work with them from the moment they woke, propose problems, stay up past midnight, and then move on to the next house, where the same thing happened. He did this in some twenty-five countries a year, well into his eighties.

![[erdos-brain-is-open-comic.png|720]]

*The doorstep, about 1975. The suitcase is half empty because he owned almost nothing; the plastic bag holds the mathematics. The host's face is the face of every mathematician who ever answered that door: this will be exhausting, and it will be the best fortnight of the year.*

The hosts did everything else. Erdős could not cook, drive, write a cheque or, in his later years, tie his shoes without help; Ron Graham managed his money and mail from a room in his own house, and a rota of friends kept him fed. In return the guest gave the one thing he had, which was a river of problems — precise, fresh, and always chosen to be just within the host's reach — and the habit of working on them together. Fifteen hundred papers came out of this, with five hundred and eleven coauthors, more than any mathematician in history. The community invented a joke to measure it: your **Erdős number** is 1 if you wrote a paper with him, 2 if you wrote one with someone who did, and so on. It stopped being a joke when someone counted.

![[erdos-number-distribution.svg|780]]

*The Erdős Number Project's count in 2004: one person at distance 0, five hundred at 1, and the bulk of working mathematics at 4 and 5. The median mathematician is five papers from him. The graph is the shortest-path problem of [[Graphs]] run over a century of journals, and it says something the papers alone do not: the whole discipline is one connected component, and he sits near its centre.*

He had a private language, and everyone who worked with him learned it. Children were **epsilons** (the mathematician's arbitrarily small quantity), and he was gentle and funny with them. Women were bosses, men were slaves; to marry was to be captured; to give a lecture was to preach. God was **the Supreme Fascist**, the SF, who kept the **Book** — the transfinite volume in which every theorem has its one perfect proof — and whose game with mankind was to hide it. The highest praise Erdős gave a proof was "that one's from the Book". A mathematician who stopped doing mathematics had "died"; one who had actually died had "left". The joke had a purpose: it let a man who had lost most of his family talk about the SF's cruelties without once saying the plain words.

He gave money away as fast as it came. When he shared the Wolf Prize in 1983–84, fifty thousand dollars, he kept seven hundred and twenty and gave the rest to relatives, students, and a scholarship in Israel in his parents' names. What he spent on instead was **problems**: he attached cash prizes to the ones he could not solve, from twenty-five dollars to ten thousand, and paid up personally, by cheque, when someone did. Several are still outstanding; the three-thousand-dollar question — whether any set of integers whose reciprocals sum to infinity must contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions — is open as this card is written, and a cheque for ten thousand dollars was cashed in 2014 for large gaps between primes.

### Act III — Six people at a party

The mathematics he left is mostly of one flavour: questions a child can state and nobody can answer. The cleanest is the one Szekeres and Klein were arguing about in Budapest in 1933, and which Erdős turned into a field.

*Six strangers are at a party. Any two of them are either friends or not. Show that there must be three who are all friends, or three who are all strangers.* Draw the six as points and colour each of the fifteen pairs red (friends) or blue (strangers); the claim is that some triangle is all one colour.

![[erdos-ramsey-party.svg|880]]

*Tool: the pigeonhole principle, twice.* Pick anyone. She has five relations, coloured with two colours, so at least three are the same — say three red. Look at those three people. If any pair of them is red, that pair with her makes a red triangle. If no pair is red, all three pairs are blue, and they make a blue triangle. Done — and five people are not enough, because the pentagon colouring on the left has no one-coloured triangle at all. The script beside this card checks both by brute force:

```
five people, sides red / diagonals blue, mono triangle? False
six people: 32768 colourings tried, 0 without a monochromatic triangle  -> R(3,3) = 6
```

That number, six, is the **Ramsey number** $R(3,3)$ — the smallest party that forces a one-coloured triple. Frank Ramsey proved in 1928 that such numbers always exist; Erdős and Szekeres, in 1935, made the subject, and the theme of Ramsey theory is his: *complete disorder is impossible*. Colour a large enough structure any way you like and some sub-structure will be perfectly regular. How large is "large enough" is where the trouble starts. $R(4,4) = 18$ was found in 1955. $R(5,5)$ is known only to lie between 43 and 46, after seventy years of computing. Erdős's own estimate of the difficulty: if an alien force demanded the value of $R(5,5)$ or it would destroy the Earth, we should marshal every computer and mathematician and find it; if it demanded $R(6,6)$, we should attack the aliens.

### Act IV — The proof that builds nothing

His deepest idea is an argument that proves things exist by refusing to find them. In 1947 he wanted a *lower* bound for $R(k,k)$: a party small enough that it *need not* contain $k$ mutual friends or $k$ mutual strangers. Constructing such a colouring is hopeless for large $k$. So he did not construct it. He said: colour every pair **at random**, a coin toss each, and *count the expected number* of one-coloured $k$-sets.

*Tool: linearity of expectation* ([[Probability Basics]]): the expected number of bad $k$-sets is the number of $k$-sets, $\binom{n}{k}$, times the probability that a given one is all red or all blue, $2 \cdot 2^{-\binom{k}{2}}$. If that expectation is **less than one**, then some colouring — he cannot say which — has *zero* bad sets, because an average below one over non-negative integers forces at least one zero. The arithmetic gives $n \approx 2^{k/2}$:

```
k=10: with n = 2^(k/2) =       32, expected monochromatic K_k = 3.67e-06  (< 1, so some colouring has none: R(10,10) > 32)
k=20: with n = 2^(k/2) =     1024, expected monochromatic K_k = 6.98e-16  (< 1, so some colouring has none: R(20,20) > 1024)
k=40: with n = 2^(k/2) =  1048576, expected monochromatic K_k = 2.57e-42
```

This is the **probabilistic method**, and it is now a pillar of combinatorics and computer science: to show a good object exists, show that a random one is good on average. The lower bound $R(k,k) > 2^{k/2}$ has barely been improved since 1947 — every construction anyone has ever written down does worse than a coin — which is the strangest thing about it. The best way to find a needle in this haystack is to prove the haystack is mostly needles.

The same taste produced, with Mark Kac in 1940, the **Erdős–Kac theorem**: the number of distinct prime factors of a random large integer is normally distributed, with mean and variance both $\ln \ln n$ — the primes obeying the bell curve of [[Normal Distribution]]. Kac had the probability, Erdős the number theory, and the theorem was finished, in Kac's account, in the twenty minutes after a lecture at which Erdős had been dozing. "Probabilistic number theory" dates from that afternoon.

### Act V — The bet, and the feud

Two honest edges, and they belong in the story rather than in a footnote, because both were chosen.

**The bet.** After his mother's death in 1971 Erdős took amphetamines — Benzedrine, later Ritalin — daily, and worked nineteen-hour days on them for twenty-five years. Friends worried. In 1979 Ron Graham bet him five hundred dollars that he could not stay off them for a month. He stopped, cold, for thirty days, collected the money, and delivered his verdict: *"You've set mathematics back a month."* He had, he said, spent the month staring at blank paper, unable to start; the ideas had not come. Then he went back to the pills and never stopped again.

![[erdos-graham-bet-comic.png|720]]

*The month, and the settlement. He was seventy-six when he took the bet and eighty-three when he left; the card does not pretend to know whether Graham was right to worry or Erdős right to refuse. Both thought they were protecting mathematics.*

**The feud.** In 1948 Erdős and Atle Selberg each held a piece of a thing everyone had said was impossible: an *elementary* proof of the prime number theorem — the count of primes below $x$ is about $x / \ln x$ — using no complex analysis. Selberg had a powerful new inequality; Erdős, hearing of it, used it within days to prove the step Selberg needed, and told people. Selberg, who had wanted to finish alone, felt his result had been taken from him; Erdős felt he had been promised a joint paper. They published separately in 1949, Selberg received the Fields Medal in 1950, and the two men did not reconcile. The mathematics was a triumph. The friendship was the price, and the record — letters on both sides, examined by historians since — does not exonerate either of them.

### Act VI — Leaving

He never slowed. In his seventies he was still publishing fifty papers a year; in his eighties he was still on the doorstep. He said he wanted to die in the middle of a lecture, having just proved a theorem, with someone in the audience asking "but what about the general case?" — and to reply "I think I'll leave that to the next generation" and fall over. He nearly got it. On 20 September 1996, at a combinatorics conference in Warsaw, he had a heart attack in his hotel room between sessions, a problem from the morning still on the board. He was eighty-three. He left; in his own dialect, he had not died, because he had never stopped.

## Honest edges

- **The drugs are not a colourful detail; they were his working method for twenty-five years.** The card reports the bet and his verdict without endorsing them. Graham and Hoffman, who knew him best, disagreed about whether the pills helped the mathematics or merely the stamina.
- **The Selberg affair has two sides.** Erdős's admirers tell it as a theft by Selberg of shared credit; Selberg's, as Erdős forcing himself into another man's result. Goldfeld's 2003 reconstruction from the correspondence finds fault and grievance on both sides and settles nothing.
- **The saint's life is partly a construction.** Erdős cultivated the eccentricities — the dialect, the suitcase, the prizes — and enjoyed being the story. That the legend is largely true does not mean it was not curated.
- **The cost fell on others.** A man with no home is a man whose hosts cook, drive, do his laundry and answer his letters. Graham did his taxes for decades. The gift of problems was real and the debt was real, and most hosts would say the exchange favoured them.

## Cultural ripples

- The **Erdős number** is the origin of every "six degrees" game; the Erdős–Bacon number combines it with the film version, and a few people (Natalie Portman, Danica McKellar) have both.
- **Proofs from THE BOOK** (Aigner and Ziegler, 1998) was begun as an eighty-fifth birthday present and published after he left; it is now in its sixth edition, and the phrase "a Book proof" is standard.
- *The Man Who Loved Only Numbers* (Hoffman, 1998) and *My Brain Is Open* (Schechter, 1998) are the two biographies; George Csicsery's documentary *N Is a Number* (1993) has him on camera, in the suitcase years.
- The quotation "a mathematician is a machine for turning coffee into theorems" is attributed to him everywhere and was said by his friend Alfréd Rényi; Erdős merely lived it.

## Where this surfaces in the vault

- **[[Graphs]]** — the Erdős number is a shortest-path problem on the coauthorship graph, and Ramsey's theorem is a statement about colourings of the complete graph; the party proof is the pigeonhole principle applied to a vertex's degree.
- **[[Probability Basics]]** — linearity of expectation and the "average below one forces a zero" step are the entire engine of the probabilistic method; [[Normal Distribution]] — the Erdős–Kac bell curve over the primes.
- **[[Prime Numbers (Vocab)]]** — Chebyshev's theorem (a prime between $n$ and $2n$) in the proof a seventeen-year-old found, and the prime number theorem behind the feud.
- **[[Fun Is the Brachistochrone]]** — the purest case in the vault of a chosen target: no syllabus, no employer, no assignment, sixty years of problems he picked himself; and the honest limit of that card, that the hunt was funded by other people's kitchens.
- **[[von Neumann the Martian]]** — the same Budapest, the same decade, the opposite temperament: one built the machine, the other refused to own a chair.
