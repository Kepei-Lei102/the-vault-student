# Famous for the Wrong Thing 费曼：出名出错了地方

> *He won the Nobel Prize for a way of drawing pictures that lets you compute the magnetism of an electron to eleven decimal places. Almost nobody who knows his name knows that. They know the safes, the bongos, the glass of ice water, the book with the funny title, and a study method he never wrote down. This is the story of a man who became famous for everything except the thing, and of why the thing was the same as everything else.*

## Cast of Characters

- **Richard Feynman** (1918–1988) — Far Rockaway, Queens; MIT, Princeton, Los Alamos, Cornell, Caltech. Nobel Prize in Physics 1965 for quantum electrodynamics. Safecracker, drummer, storyteller, and the most watched witness of the *Challenger* inquiry.
- **Melville Feynman** (1890–1946) — his father, a uniform salesman who taught the boy that knowing the name of a bird tells you nothing about the bird, and that a man in a uniform is only a man.
- **Arline Greenbaum** (1920–1945) — his first wife, married while she was dying of tuberculosis; the author of the sentence *"What do you care what other people think?"*
- **Julian Schwinger** (1918–1994) and **Sin-Itiro Tomonaga** (1906–1979) — the two who shared the 1965 prize, having reached the same physics by equations rather than pictures.
- **Freeman Dyson** (1923–2020) — the young Englishman who, on a Greyhound bus in 1948, saw that Feynman's pictures and Schwinger's equations were the same thing, and wrote the paper that made the pictures win.
- **General Donald Kutyna** (b. 1933) — Air Force officer on the Rogers Commission, who steered Feynman toward the O-rings with a story about a carburettor.
- **Ralph Leighton** (b. 1949) — the drumming partner who taped the stories and turned them into *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*

## 中文锚点

**理查德·费曼**（Richard Feynman，1918–1988）是二十世纪最出名的物理学家之一，但他出名的地方几乎全"错"了。他 1965 年拿诺贝尔奖，是因为**量子电动力学**：一套用**小图画**做计算的方法——每张图是事件发生的一种可能路径，把所有图加起来，就算出电子的磁性，和实验结果相符，一直到**小数点后十一位**（这张卡真的把这个数算了一遍）。可是记住这件事的人很少。人们记住的是：他在**洛斯阿拉莫斯**造原子弹的那几年，把存放全部机密的保险柜一个个打开，留下纸条"猜猜是谁？"（保险柜标着一百万种组合，他发现机械公差让每个转盘只剩二十个有效位置，于是一百万变成八千；再加上物理学家都爱用 π 和 e 当密码——这正是 [[Data Security]] 里"密码强度取决于攻击者知道什么"的那一课）；他在康奈尔食堂里看一个**盘子**在空中摇晃，为了好玩去算晃动和旋转的比例，结果一路算到了诺贝尔奖；他在巴西的桑巴乐队里打**鼓**；他花十年想去一个叫**图瓦**的地方，只因为它的邮票好看；他写了一本叫《**别闹了，费曼先生**》的书，全世界都当笑话集读；1986 年**挑战者号**爆炸后，他在电视直播的听证会上把一个橡胶 **O 形环**夹进一杯**冰水**，几秒钟就说明了 NASA 几个月没说清的事——而这张卡把工程师在前一天晚上**没画出来**的那张图补上了：他们只画了出过事故的七次发射，看不出规律；把没出事的十六次也画上，低温和事故的关系一目了然。还有一件他自己完全不知道的事：今天全世界学生都在用的"**费曼学习法**"——把一个概念讲给小孩听、讲不通的地方就是没懂的地方——是他死后几十年别人替他起的名字，他从未写过这样一套方法（[[Stigler's Law of Eponymy]] 的又一个例子）。这张卡讲六幕：远洛克威的父亲和收音机；阿琳，那个他明知会死也娶了的姑娘，和她那句"**你管别人怎么想**"；原子弹、保险柜和一封写给亡妻的信；盘子、小图画和那个算到十一位的数；讲课、打鼓和一本书；最后是冰水，以及他留在报告附录里的那句话：**大自然不能被糊弄**。

## The Story

### Act I — Far Rockaway

Melville Feynman sold uniforms. He would take his son walking in the Catskills and say: you can learn the name of that bird in every language on Earth, and when you are done you will know nothing about the bird — now let's look at what it *does*. He would open a newspaper to a photograph of the Pope with a crowd kneeling before him and say: the difference between that man and the others is the hat. The boy grew up unable to be impressed by a uniform, which was a problem at Los Alamos and a gift at the *Challenger* hearings, and unable to mistake a name for knowledge, which turned out to be the whole of his method.

He fixed radios. In the Depression a boy who could fix a radio was worth having, and he was known in the neighbourhood as the kid who *fixes radios by thinking* — he would stand and stare at a set for a while, then reach in and swap two valves, because the fault he had been told about could only come from one place. At MIT he learned physics faster than it was taught; at Princeton, at his first tea with the dean's wife, he was asked whether he wanted cream or lemon and said "both", and she said the sentence that became the title of his book. His thesis, with John Wheeler in 1942, contained the idea he would be given the Nobel Prize for twenty-three years later: that a particle going from here to there does not take one path but, in a definite sense, takes *all* of them, and that what we see is the sum.

### Act II — Arline

Arline Greenbaum was the girl from Far Rockaway he had been in love with since he was fifteen. Around the time he finished the thesis she was diagnosed with tuberculosis, then incurable, and given a few years. His family begged him not to marry her. He borrowed a station wagon, fitted it with a mattress, drove her to Staten Island, married her before a clerk with two strangers for witnesses, and kissed her on the cheek because a kiss on the mouth could infect him. It was June 1942.

When he went to Los Alamos in 1943 she went to a sanatorium in Albuquerque, a hundred miles away, and he hitch-hiked down every weekend. They wrote every day, and because the letters passed through the Army censor, they teased it: she sent letters cut into jigsaw pieces, and once a letter in a code she had made up without enclosing the key, which the censor sent back. Her phrase for his anxieties about what his colleagues or family or superiors would say was *"What do you care what other people think?"* He used it for the rest of his life and put it on the cover of his second book.

She died on 16 June 1945, a month before the first bomb. The clock in her room had stopped at 9:21, the minute of her death, and the nurse pointed it out as a sign. He worked out that the clock was old and delicate and had stopped when the nurse picked it up to read the time in the dim room — and he wrote that reasoning down, that evening, because he could not bear a mystery even then. Sixteen months later he wrote her a letter. It was found among his papers after his death, worn from being taken out and read. *"I love you, sweetheart… Please excuse my not mailing this — but I don't know your new address."*

### Act III — The safes

At Los Alamos he was twenty-five, running the group that did the hand and machine computation for the bomb's design. Boredom is dangerous in such a man. He noticed that the filing cabinets holding the entire secret of the atomic bomb were locked with three-wheel combination locks, and he set out to understand them the way he had understood radios.

![[feynman-safecracker-comic.png|720]]

*A colleague's office, 1944. The nine filing cabinets of the Manhattan Project's theoretical division, containing every secret there was, opened one after another in an afternoon; and the note. He signed some of them "Feynman the safecracker"; this one he did not sign.*

He found two things, and the script beside this card does the arithmetic. First, the dial had a hundred numbers but the mechanism forgave an error of two either way, so only every fifth number was distinct: twenty positions per wheel, and the million combinations on the label were really eight thousand — a day's patient work rather than four months. Second, a safe standing open still has its last two numbers set on the wheels, and you can read them off the dial by feel in a few seconds while chatting to its owner. He kept a notebook of last-two-numbers for every safe on the site. What remained was one wheel and twenty tries: three minutes. When he needed a document from a colleague's cabinet and the colleague was away, he tried the physicist's favourite constants first — $\pi$ as 31-41-59, $e$ as 27-18-28 — and $e$ opened all nine cabinets, because the man had used the same number for all of them. The Army's answer, when the base locksmith let slip that about one safe in five was still on its factory setting, was a memo instructing everyone to change their combination if Feynman had been in their office.

The lesson is not about safes. A lock's strength is not the number on its label; it is the number of possibilities *the attacker's model of you* leaves open. The million became eight thousand because of the mechanism, and eight thousand became twenty because of habit, and twenty became one because physicists like $e$. [[Data Security]] tells the same story about passwords, where the substitutions everyone thinks of are the first thing every cracking tool tries.

On 16 July 1945 he watched the Trinity test through a truck windscreen, the only person present not wearing dark glasses, because he had worked out that the glass stopped the ultraviolet and the rest could not hurt him. After Hiroshima he sat in a bar in New York computing the radius of destruction of the bomb centred on every restaurant he could see, and could not understand why anyone was building a bridge. *I simply didn't think,* he said later of the years before — the honest edge of Act III, and this card keeps it.

### Act IV — The plate

At Cornell, in 1946, he could not work. Then one lunchtime someone in the cafeteria threw a plate in the air, and he watched the red Cornell medallion on its rim go round as the plate wobbled, and noticed the medallion went round faster than the wobble. He went off to work out the ratio from the equations of a spinning rigid body — two to one (his memoir remembers which of the two was faster the wrong way round; it is the wobble) — and showed Hans Bethe, who asked what the importance of it was. *"There's no importance whatsoever. I'm just doing it for fun."*

![[feynman-plate-comic.png|720]]

*The Cornell cafeteria. In his own telling the whole of the Nobel Prize came out of "piddling around with the wobbling plate": from the rotation to the electron's spin to the Dirac equation to the pictures.*

The pictures were the thing. Quantum electrodynamics — the theory of light and electrons — had been giving infinite answers since the 1930s. In 1947–48 three people fixed it. Tomonaga in Tokyo and Schwinger at Harvard did it with equations of such difficulty that at the Pocono conference in 1948 Schwinger lectured for a whole day and only Fermi and Bethe kept up. Feynman did it with drawings: a line for an electron, a wiggle for a photon, a dot where they meet, one drawing for each way the event could happen, a simple rule for turning each drawing into a number, and the instruction to add them all up. Nobody at Pocono understood him. Then Dyson, who had spent the summer driving across America with Feynman and the autumn studying Schwinger, saw on a bus in Nebraska that the two were identical, and wrote it down. Within two years everybody was drawing the pictures and nobody was doing it Schwinger's way.

![[feynman-diagram.svg|900]]

*The picture, the rule, and the number. Left: the simplest way two electrons can repel, by exchanging one photon; each dot costs a factor of the charge, so two dots gives the size of the effect and every further pair of dots gives a correction about 137 times smaller. Right: the diagram behind the Nobel. An electron in a magnetic field emits and reabsorbs its own photon, and that loop changes its magnetism by $\alpha/2\pi = 0.001161$. The script beside this card adds the first five orders — one diagram, then seven, then seventy-two, then 891, then 12 672 — and lands within $5 \times 10^{-12}$ of the value measured in 2023. No other prediction in science has been checked to that precision.*

The Nobel came in 1965, shared three ways. A reporter woke him at four in the morning to tell him; he considered refusing it, decided that refusing would make a bigger fuss than accepting, and gave a Nobel lecture that was mostly a list of the ideas that had not worked. The diagrams are now the alphabet of particle physics, printed on the side of his van, and the *number* they compute — the electron's magnetism — is the most precisely verified prediction anyone has made about anything. This is the thing he is not famous for.

### Act V — The lectures, the drums, the book

In 1961 Caltech asked him to reteach introductory physics, and for two years he gave the lectures that became *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*. He judged the experiment a failure in the preface — *I don't think I did very well by the students* — because the freshmen it was meant for could not keep up, while the graduate students and the professors filled the back rows. The books have been in print for sixty years and are free online; they are the most read physics textbook ever written, by a man who thought they had not worked.

In Brazil in 1951 he learned the *frigideira* and marched in Carnival with a samba school. At Caltech he played bongos for a ballet; the drums got into the newspapers, and he wrote a furious letter when a Swedish encyclopedia described the Nobel physicist partly in terms of his drumming, on the grounds that a scientist should not be remembered as a man who plays bongos. He was, of course, and is. In 1977, at dinner, he asked his friend Ralph Leighton whatever had become of Tannu Tuva, whose triangular stamps he had collected as a boy, and the two spent the next decade trying to get there; the invitation from Moscow arrived after his death.

Leighton also taped the stories — the safes, the tea, the Brazilian band, the Las Vegas bars — and *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985) sold more copies than any book about a physicist before it. It is why the world knows his name. Most of its readers do not know what he won the prize for, and the book barely tells them.

And then the thing he never wrote at all. Somewhere in the 2010s a four-step study recipe began to circulate online under the name **the Feynman Technique**: pick a concept, explain it to a child, find the gaps, simplify. He never named it; the nearest thing in his own hand is the notebook he kept as an undergraduate, titled *Notebook of Things I Don't Know About*, and the rule he left on his blackboard. The name is a case of [[Stigler's Law of Eponymy]] running forwards: the discoverer did not get the credit, the credit was given to a man for something he did not discover. [[The Feynman Technique]] in this vault keeps the name, because the principle under it — that understanding is the ability to rebuild — is genuinely his.

### Act VI — Ice water

On 28 January 1986 the shuttle *Challenger* broke up seventy-three seconds after launch. The presidential commission under William Rogers was mostly officials; Feynman, already ill with the cancer that would kill him, was the one member who went to the engineers rather than the managers. General Kutyna, an Air Force officer on the commission, told him one Saturday morning about the carburettor on his Corvette and how rubber seals stiffen when the weather is cold, and then said nothing more. Kutyna had heard it from the astronaut Sally Ride, who had been shown the data and could not say so.

![[feynman-ice-water-comic.png|720]]

*The public hearing, 11 February 1986. He had a piece of the O-ring rubber, a small clamp and a pair of pliers from a hardware store, and a glass of ice water he asked the staff for. He squeezed the rubber, held it in the ice for a while, and released it: it did not spring back. "I believe that has some significance for our problem." It had been thirty-one degrees Fahrenheit at the launch pad.*

The engineers at Thiokol had known. On the evening before the launch they had argued against flying in the cold, and had lost, and one of the reasons they lost is a chart. This card redraws it.

![[feynman-oring.svg|900]]

*The twenty-three flights before Challenger. Left: what the engineers put on the table — the seven flights with O-ring damage, plotted against launch temperature. There is no pattern; the worst flight was at 75 °F. Right: the same seven, plus the sixteen flights with no damage, every one of them warm. Now the pattern is impossible to miss, and the logistic curve fitted to all twenty-three (the script reproduces the 1989 coefficients exactly) predicts that at 31 °F about five of the six rings would be damaged. The clean flights were left off because nobody thought a flight with no damage was evidence. It was the only evidence that mattered.*

The commission's report has an appendix he insisted on writing alone. In it he records that the working engineers put the chance of losing a shuttle at about **1 in 100** and management at **1 in 100 000**, and asks what could justify a factor of a thousand. Over twenty-five flights, the engineers' number gives a one-in-four chance of a disaster and the managers' number gives one in four thousand; the actual record, two lost in a hundred and thirty-five, is 1 in 67. The appendix ends with the sentence that is the whole of Melville's lesson about hats, forty years on: *"For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled."*

He died on 15 February 1988. His last words were that he would hate to die twice, because it was so boring. On his blackboard at Caltech were two lines, left where the photograph found them: *What I cannot create, I do not understand*, and *Know how to solve every problem that has been solved*.

## Honest edges

- **The women.** *Surely You're Joking* contains a chapter on how to pick up women in bars by treating them with contempt, told as a joke on himself; his second wife's divorce complaint in 1956 cited that he did calculus in bed and played drums at night; the biographies record affairs, including with the wives of colleagues. The book is a self-portrait, curated by its subject and his friend from tapes, and the charm in it is real and so is the ugliness. The card keeps both.
- **Los Alamos.** He joined the project because the Germans might build the bomb first, and did not reconsider when Germany surrendered in May 1945; the celebration at Los Alamos after Hiroshima, in his own account, included him drumming on a jeep. *I simply didn't think* is his verdict, not this card's.
- **The ice water was not a solo.** The demonstration was his, and the theatre of it was entirely his. The direction came from Kutyna, and Kutyna's came from Sally Ride, who could not speak; Kutyna said so only after her death in 2012. The most famous moment of a famously independent man was a relay.
- **The anti-honours pose.** He resigned from the National Academy of Sciences because he disliked an organisation whose purpose was to decide who was worthy to join it, and he accepted the Nobel Prize, the Einstein Award and the Oersted Medal. Both things are true.

## Cultural ripples

- The **diagrams** are printed on his van, on T-shirts and on the walls of every particle-physics department; they are the standard notation of the field, and the way the electron's magnetism is computed to twelve digits today is by counting them — 12 672 at fifth order, done by computer.
- The **ice water** is the reference image for expert testimony: one person, one object, one demonstration, against a room of institutional language. Edward Tufte's *Visual Explanations* (1997) made the O-ring chart the reference case for what a missing data point costs.
- The **Feynman Technique** exists in every study-skills book and app, attributed to a man who would not have recognised the name. **Cargo cult science**, from his 1974 commencement address, is the standard phrase for research that has the form of science without the substance.
- *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, free at Caltech's site since 2013, remain the book a physics student is handed when they ask what physics is actually like.
