# Newton vs Hooke 牛顿与胡克之争

> *"If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants."*
> — Isaac Newton to Robert Hooke, **letter dated 5 February 1676**. Read for three centuries as humility. Read at the time as a *barbed pun on Hooke's height*.

> *"Mr Newton is in nothing more wanting than in candour and ingenuity."*
> — Robert Hooke, sometime around 1672, on Newton's first optics paper.

> *"There is no possibility of dealing with you safely."*
> — Newton to Hooke, March 1673, breaking off correspondence.

## Cast of Characters

- **Isaac Newton** (1642–1727) — Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge from 1669. Brilliant, secretive, vindictive. Worked alone, hated criticism, kept results in his drawer for decades. By the time he died he had won every priority fight he was in.
- **Robert Hooke** (1635–1703) — Curator of Experiments at the Royal Society from 1662; surveyor and architect of half of London after the 1666 Great Fire. Brilliant, restless, prolific, and pathologically generous with ideas — he gave away so many half-finished concepts that he ended up fighting priority disputes with almost every major scientist of his generation. Author of *Micrographia* (1665), inventor of the term **cell** in biology, formulator of **Hooke's Law** ($F = kx$), early proponent of the **wave theory of light** and the **inverse-square law of gravity**. *Short, stooped, increasingly sour as he aged.*
- **Edmond Halley** (1656–1742) — Astronomer Royal, friend of both, eventually the diplomatic bridge that pulled the *Principia* out of Newton. Without Halley, no *Principia*. (And the comet, yes.)
- **Robert Boyle** (1627–1691) — Hooke's earlier employer; Hooke built the vacuum pump that gave us Boyle's Law. Caught between Newton and Hooke later.
- **Christopher Wren** (1632–1723) — Architect of St Paul's, Royal Society president before Newton, Hooke's collaborator on London reconstruction. *Wren himself proposed the inverse-square law in 1684* — Hooke wasn't even alone in seeing it.
- **John Flamsteed** (1646–1719) — Astronomer Royal, supplier of data; got into his own ugly fight with Newton later. Bystander to the Hooke one.

## 中文锚点

**牛顿与胡克之争**：牛顿一生最长、最毒、也最被低估的一场科学优先权战争。比起后来与莱布尼茨那场关于微积分的国际大战，**牛顿 vs 胡克才是牛顿的训练场** —— 他在这场战争里发明了他的全套战术：拖延发表、匿名指控、利用学会权力打压对手、死后系统性地抹去对方痕迹。

战场两个：

1. **光学战 (1672)** —— 牛顿向皇家学会展示他的棱镜实验和粒子说光学理论；胡克立刻批评，说这些「不新」、粒子说错了。牛顿大怒，退出公开发表二十年。
2. **引力战 (1679-1687)** —— 胡克 1679 年写信给牛顿，明确提出**轨道运动 = 直线惯性 + 中心吸引**这个核心想法，并猜测引力遵循距离平方反比律。牛顿私下接受了想法，但拒绝公开承认。1687 年《自然哲学的数学原理》(Principia) 出版时，胡克的贡献被系统性地抹去。

胡克 1703 年去世。牛顿当年被选为皇家学会会长。**胡克的肖像从此再没有出现** —— 没有一幅确认过的胡克画像传到今天，这在胡克这种级别的科学家身上极为反常。是牛顿主动销毁还是单纯被时间遗忘，史学界至今没有定论，但**机会和动机都明明白白**。

这场战争还教给牛顿一招他后来用在莱布尼茨身上的杀手锏：**用匿名报告作伪中立**。1712 年皇家学会指控莱布尼茨剽窃的「中立委员会报告」是牛顿亲笔起草的；他在跟胡克的战争里就练过类似的招数 (1675 年的反驳是匿名发到学会公告里的)。

## Why Hooke is the dress rehearsal for Leibniz

[[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]] is the famous fight — Newton vs Leibniz, 1700s–1720s, calculus, international, brutal. But the Calculus Priority Dispute didn't come from nowhere. Newton had been here before.

The Newton vs Hooke fight had every move that would appear in the Leibniz fight:

| Newton's tactic | Used against Hooke | Used against Leibniz |
|---|---|---|
| Sit on results for years/decades | Calculus (1665) → published 1693+; optics (1666) → published 1672 only after Hooke independently published | Calculus (1665) → published 1693+; Leibniz published 1684 |
| Refuse priority while asserting it | "I had this in my drawer" defence | "I had this in my drawer" defence |
| Anonymous attacks | Used the Royal Society *Philosophical Transactions* to rebut Hooke without signing | Wrote the 1712 *Commercium Epistolicum* "neutral report" himself, anonymously |
| Weaponise institutional power | Avoided publishing until Hooke died (1703); then floodgates open | Became Royal Society president 1703; appointed the committee that ruled against Leibniz in 1712 |
| Erase the rival posthumously | Hooke's portrait disappears after 1703 | Leibniz's reputation in Britain stays ruined for ~150 years |

Same playbook. Hooke was the rehearsal; Leibniz was the championship match.

**Why this matters for the vault's story arc.** Newton is not a single-event villain; he is *consistent*. When you read *Principia* and *Opticks* and the *Methodus Fluxionum* and the *Commercium Epistolicum* as a sequence, you see the same operator running for sixty years. The Hooke fight is the proof that this is who Newton *was* — not what he became after losing his composure to Leibniz.

## Act I — The Optics Skirmish (1672)

January 1672, Newton — age 29, six weeks into being a Fellow of the Royal Society — sent his "New Theory About Light and Colours" to the Society's *Philosophical Transactions*. The paper described his prism experiments: white light is a *composite* of differently-refrangible coloured rays, and Newton supported this with a corpuscular (particle) model of light.

**Hooke responded within a week.**

Hooke's response did three things, all of them irritating:
1. **"This isn't new."** Hooke claimed he had already done these experiments and reached the same conclusion about composition.
2. **"And your model is wrong."** Hooke had been pushing a **wave theory** of light since *Micrographia* (1665). He argued Newton's particle theory was the wrong interpretation of correct data.
3. **"Allow me to suggest improvements."** Hooke offered, in print, ways the experiments could be done "more rigorously."

Hooke, in 1672, *was the most prestigious experimentalist alive in England*. As Curator of Experiments at the Royal Society he ran the weekly demonstrations; he had built telescopes and microscopes and air pumps; *Micrographia* had sold spectacularly. He genuinely had touched colour-refraction before. **But the rigour was Newton's** — Newton's *experimentum crucis* (the two-prism experiment where each colour fails to be further decomposed) was decisive in a way Hooke's earlier observations weren't.

Newton, blindsided, replied at length. The reply was the public-facing one; in private he was furious. By June 1672 Newton wrote to the Society's Secretary saying he wanted to *withdraw* his paper. By March 1673 he had broken off correspondence entirely, writing the infamous line: *"There is no possibility of dealing with you safely."* The Society persuaded him to stay, but Newton's response was structural: **he simply stopped publishing experimental results for the next eighteen years.**

> [!info] Recall the cost
> Recall that Newton's *Opticks* — his full, mature theory of light — wasn't published until **1704**, the year after Hooke died. He sat on it for thirty-two years. Some of this was perfectionism. Most of it was Hooke.

## Act II — The "Giants" Letter (5 February 1676)

In late 1675 the dispute had cooled enough that the Society pushed for reconciliation. Hooke wrote a courteous letter to Newton; Newton replied on 5 February 1676 with what is now the single most quoted line in the history of science:

> *"If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of giants."*

Generations of schoolchildren have been taught this as Newton's modesty, his acknowledgement that all science is cumulative, his graceful tip of the hat to predecessors. **Read in 1676 it was none of those things.**

The phrase *itself* is ancient: it appears in John of Salisbury's *Metalogicon* (1159), where it's attributed to Bernard of Chartres. Newton didn't invent it; everyone in the Royal Society's reading circle would have recognised the medieval reference. So Newton wasn't being humble in some daring new way — he was deploying a stock phrase. Why?

**Because Hooke was short and stooped.** Hooke had spinal curvature from childhood, possibly tubercular. He was visibly small in a way contemporaries commented on. Newton's letter, in context, was offering Hooke the comfort of being one of "the giants" — while drawing exactly the kind of attention to height that everyone present would have caught.

This reading isn't a recent revisionism. Stephen Hawking and Stephen Inwood (Hooke's biographer) both make the case. The letter's surrounding sentences are technically correct flattery about Hooke's earlier work, but the giants line itself reads, in period, as a needle.

![[newton-vs-hooke-giants-letter-comic.png|620]]

> [!tip] The deeper joke
> Robert Hooke was the curator of *experiments* at the Royal Society — he set up the apparatus, ran demonstrations, kept the records. Standing on a giant's shoulders is the kind of thing an experimenter literally does in a chair-on-a-table apparatus when reaching for a high cabinet. Newton was very probably making a joke that worked on at least three levels: (i) the medieval cliché, (ii) Hooke's stature, (iii) Hooke's actual job. *Newton at 33 was already an operator.*

## Act III — Gravity, the High-Stakes Round (1679-1684)

November 1679. Hooke, newly elected Secretary of the Royal Society, wrote to Newton — formally, professionally — asking for any "philosophical communications" Newton might offer. The letter was a courteous reopening of correspondence after the 1672 break.

In the *same letter*, Hooke described a model of planetary motion. The model had two ingredients:
1. **Bodies move in straight lines unless acted on by a force.** (What we now call Newton's First Law — but Hooke had it first, in print, as early as 1670.)
2. **The Sun exerts an *attractive force* on planets, directed toward itself, that bends straight-line motion into an orbit.**

Hooke went further. He proposed — *speculatively, without proof* — that the attractive force followed an **inverse-square law**: $F \propto 1/r^2$.

This is the load-bearing intuition of the whole *Principia*. The framing of "orbit = inertial motion + central attractive force" is the conceptual jump. Hooke is the person who jumped.

But Hooke could not derive the consequence — that an inverse-square central force produces an **elliptical** orbit. (Kepler had observed ellipses; nobody had derived them.) The derivation requires calculus, and Hooke didn't have it. **Newton did.**

Newton received Hooke's letter, thought about it privately, and *did not respond constructively*. In a separate exchange weeks later, Newton sneered at one of Hooke's geometric figures. Hooke, despite the snub, kept the correspondence going for a year and then let it lapse.

**Five years later, August 1684, Edmond Halley visited Newton in Cambridge.** Halley had been talking to Wren and Hooke in a coffeehouse: all three suspected inverse-square but none could derive the ellipse. *Christopher Wren had even offered a 40-shilling book as a prize* to whoever produced the proof first. Halley asked Newton: "What shape do planets trace if the central force is inverse-square?" Newton answered immediately: *"An ellipse. I have calculated it."*

Halley pressed: *show me*. Newton couldn't find the calculation in his papers. He promised to send it. He sent it in November 1684 (the *De Motu Corporum in Gyrum* tract). Halley, recognising what he had, urged Newton to expand it. Newton expanded it for 30 months. The result was the *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, 1687.

> [!info] Beyond syllabus — what does Hooke deserve credit for?
> Hooke deserves credit for the **framing** — the conceptual model that orbit = inertia + central attraction. He deserves credit for **guessing inverse-square** and saying so in writing in 1679. He does NOT deserve credit for the *Principia*'s derivations — those are entirely Newton's, and they required calculus, the universal-law-of-gravitation extension to all bodies, and a vast supporting framework that Hooke never produced.
>
> The honest verdict: Hooke planted a seed and named the species; Newton grew the tree. Both contributions are real. **The fight is over whether the seed deserves the credit.** Modern philosophy of science usually says yes — speculation that turns out right has independent value. Newton, in 1687, said no.

## Act IV — The Principia and the Snub (1687)

When *Principia* went to press, Hooke discovered that his name appeared *minimally* in the parts dealing with gravity, and that the inverse-square hypothesis was credited to "Wren, Hooke, and Halley" together — i.e. Hooke was reduced to one-third of the credit even for the part he had clearly led on.

Hooke wrote to the Society demanding acknowledgement. Newton's response, in a letter to Halley (20 June 1686), was vicious:

> *"He has done nothing... yet now he must, forsooth, have all... Mathematicians that find out, settle and do all the business must content themselves with being nothing but dry calculators and drudges, and another that does nothing but pretend and grasp at all things must carry away all the invention."*

The word "drudges" matters. Newton was casting Hooke — the man who designed half of London after the Great Fire, who invented the spring watch, who saw cells through a microscope, who built the air pump — as a *layabout speculator* compared to himself the proper mathematician. It is one of the most aggressive things Newton ever wrote.

The published *Principia* contained one terse acknowledgement: that Wren, Hooke, and Halley had "separately inferred the law of decrease as the squares of the distances increase" but had not derived consequences. *That was it.* In Book III's earlier draft Newton had been more generous; he edited it down ruthlessly before publication.

**They never spoke kindly to each other again.** Their meetings at the Royal Society in the 1690s were openly cold. Hooke continued his work — barometers, watches, surveys, microscope improvements — but the gravity fight had cost him his last shot at the kind of fame his earlier work had earned. He grew bitter, took fewer pupils, lived increasingly alone. He died in March 1703.

## Act V — The Aftermath (1703–1727)

Hooke died on **3 March 1703**, alone in his lodgings at Gresham College. The funeral was small. Newton was elected President of the Royal Society on **30 November 1703** — eight months later, almost to the day.

What happened next is partly documented, partly inferred, and partly legend. Sorting these is genuinely hard, and historians still disagree.

**Documented:** Newton oversaw the Royal Society's move from Gresham College (Hooke's territory for 40 years) to new premises at Crane Court in 1710. Hooke's apparatus — which he had built and curated for decades — was largely lost in the transition. Newton was directly in charge.

**Documented:** Newton's *Opticks* was published in 1704, the year after Hooke's death — having been suppressed for 32 years. Newton's preface explicitly noted that he had delayed publication to "avoid being engaged in disputes."

![[newton-vs-hooke-opticks-drawer-comic.png|560]]

**Strongly inferred:** *No confirmed portrait of Robert Hooke survives.* For a scientist of his rank — Curator of Experiments, Royal Society Secretary, architect of post-fire London, author of one of the bestselling scientific books of the century — the absence is bizarre. Other scientists of comparable stature have multiple surviving portraits. Hooke's only surviving image is a 2003 reconstruction based on contemporary descriptions. **Whether Newton actively destroyed a portrait when the Society moved is unproven. That the most likely person to have done so was on the premises during the move, with motive, is fact.**

**Apocryphal but contemporary:** Stories circulated within the Society for decades afterward that Newton had Hooke's portrait removed from the wall when he became President. The historian Lisa Jardine — Hooke's most recent biographer — argues the evidence points to negligence rather than active destruction, but the negligence was *Newton's* to commit.

**Documented:** When the *Commercium Epistolicum* committee was appointed in 1712 to "investigate" Leibniz's calculus priority, Newton — as Royal Society President — appointed the committee, supplied the documents, wrote the report, and published the verdict against Leibniz. This is exactly the institutional-weaponisation move he had been rehearsing for forty years.

> [!info] Recall: this was Newton's *second* such operation
> Recall from [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]] §IV that Newton's *Commercium Epistolicum* (1712) was the published report of a "neutral committee" that he himself appointed, drafted, and ghostwrote. Reviewers at the time praised its impartiality. **The Hooke fight is where Newton learned that the right institutional setup makes anonymous self-defence look like third-party adjudication.** By the time Leibniz showed up, Newton had been doing this since 1675.

## Hooke's actual legacy

Newton's posthumous power campaign worked. Through the 18th and most of the 19th centuries, Hooke was remembered (if at all) as a footnote: the man who got too much credit for things Newton did. Modern scholarship has revised this drastically. A partial list of what Robert Hooke actually accomplished:

- **Hooke's Law** ($F = kx$) — published as the anagram "ceiiinosssttuv" in 1676 (which decodes to *ut tensio sic vis*, "as the extension, so the force"), revealed solving 1678. The foundational law of elasticity; the basis for every spring, oscillation, vibration analysis. Still on every modern physics syllabus — the vault's physics-side treatment lives at [[Hooke's Law for Springs]] with the full Taylor-expansion frame.
- **"Cell" in biology** — coined the word in *Micrographia* (1665) when he saw the chambers of cork through a microscope and thought they resembled monks' cells in a monastery.
- **Wave theory of light** — defended against Newton's particle theory; vindicated classically by Young (1801), Fresnel (1820s), and Maxwell (1860s). **But neither man was right.** The wave picture won the 19th century, then Einstein's photons (1905) brought the particle back, then de Broglie and Born (1924–26) revealed both pictures were *partial* — light is wave-AND-particle, and the right framework requires complex amplitudes (see [[Euler's Formula and De Moivre's Theorem]] and [[Stories/The Argument for i]] §V). **The irony is sharp: Hooke and Newton fought viciously over which simple picture was correct, and the universe's answer was "both, neither, both."** This is the deepest twist in the whole story — they spent 30 years tearing each other apart over a both/and that neither would have accepted.
- **Architect of post-fire London** — surveyed 8,394 properties personally after the 1666 Great Fire of London. Designed the Royal Greenwich Observatory, Bethlem Hospital, the Monument to the Great Fire (with Wren). Walked half of London for two decades doing the work that paid for his science.
- **Spring-balanced clock** — Hooke claimed priority over Huygens for the spring-regulated watch (yes, *another* priority fight); the truth is they probably had the idea independently around the same time.
- **Microscopy** — *Micrographia* (1665) was a runaway bestseller; Samuel Pepys recorded staying up all night reading it. Hooke's flea engraving is one of the most famous scientific illustrations ever made.
- **Inverse-square law of gravity** — guessed in 1679, lost the credit fight in 1687, recovered partial credit in the historiography of the last 80 years.

> [!tip] The Hooke-Newton wave-particle irony
> **Wave and particle are both right *and* both wrong.** Newton's corpuscles capture the photon-like quantisation of light's energy; Hooke's waves capture the interference and diffraction patterns. Neither captures the other's truth. *Both pictures are projections of a deeper object that the 17th century didn't have the mathematics to describe* — the complex-amplitude wavefunction, which requires $i$ (see [[Stories/The Argument for i]] §V). Light is a wave when you ask it wave-shaped questions; it is a particle when you ask it particle-shaped questions. Bohr called this **complementarity**.
>
> The Hooke vs Newton fight, read from a 21st-century vantage, is a 250-year-long argument about a both/and that neither man could have accepted as the answer. Their successors — Young, Fresnel, Maxwell on the wave side; Einstein, de Broglie, Born on the particle side — each thought they were *closing* the case. Each was, in fact, **clarifying half the picture**. The closing arrived only when quantum mechanics let go of the question's premise: light isn't a wave that sometimes pretends to be a particle, or a particle that sometimes pretends to be a wave; it's a quantum object whose classical projections are *both* of those things and *neither*.

![[wave-particle-350-year-argument.svg|697]]

*The 350-year swing: Hooke and Newton both propose in 1672 (their fight is the leftmost moment); Newton's authority gives particles a century; Young's 1801 double-slit flips it to waves; Maxwell consolidates the wave picture in 1864; Einstein brings the particle back in 1905; de Broglie and Born resolve the standoff in 1925-26 by accepting both pictures as partial. The modern view sits where the line ends.*

## The lesson

Three takeaways, ordered by importance:

1. **Priority disputes don't come from priority. They come from character.** Newton fought Hooke, Flamsteed, Leibniz, and (briefly) Boyle. Hooke fought Newton, Huygens, Hevelius, and Oldenburg. Both men were brilliant, both men were right about important things, both men ended up alone. The lesson isn't that one was bad and one was good; it's that **scientific giants are sometimes also people who can't share credit.** The vault treats this honestly — see [[Stories/The Bernoulli Family]] for the same dynamic playing out across three generations of brothers, fathers, and sons.

2. **The institutional weapon is the unforgivable move.** Newton's specific innovation in scientific warfare wasn't just *being* combative — Hooke was combative too. It was *using the Royal Society as the venue for a fight he was simultaneously presiding over*. Hooke didn't have that kind of institutional power; Newton did, and used it. **The same pattern reappears in [[Stories/The Calculus Priority Dispute]] §III with the 1712 *Commercium Epistolicum*.**

3. **Hooke's reputation has recovered. Newton's has held.** Both these things are correct. Modern science remembers Hooke for the things he actually did (Hooke's law, cells, springs, gravity-conjecture); it remembers Newton for the things he actually did (the *Principia*, calculus, the spectrum, the *Opticks*). The *dispute* — Newton's vendetta — has shrunk to a footnote. **In the long run, the work survives the fight.** That is the consolation the present-day reader can take.

> [!tip] Stories bridge — [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]]
> Hooke is one of the eponymy story's central case studies — for *both* directions. **Hooke's Law** ($F = kx$) was almost certainly known to medieval bow-makers and renaissance gunsmiths who tuned springs by feel. The name attaches to Robert Hooke not because he *first* observed elasticity but because his anagram-reveal in 1678 published it in a way that made the discovery citable. Same pattern as Maclaurin and Taylor (see [[Maclaurin Series]] §"Stigler's Law strikes again"). **Hooke was a victim of eponymy injustice on the inverse-square law row (Newton's vendetta and Wren's parallel claim diluted his credit); he was also, by Stigler's criterion, a beneficiary of it on the elasticity row.** Both sides of the same coin.
