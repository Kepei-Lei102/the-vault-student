# Laplace and Napoleon 拉普拉斯与拿破仑

> *"Je n'avais pas besoin de cette hypothèse-là."* — "I had no need of that hypothesis."
> — Pierre-Simon Laplace to Napoleon Bonaparte, **as the legend has it**. The only eyewitness wrote down something quieter.

> *"He carried the spirit of the infinitely small into the administration."*
> — Napoleon, on St Helena, explaining why his Minister of the Interior lasted six weeks.

> *"What we know is little; what we do not know is immense."*
> — Laplace's last words, as his friend Fourier reported them, "articulated with difficulty".

## Cast of Characters

- **Pierre-Simon Laplace** (1749–1827) — a cider merchant's son from Normandy, meant for the Church; the man who finished Newton's solar system, invented the modern theory of probability twice over, served every French government of his lifetime, and became a marquis under the king whose predecessor's execution he had lived through.
- **Napoleon Bonaparte** (1769–1821) — artillery cadet examined by Laplace in 1785; First Consul who made him a minister in 1799 and sacked him within weeks; Emperor to whom the *Mécanique céleste* was dedicated, until the dedication was quietly removed.
- **Jean le Rond d'Alembert** (1717–1783) — the Paris grandee who ignored the boy's letter of introduction and answered his mathematics the same day.
- **Joseph-Louis Lagrange** (1736–1813) — the other giant of the age; the rival whose ideas fill the *Mécanique céleste* without much credit, and who is given the best line in the legend.
- **William Herschel** (1738–1822) — discoverer of Uranus, visiting Paris in the Peace of Amiens; the one person who was in the room at Malmaison and kept a diary.
- **Edmond Halley** (1656–1742) and **Isaac Newton** (1643–1727) — the ghosts. Halley found the anomaly in 1695; Newton thought God would have to fix it.
- **Lucien Bonaparte** (1775–1840) — Napoleon's brother, who got Laplace's job.
- **Joseph Fourier**, **François Arago**, **Augustus De Morgan**, **Hervé Faye** — the tellers, whose retellings are the story's second plot.

## Act I — The cider merchant's son and the letter nobody read (1749–1773)

Pierre-Simon Laplace was born on 23 March 1749 in Beaumont-en-Auge, a hill village in Normandy, into a family that was comfortable rather than grand: his father Pierre traded in cider and held minor office in the parish; his mother's people were farmers. He went to the Benedictine priory school in the village from seven to sixteen, and then to the University of Caen to read theology, because a clever boy from a family like that became a priest. Two teachers at Caen, Gadbled and Le Canu, noticed what he actually was, and at nineteen he left for Paris without a degree, carrying Le Canu's letter of introduction to the most powerful mathematician in France, d'Alembert. [MacTutor]

The story of what happened next comes down through Fourier's eulogy and Rouse Ball's retelling, and it is the first of Laplace's choices worth reading. D'Alembert did not answer the letter. So the boy wrote him a second one, not about himself but about mechanics, laying out the general principles as he understood them. D'Alembert replied the same day: a man who could write that needed no recommendation, he had recommended himself. Within days Laplace had a post teaching mathematics at the École Militaire, and by twenty-four he was in the Académie des Sciences. He had discovered, at nineteen, that the way past a closed door in Paris was to send the work instead of the plea. He would never afterwards be modest about the work, and the door never closed on him again. [Ball; MacTutor]

A visitor to the Académie in 1780, the Swedish astronomer Lexell, wrote home that the young Laplace "let it be known that he considered himself the best mathematician in France" and "wanted to pronounce on everything". Lexell added that he probably was the best, which was the problem. [MacTutor]

## Act II — The examiner and the cadet (1784–1785)

![[laplace-napoleon-examiner-comic.png|560]]

In 1784 Laplace took the post of examiner to the Royal Artillery Corps: every cadet who wanted a commission in the artillery had to pass a mathematics examination, and Laplace set and judged it. In September 1785 one of the candidates was a thin, sixteen-year-old Corsican from the École Militaire, Napoleone di Buonaparte, who had crammed the course in one year instead of the usual two because his father had died and the money had run out. Laplace passed him. He graduated forty-second of fifty-eight and was commissioned a second lieutenant of artillery that September, to a regiment at Valence. The examiner would not have remembered the candidate. The candidate, it turned out, remembered the examiner. [MacTutor; napoleon.org]

The same year Laplace read to the Académie the memoir that made him. It answered a question ninety years old.

**Halley's anomaly.** In 1695 Edmond Halley compared the recorded positions of Jupiter and Saturn against old observations and found that Jupiter was running ahead of where Kepler's laws put it and Saturn was falling behind, steadily, century after century. Extrapolated, the drift was fatal: Jupiter spiralling inward, Saturn outward, the solar system slowly unwinding. Newton knew of it, and in the *Opticks* he drew the theological conclusion: the irregularities of the planets would grow "till this system wants a reformation", and the reformer would be God, resetting the clockwork from time to time. Leibniz jeered that Newton's God was a poor watchmaker who had to wind and mend his own work; Newton's friend Clarke replied that a God who had to do nothing would be a God who did nothing, and the Leibniz–Clarke letters of 1715 are the moment the question "does the solar system need looking after?" became a religious one. Euler tried the Jupiter–Saturn problem for two Paris prizes and failed; Lagrange tried in 1766 and failed. [Newton, *Opticks* Query 31; Leibniz–Clarke]

**Laplace's answer.** In 1773 he had already shown that, to first order in the masses, planetary perturbations produce no *secular* change in the sizes of the orbits at all: the semi-major axes oscillate but do not drift. That left the mean motions unexplained. In 1785 he found the reason: five Jupiter years are almost exactly two Saturn years (59.3 against 58.9), so the two planets pull on each other in a pattern that very nearly repeats, and the small mismatch makes the pattern's effect accumulate and then reverse, with a period of roughly nine hundred years. Halley's "acceleration" was one flank of a wave. Jupiter's longitude swings about 21 minutes of arc either way; Saturn's, being lighter and pulled by the heavier planet, about 49. Nothing is unwinding. When Laplace put the periodic term back into the tables, the ancient observations, the medieval ones and Halley's own fell into line. [Laplace 1785–86, *Théorie de Jupiter et de Saturne*]

![[laplace-great-inequality.svg|800]]

You do not have to take his word for it. `laplace-great-inequality.py` integrates the Sun, Jupiter and Saturn under Newton's law and nothing else, with the two planets' observed periods, for six thousand years, and converts each state to its mean longitude. The fitted mean motions do not drift. What is left after subtracting them is a wave of period 930 years with amplitudes of 22.6′ for Jupiter and 55.6′ for Saturn, against Laplace's 21′ and 49′; the modern value of the period from the mean motions themselves, $1/\lvert 2n_J - 5n_S\rvert$, is about 880 years, and a single sinusoid fitted to a 6000-year run lands a little long because the wave is not quite a sine. Box any one century of it and the residual is a straight line, sloping one way for Jupiter and the other way for Saturn. That straight line is what Halley saw.

That is the whole of the theology, done as arithmetic. Newton's God had a job: to correct a drift. The drift was a wave. The job did not exist.

## Act III — The system of the world (1796–1805)

Laplace kept his head through the Terror, which is not nothing: Lavoisier, who had sat on the commission on weights and measures with him, went to the guillotine in 1794; Condorcet died in a cell. Laplace left Paris for Melun during the worst of it and came back to sit on the commission that gave the world the metre, and then on the Bureau des longitudes. He had a gift for being useful to whoever was in charge, and the metric system was the first of the things it produced. [MacTutor]

In 1796 he published the *Exposition du système du monde*, a book without a single equation, for the educated public. Its last pages carry the nebular hypothesis: the Sun and planets condensed out of a vast, hot, slowly rotating cloud that shrank and spun faster as it cooled, throwing off rings that became planets. It is why all the planets go round the same way in nearly the same plane, which no design argument had explained and which the cloud explains for free. Kant had guessed something like it in 1755; Laplace, who read no German, arrived on his own, and his version was the one with the physics. [Laplace 1796]

Then the great book. The *Traité de mécanique céleste* came out in five volumes between 1799 and 1825, and it did for the solar system what the *Principia* had begun: every known irregularity of every planet and moon, derived from gravitation alone. The third volume, 1802, carries a dedication to the First Consul. The fourth, 1805, to "Napoleon the Great". Later editions carry neither. And the book has a famous fault: it does not say where its ideas come from. Lagrange's methods and Legendre's are everywhere in it, unnamed, and Laplace's colleagues knew it. He was not generous with credit. He was, oddly, generous with results: at the country house in Arcueil where he and the chemist Berthollet ran a private academy from about 1805, the young men, Biot, Poisson, Arago, Gay-Lussac, got problems, encouragement, and in Biot's account theorems Laplace had already proved and let them publish as their own. The same man kept every idea he could reach and gave away the ones he had already used. [MacTutor; Biot]

## Act IV — Six weeks at the Interior (November–December 1799)

On 9 November 1799, the 18th of Brumaire, General Bonaparte overthrew the Directory. Three days later he made Laplace Minister of the Interior. The choice was a signal to the scientific establishment that the new regime was theirs, and it was a disaster for the ministry, and Napoleon, who never forgot an error, wrote its epitaph on St Helena:

> A geometer of the first rank, Laplace did not take long to reveal himself as a worse than mediocre administrator; from his first actions we recognised our mistake. Laplace saw no question from its true point of view: he sought subtleties everywhere, had only doubtful ideas, and finally carried the spirit of the infinitely small into the administration.

Lucien Bonaparte replaced him on 25 December. Laplace was six weeks a minister. [Napoleon, *Mémoires*; MacTutor]

He was not discarded. He went to the Senate, became its chancellor in 1803, and was made a Count of the Empire in 1806. The lesson he took from the ministry, if he took one, was that the way to serve power was from a seat where nobody expected decisions. He held that seat under Napoleon and then under the king who replaced him.

## Act V — Malmaison, 8 August 1802

![[laplace-napoleon-malmaison-comic.png|620]]

Now the famous scene, and first what actually happened.

In the summer of 1802, during the short peace between Britain and France, William Herschel came to Paris. On 8 August he and Laplace were received by the First Consul at Malmaison. Herschel kept a diary, and it is the only account by anyone who was present. Napoleon, he wrote, asked a great many questions about astronomy, and then, speaking of the extent of the heavens, exclaimed something like "And who is the author of all this!" Laplace "wished to show that a chain of natural causes would account for the construction and preservation of the wonderful system"; the First Consul "rather opposed" him. That is the whole of it. No hypothesis, no *sire*. A head of state making a pious exclamation, a scientist explaining that the system takes care of itself, the head of state pushing back. [Herschel's diary, 8 August 1802]

The sentence everyone quotes was first printed decades later. Arago told it, and Victor Hugo wrote down Arago's version: Napoleon, "How is it that you make the whole system of the world, you give the laws of all creation, and in your whole book you speak not once of the existence of God?" and Laplace, "*Sire, je n'avais pas besoin de cette hypothèse-là*." Augustus De Morgan, the English mathematician, printed the story in his *Budget of Paradoxes* in 1872, saying it was well known in Paris and had never been given in full; his Napoleon says "they tell me you have written this large book on the system of the universe and have never even mentioned its Creator", and his Laplace answers "bluntly". Then the tellers added the topper. Lagrange, told of the exchange, is said to have remarked: "Ah, but it is a beautiful hypothesis; it explains many things." In some versions Laplace himself gets that line too, which is a tell: a good line looks for the most famous mouth. [Hugo; De Morgan]

**What "that hypothesis" was.** In 1884 the astronomer Hervé Faye, who had known people who had known Laplace, insisted the line had been misread for eighty years. Laplace was not saying he had no need of God. He was saying he had no need of *Newton's God*, the one who had to intervene from time to time to reset the planets, because Act II had shown that the resetting never happens. The hypothesis was the intervention, not the existence. Read that way, the line is not a slogan of atheism; it is the summary of the *Mécanique céleste*, and it is exactly what Herschel heard: a chain of natural causes accounts for the construction *and the preservation* of the system. [Faye 1884]

**The honest edge.** Faye was a believer defending a dead man's reputation, and Laplace's private naturalism went well beyond the planets; nobody who knew him thought him devout, and later Catholic writers could only argue that he was reconciled on his deathbed. So both things are true. The sentence was polished by a century of retelling, and the position behind it was real. What the legend loses is the *precision* of it. Laplace had earned the right to drop one specific hypothesis by doing ninety years of unfinished arithmetic, and the earned version is better than the slogan.

## Act VI — The demon and the sunrise (1812–1814)

The same man wrote the most famous sentence of determinism ever put on paper, and the reason he could write it is Act II. In the *Essai philosophique sur les probabilités* of 1814, the popular introduction to his enormous *Théorie analytique* of 1812, he imagines an intelligence that knew, at one instant, every force in nature and the position of every body, and was vast enough to do the analysis: for it, "nothing would be uncertain, and the future, like the past, would be present to its eyes". Nobody has been able to leave the image alone since; it has been called Laplace's demon for a century, though Laplace called it an intelligence and meant it as a description of what his mechanics *aimed at*, not of anyone who existed. [Laplace 1814]

Then, in the next breath, the point of the book: we are not that intelligence. Probability, he says, is relative in part to our ignorance and in part to our knowledge. The man who had just declared the universe a clock spent the rest of the essay building the tools for a creature who does not know where the hands are. He revived and generalised Bayes's rule, which had been published posthumously in 1763 and forgotten; he derived from it the **rule of succession**: if an event has happened $n$ times out of $n$, and you knew nothing beforehand, the probability it happens again is $(n+1)/(n+2)$. His own example is the sunrise. Taking history at five thousand years, 1 826 213 days, he wrote that one may bet 1 826 214 to 1 on the sun rising tomorrow, and then added the sentence people leave out: that the number is far larger for anyone who knows the mechanism that makes the sun rise, because he no longer needs induction. The clock and the ignorance, in one paragraph. [Laplace 1814]

`laplace-rule-of-succession.py` recomputes his number, derives the rule by integrating over a flat prior, and checks it by brute force: two million imaginary worlds with their own unknown $p$, filtered to the ones where the first $n$ trials all succeeded, and the fraction where trial $n+1$ succeeds too comes out at $0.6659$, $0.8004$, $0.9163$ for $n = 1, 3, 10$ against $2/3$, $4/5$, $11/12$. The $+1$ and $+2$ are two imaginary trials, one success and one failure, that the flat prior smuggles in: Laplace's hedge against ever being certain.

Two years earlier the *Théorie analytique* had proved the theorem that made the [[Normal Distribution]] universal, the central limit theorem in its first general form, and had shown why least squares, which Legendre had published and Gauss had claimed, was the right thing to do with astronomical errors. The methods he built to deal with not knowing are the ones that survive in every statistics course; the *Mécanique céleste* is read by historians. That is the joke of his life and the point of it: the determinist's lasting gift was the mathematics of doubt.

## Act VII — The weathervane (1814–1827)

![[laplace-regimes-timeline.svg|900]]

In April 1814, with the allies in Paris, the Senate voted to depose the Emperor. Laplace voted with it. During the Hundred Days, when Napoleon came back, he left the city. When Louis XVIII came back for good, Laplace was made a marquis and a peer of France in 1817, and the dedications to Napoleon disappeared from his books. The pattern of his political life is a straight line through seven regimes: he had been a royal examiner under Louis XVI, a commissioner for the Republic, a minister and a count for Napoleon, and now a marquis for the Bourbons, and at each turn he was on the side that had just won. In 1826 he refused to sign a declaration by the Académie in favour of the freedom of the press, and his colleagues did not forgive him for it. [MacTutor; Ball]

It is easy to sneer, and the sneer misses something. The ministry showed he could not govern; the sneer assumes he wanted to. What he wanted, by every account, was to finish the system of the world and to be recognised as the man who had, and every regime got the same trade from him: his prestige, his committees, his metre, in exchange for being left alone to work. Lavoisier, who stood on principle, died at fifty. Laplace worked until seventy-seven. The choice is not admirable; it is legible, and it is the one he made at nineteen when he sent d'Alembert the mechanics instead of the plea.

He died in Paris on 5 March 1827, a few weeks short of seventy-eight. The Académie cancelled its meeting. Fourier, who gave the eulogy, reported that his last words, spoken with difficulty, were to the effect that what we know is little and what we are ignorant of is immense; De Morgan preferred a bleaker report, that he had said "man pursues nothing but phantoms". Either is a strange last line for the author of the demon. Both are the right last line for the author of the *Essai*. [Fourier 1829; De Morgan]

## Honest edges

- **The stability he proved was not the stability there is.** Laplace's theorems are first order in the masses and eccentricities. Poincaré showed in 1889 that the three-body problem has no general solution of the kind Laplace assumed, and Jacques Laskar's integrations in 1989 showed that the inner solar system is chaotic, with a horizon of a few million years beyond which Mercury's orbit cannot be predicted at all and a small chance of catastrophe over billions. The great inequality is real and Laplace's explanation of it is right; the sentence "the solar system is stable" that was built on it is not, and the demon, it turns out, would need infinitely precise data even in principle. [Poincaré; Laskar]
- **Credit.** The *Mécanique céleste* took from Lagrange and Legendre without naming them; the rule of succession is Bayes's theorem, which Laplace rediscovered and then extended past anything Bayes had done, and which carries Bayes's name by the rule [[Stories/Stigler's Law of Eponymy]] describes; least squares was Legendre's in print, Gauss's by claim, and Laplace's by justification.
- **The quote.** Second-hand at best, first printed seventy years after the event, with the best line handed to Lagrange or to Laplace depending on the teller. The diary of the one man in the room does not contain it. Tell the story with that in it, or do not tell it.

## Cultural ripples

- **"I had no need of that hypothesis"** became the motto of scientific naturalism, quoted by people who have never heard of the Jupiter–Saturn problem it was actually about. The sharpened version outlived the precise one, which is the usual fate of a good sentence.
- **Laplace's demon** is the standard name for strict determinism in philosophy, physics and fiction, and every argument about free will, chaos and quantum mechanics has to walk past it. Chaos took away its data; quantum mechanics took away its premise; it still stands there.
- **The nebular hypothesis**, now the Kant–Laplace theory, is how every planetary system is still thought to form, with the physics rewritten several times over.
- **Bayesian inference**, which Laplace built and the twentieth century's frequentists tried to bury, came back with the computer and now runs spam filters, medical diagnosis and the codebreaking at Bletchley that [[Conditional Probability]] describes.
- **The metric system**, which he helped found, is the one part of his public service that every reader of this card uses daily.
